### Fill and Dump (2026-10-10)

The first of the gap review's new games, built from its sketch (roadmap chunk 4): a lap and toddler game in Counting Cove, `src/games/fill-dump/`, region `counting-cove`, bands lap 1–2 and toddler 1–4. *Sketch as written:* "Fun action: tip a basket and watch fruit tumble out with a chime for each piece; drag or tap pieces back in. Skill: containment and capacity. Smallest round: a big basket and a bin; any dump is a success; the round ends when everything is back in or after a few dumps. Support: lap has no wrong move; toddler fills a basket with a stated number; preschool tips a jug to a line, where the flow slows near the line and an overfill spills into a tray that can be poured back; a glow marks the line after two overshoots. Deeper play: compare two cups, "which holds more?", pour one into the other. Co-play: the grown-up dumps and she fills."

*Built:* taps only (a one-year-old will not drag), no bin (the grass is the bin), and the "stated number" levels end on a green check rather than on reaching the number, because a game that stops the moment the count is right has no wrong move and the number would carry no decision. Levels 1–2 tip out and scoop (3 pieces once, then 4 pieces twice); levels 3–4 put in one to three of five, then three to five of seven. The rules are a reducer in `logic.ts` with tests: any taps at all finish levels 1–2 with no miss; a ghost finger finishes levels 3–4 from any state in at most pieces + 1 taps; landing places are separate cells of a grid at least 128 units apart in four view shapes. A wrong check is the only miss; every second miss glows a fruit to add, the basket to take one out of, or the check. **Pour slice built:** see [the pour-mode note](#fill-and-dump-pour-mode-2026-10-10). "Which holds more?" and Quick Tricks' third show remain proposed.

**What the whole pass taught, for the next game that wants a bot:**

- Confirm the real input before planning around a scan: Splish Splash, Stamp Studio, Rainbow Fingers and Sound Garden had been listed as window-listener games, but their touches arrive on their own objects, so the ghost reached them with a `tap` or a `trace` and no change to the game.
- `onTap` answers on pointerdown, so the hand's `when` and its press are one moment (a car only on green needs no slack). The intent's `pause` sets the gap before the next touch, and the hand cannot tap faster than `MIN_TAP_GAP` (0.87 s); a game that judges a rhythm only by long against short gaps (Sound Garden's echo) is still demonstrated, by slowing every gap together (`echoRest`, tested against the game's own `judgeEcho`).
- A toy's bot must end the round the way a person does, because `fingerdemo` asks for a finished round with no wrong move (Stamp Studio: five stamps and the green arrow; Fluffy Salon: a stroke with each tool, then the mirror), and a bot must choose from what the player can see, never hidden state (Lemonade Stand's `forecastChoice`).
- A game that applies an effect once per pointer event (Fluffy Salon's fur) depends on the frame rate: the app caps a step at 0.05 s, so the hand's events can be 21 units apart, and the test runs the bot's strokes at that spacing and at 7, with an estimate of the hand's time against the level's 15-second hint timer.
- A demonstration window narrows the card's text column, so the card gets taller: measure every level's card in landscape and portrait, not only the default level the `howto` suite checks, and trim the copy before touching the layout (Stamp Studio's and Lemonade Stand's had to be trimmed).
- A bot that only plays right can still be told off by an overlap: Splish Splash counted a miss when a stroke slid from a just-cleaned ear into the head's patch; a finger still within reach of a patch it scrubbed in the same touch is no longer told off, and one that slides on to a different part still is (`wrongTouches`). Probe an unclean demonstration (`misses > 0`) before changing a rule, and compare coordinates in one space.

### Lemonade Stand: the plan as written before it was built

The plan that preceded the build. What was built, and where it differs, is in the [roadmap](../ROADMAP.md#first-slice-lemonade-stand-built-2026-10-08); the game's behavior is in [GAMES.md](../GAMES.md#lemonade-stand).

The plan as written before it was built:

**Why it is a new game.** Market Stall teaches composing a known amount with coins: the child pays, makes a second combination or gives change. Lemonade Stand asks her to choose uncertain quantities, observe a small system across days and revise a plan. It may reuse the stall, shell coins, critters and weather art, but putting it after Market Stall's level 6 would make one saved ladder hold two unrelated interactions. Give it its own stable ID and records; first support the `school` band, with later 8–11 modes belonging to Maker Harbor or another elementary zone rather than a new grade gate.

- **Fun action:** look at a lively weather-and-crowd forecast, choose how much lemonade to make, open the striped stand, and watch critters arrive until the SOLD and LEFT jars settle. The payoff is an immediate little crowd scene followed by one more informed choice.
- **Learning goal:** interpret a forecast and a picture table; compare made, sold, left and shells earned; reason about demand; change one variable after evidence. Deeper play adds unit cost, revenue and profit without pretending one simulated market is a law of real life.
- **Smallest complete round:** three days. Each day shows a spoken picture forecast and three large batch cards (for example 4, 8 or 12 cups). Demand is deterministic from the round seed and visible conditions. Opening the stand animates sales, then adds one row to a picture table. After day three, the pet points out one comparison and the round finishes with its ordinary sticker. Extra cups go to a picnic table for friends; they are not thrown away. The first modes cannot run out of shells.
- **Starting support:** the first plan has one changing factor, weather, and its three demand bands never overlap. The forecast and the previous table row remain replayable. A help request circles two comparable rows and says one useful relationship, such as “Both days were hot. More friends came when a cup cost two shells.” Choosing a weak batch or price is an experiment, not a miss; it produces honest feedback and the next day remains playable. Requested help counts as a hint, but sales never become adaptive misses.
- **Deeper modes:** add a visible event to the forecast (a ferry arriving or a quiet rainy morning); then choose a price of 1, 2 or 3 shells as well as quantity; then run a five-day market week with a fixed starter purse and simple ingredient cost. Price and weather each matter, customers give short picture/speech feedback, and the table can hold one variable still for a fair comparison. In this older mode a rainy overproduction day may produce a real operating loss: the leftover lemonade is shared, but its ingredients still cost shells. The loss is small, causal and recoverable; there is no debt, bankruptcy or failed week, and the host supplies a clearly named minimum restock if the purse cannot fund the next day's smallest batch. Recipe quality and advertising wait until those two choices are readable; the first build does not need Coolmath's full supply shop.
- **Stopping and rewards:** every planned day completes, with a clear “close the stand” option between days and a saved in-progress week only if the first play shows three days are too long for one sitting. The purse exists only inside this round. A result may save one bounded market-week chart to the journal later, but shells never buy stickers or determine whether the round completes. A low-profit week is evidence to interpret, not a miss.
- **Rules and checks:** put demand, price response, costs, forecasts, table rows and help selection in `logic.ts`. Over many seeds, assert demand stays within the prepared batch choices; hotter/equally priced comparable days never lower the modeled demand; a higher price never increases the same day's demand; revenue, cost and leftovers reconcile exactly; every help comparison is true and suggests a change that can affect the next day; and reloading the same seed reconstructs the same week. One filtered browser play should cover the first and deepest initial mode, a low-sales experiment, requested help, the end table and portrait.

### The Fun and arcade review: why they play, and the evidence

**Why they play, and the stance this takes.** AARP's 50+ players give fun (86%), relaxation (79%), keeping minds sharp (78%), being challenged (74%) and stress relief (71%); 77% see play as part of healthy aging; 69% say games are not designed with them in mind, and ads are a major annoyance (the couch has none). Ease of play ranked highest. De Schutter and Vanden Abeele argue for *playfulness over usefulness* and *growth over decline*, which matches "fun, not educational": no skill is claimed, and a game is described as a game with a score. **No game here is offered as a health measure.** The brain-training evidence is narrow: a large review found training improves the trained tasks, with little transfer to distant tasks and everyday life (Simons et al., 2016). The 2026 ACTIVE follow-up found about a 25% lower dementia diagnosis rate for one arm only, speed-of-processing training with booster sessions. None of this supports a game proposed here, and none should be cited as if it did.
