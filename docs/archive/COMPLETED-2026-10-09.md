### A bot demonstration on every game's how-to card: the build log

The item as it stood on 2026-10-09 with its per-bot rules, timings and fixes. The open work (4 games) is in the [roadmap](../ROADMAP.md#ghost-finger-demonstrations-the-remaining-4-games).

- [ ] **A bot demonstration on every game's how-to card (started 2026-10-08).** The rule (the developer, 2026-10-08): **show the input the person is actually using.** On the island's card that is a ghost finger on a touch screen and a mouse pointer clicking and dragging on a desktop (the last real pointer decides, so a touch laptop follows whichever was used last); couch play keeps its controller and, on a laptop, key-cap demos, chosen by its own **Where I'm playing** setting. Before this item the island's card showed a bot only for the fifteen games the couch also plays, and that bot pressed controller buttons, which is wrong for touch; the rest were text alone. A game gains an optional `autotouch()` beside `autoplay`: it names what a capable child would do next (tap a spot on an object, drag an object to a spot, trace a path), and `Demo` plays it with a code-drawn hand that travels, presses and sends the events straight to that object's own `onTap`/`draggable` handlers (`emit` on the target, so the card's real Play and Back buttons are never reached and nothing is hit-tested). The game only chooses targets, by rules in `logic.ts` that a test can check; timing, the hand and the card's note belong to the harness. The demonstration plays **her current level** on a fixed seed, so it matches the card's "this level" line; couch demos keep the couch's own demo level. Where a game has `autotouch` the card shows the finger; a game without one is text alone. **The fifteen couch games come first**: they are the only island cards that show a controller highlight to someone holding a tablet or using a mouse, which is the thing this item exists to fix (all fifteen are done as of 2026-10-09: ten as plain taps and drags, and Egg Catch, Bounce Back, Bouncy Launch, Peg Garden and Bumper Garden as the first steering, pull-and-release and timed-tap bots). ### Lemonade Stand: the plan as written before it was built

The plan that preceded the build. What was built, and where it differs, is in the [roadmap](../ROADMAP.md#first-slice-lemonade-stand-built-2026-10-08); the game's behavior is in [GAMES.md](../GAMES.md#lemonade-stand).

The plan as written before it was built:

**Why it is a new game.** Market Stall teaches composing a known amount with coins: the child pays, makes a second combination or gives change. Lemonade Stand asks her to choose uncertain quantities, observe a small system across days and revise a plan. It may reuse the stall, shell coins, critters and weather art, but putting it after Market Stall's level 6 would make one saved ladder hold two unrelated interactions. Give it its own stable ID and records; first support the `school` band, with later 8–11 modes belonging to Maker Harbor or another elementary zone rather than a new grade gate.

- **Fun action:** look at a lively weather-and-crowd forecast, choose how much lemonade to make, open the striped stand, and watch critters arrive until the SOLD and LEFT jars settle. The payoff is an immediate little crowd scene followed by one more informed choice.
- **Learning goal:** interpret a forecast and a picture table; compare made, sold, left and shells earned; reason about demand; change one variable after evidence. Deeper play adds unit cost, revenue and profit without pretending one simulated market is a law of real life.
- **Smallest complete round:** three days. Each day shows a spoken picture forecast and three large batch cards (for example 4, 8 or 12 cups). Demand is deterministic from the round seed and visible conditions. Opening the stand animates sales, then adds one row to a picture table. After day three, the pet points out one comparison and the round finishes with its ordinary sticker. Extra cups go to a picnic table for friends; they are not thrown away. The first modes cannot run out of shells.
- **Starting support:** the first plan has one changing factor, weather, and its three demand bands never overlap. The forecast and the previous table row remain replayable. A help request circles two comparable rows and says one useful relationship, such as “Both days were hot. More friends came when a cup cost two shells.” Choosing a weak batch or price is an experiment, not a miss; it produces honest feedback and the next day remains playable. Requested help counts as a hint, but sales never become adaptive misses.
- **Deeper modes:** add a visible event to the forecast (a ferry arriving or a quiet rainy morning); then choose a price of 1, 2 or 3 shells as well as quantity; then run a five-day market week with a fixed starter purse and simple ingredient cost. Price and weather each matter, customers give short picture/speech feedback, and the table can hold one variable still for a fair comparison. In this older mode a rainy overproduction day may produce a real operating loss: the leftover lemonade is shared, but its ingredients still cost shells. The loss is small, causal and recoverable; there is no debt, bankruptcy or failed week, and the host supplies a clearly named minimum restock if the purse cannot fund the next day's smallest batch. Recipe quality and advertising wait until those two choices are readable; the first build does not need Coolmath's full supply shop.
- **Stopping and rewards:** every planned day completes, with a clear “close the stand” option between days and a saved in-progress week only if the first play shows three days are too long for one sitting. The purse exists only inside this round. A result may save one bounded market-week chart to the journal later, but shells never buy stickers or determine whether the round completes. A low-profit week is evidence to interpret, not a miss.
- **Rules and checks:** put demand, price response, costs, forecasts, table rows and help selection in `logic.ts`. Over many seeds, assert demand stays within the prepared batch choices; hotter/equally priced comparable days never lower the modeled demand; a higher price never increases the same day's demand; revenue, cost and leftovers reconcile exactly; every help comparison is true and suggests a change that can affect the next day; and reloading the same seed reconstructs the same week. One filtered browser play should cover the first and deepest initial mode, a low-sales experiment, requested help, the end table and portrait.

### The Fun and arcade review: why they play, and the evidence

The stance paragraph and the evidence labels of the 2026-10-09 [fun and arcade review](../IDEAS.md#fun-and-arcade-review-2026-10-09), moved here. The review keeps its two tracks, its Lander and Drift sketch, its decisions and its comfort-options audit.

**Why they play, and the stance this takes.** AARP's 50+ players give fun (86%), relaxation (79%), keeping minds sharp (78%), being challenged (74%) and stress relief (71%); 77% see play as part of healthy aging; 69% say games are not designed with them in mind, and ads are a major annoyance (the couch has none). Ease of play ranked highest. De Schutter and Vanden Abeele argue for *playfulness over usefulness* and *growth over decline*, which matches "fun, not educational": no skill is claimed, and a game is described as a game with a score. **No game here is offered as a health measure.** The brain-training evidence is narrow: a large review found training improves the trained tasks, with little transfer to distant tasks and everyday life (Simons et al., 2016). NeuroRacer (46 adults aged 60 to 85, 12 hours on a custom adaptive driving-and-sign game) cut a multitasking cost from 64% to 16%, stable six months later, but it was small and custom-built. The 2026 ACTIVE follow-up found about a 25% lower dementia diagnosis rate for one arm only, speed-of-processing training with booster sessions. None of this supports a game proposed here, and none should be cited as if it did.

### Cozy games, motivations and profiles (2026-10-09)

The evidence behind [Profiles first](../ROADMAP.md#profiles-first-one-map-for-every-age) and the cozy rows above. **All of it is from search excerpts and none was opened**, so it is background and not proof.

- **What older players want less of.** Quantic Foundry's survey work, as covered by the press in 2016 (the sample size was reported inconsistently), found Competition falls most with age among its twelve motivations, Strategy holds steadiest, and no motivation grows with age. *Use:* the grown-up place leads with low-competition play, and a face-off is an option and never the default.
- **What "cozy" means.** There is no agreed definition. The most consistent marker in essays and blogs is the absence of a fail state, with low stakes, no timers, gentle art, and tending, making or decorating; one journal article surveyed 277 cozy-game players and notes that little is known about who they are. *Use:* the project's rules already match, so the cozy builders, Unpack the Room and Cottage Garden belong. A blog's market statistics were not used.
- **Tweens.** YPulse's 2026 report on 1,500 children aged 8 to 12 names Roblox, Minecraft and Fortnite as where friendships happen, with building platforms leading. *Use:* building, creating and obstacle-course games for a 9 to 12 place, not shooters (Fortnite is outside the boundaries).
- **Starting from age.** Khan Academy Kids' path offers activities by age, level and past performance (a store listing; that a child picks an age at setup was not confirmed). Netflix lets an account lock a profile with a PIN (older guides; current behavior unconfirmed). *Use:* age to start and play to adjust matches the first; Puddle Island chooses no lock at entry, because the cost of a toddler opening a grown-up's card is low once destructive actions are protected.

### The gap review: what the registry showed

Read from each game's `bands`, `region` and level table on 2026-10-09 (counts of games live in the roadmap and README, not here):

- **Wonder Woods mostly replays the top levels of older games.** Lemonade Stand is the only game that begins in the `school` band, and fourteen more begin at pre-K. Of the games that reach it, 23 give the band only one or two levels (Bubble Pop, Jelly Drums, Pattern Train, Memory Match, Letter Trails, Song Maker, Size Parade and others). A game designed for ages 6–8 is the gap; [deepening short ladders](../ROADMAP.md#next-up) closes only part of it.
- **Thin cells in the subject-by-band grid.** At school: Bubble Beach has one game, Barnyard and Rainbow Meadow two each, Treehouse three (all creative toys). At lap: Puzzle Peaks has one (Puzzle Pals). Treehouse (art) and Bubble Beach (senses) have four games each and Music Mountain five, the smallest subjects.
- **Ages 8–11 have no band.** Each 8–11 proposal below is tagged either *fits the school band* (a school ladder has room to about age 9) or *needs the band and zone decision* (the roadmap's E1 zone-identity item and the band audit in [AGENTS.md](../../AGENTS.md)).
- **The best-tested puzzles are couch-only.** Sudoku Garden, Lantern Lights, Picture Logic, Word Search, Island Bridges and Pond Conga have solvers, generators, hints and rule tests, but live in `GROWNUP_GAMES`, so the island cannot list them.
- **Reading and writing stop early.** Letter Trails traces capitals and names; Word Monsters ends at three-letter words and word families; every prompt in the catalog is spoken. No game asks her to read a printed word for its meaning, or to write a numeral or a lowercase letter.
- **Time stops at the clock.** Clock Tower is the only time game. No game names days, months or seasons or shows a life cycle.
- **The lap band has no song she already knows.** Song Maker generates random songs from a seed.
