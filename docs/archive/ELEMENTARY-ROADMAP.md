# Growing Puddle Island through elementary school

> **Archived 2026-10-07.** Folded into the [roadmap's later horizons](../ROADMAP.md#later-horizons-elementary). Kept as it stood for reference; its checklists are not maintained here.

The long-term plan is a familiar island that grows from early play through **kindergarten–grade 5, roughly ages 5–11**. Keep extending favorite games, introduce new interactions when they add something useful, and connect them through places, characters, and projects. Younger activities remain available throughout.

This is a content and product roadmap, not a release schedule or a completed curriculum. Ages and grades are rough planning guides, with overlap and support chosen for the child. The current app has five bands: four through pre-K and an early-school band (6–8) whose place is **Wonder Woods**. Apart from that place and the games noted in E1, the elementary zones, modes, and systems below are **proposed**. [ROADMAP.md](../ROADMAP.md) holds the active implementation checklist; the [arcade](../IDEAS.md) and [microgame](../IDEAS.md) notebooks hold the larger idea pool.

For the current prototype phase, the [party follow-up](PARTY-AND-SWITCH.md) and [play and progression review](PLAY-AND-PROGRESSION.md) take priority: prove a three-choice party route, then deeper adult-playable courses and personal goals in the browser, then build the small room. The developer's daughter is not yet one and an iPad is not readily available. Physical-device and child-observation milestones below stay open without blocking these prototypes; adult enjoyment does not establish child readiness.

## The shape of the journey

| Horizon | What grows | Places and example experiences | A useful milestone |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Foundation: current lap through pre-K** | Reliable touch play, spoken support, finding favorite games, completing and saving a round | Existing Lagoon, Meadow, Hills, and Peak; Windy Picnic and the pet room are first connecting projects | The current catalog is easy to find and comfortable to use on the iPad; a short project can resume after a break |
| **Early elementary: roughly K–1, ages 5–7** | Combine small quantities, follow and explain short sequences, use word/picture clues, measure and compare | **Wonder Woods**; help set up a woodland picnic, balance supplies, program a delivery route, follow a narrated clue | One new zone with two deeper familiar games, one new activity, and a small optional adventure |
| **Middle elementary: roughly grades 2–3, ages 7–9** | Equal groups and fractions, meaningful word parts, evidence in stories, loops and conditions, designing and testing | **Maker Harbor** and **Storybook Square**, built in separate small expansions; organize a ferry fair or investigate mixed-up invitations | A project combines skills across games and produces something the child can revisit or revise |
| **Upper elementary: roughly grades 4–5, ages 9–11** | Fraction/decimal relationships, area and volume, interpreting data, comparing explanations, longer programs and creative projects | **Discovery Marsh** and **Skywatch Isles**; design a visitor habitat, investigate a water route, build a model observatory | Several valid approaches work, the child can compare results, and a saved project survives multiple play sessions |

The near-term **6–8 pilot** bridges the early and middle horizons. These rows are not a proposal for one runtime band per school grade. They guide which experiences to author next; they do not lock places or assign a grade to the child.

## Two kinds of growth

**Games grow through families of modes.** A counting game can become equal sharing, then grouping, then a small recipe or packing problem. A route game can gain loops, conditions, and reusable subroutes. Keep the original activity recognizable, with a deeper decision at each step. A new mode should earn its place through play, rather than simply showing larger numbers or requiring quicker responses.

**Zones grow through a setting and a purpose.** A harbor brings together routes, loads, signs, and construction; a story square brings together clues, performance, and language. A zone can include several subjects and support more than one learning range. Reuse the same game identity across places, with an appropriate mode and a clear return route.

Some favorites should remain simple toys. Bubble popping, dressing a pet, and free painting can remain enjoyable breaks for an older child. There is no requirement to stretch every game to grade 5 or replace quiet play with a harder challenge.

The presentation can grow too. The same pet gradually becomes a companion in planning and investigating; older modes can use quieter feedback, richer tools, and optional explanations without changing the welcoming art style. A saved project shelf lets the child choose what to continue or show a grown-up. Keep narration replayable, help easy to request, and celebrations at meaningful stopping points rather than interrupting every small action.

## How familiar games could keep growing

Everything in the three progression columns is a proposed extension, not a description of current coverage. Choose modes according to readiness and interest; do not automatically advance the whole library at once.

| Existing family | Early elementary | Middle elementary | Upper elementary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Duck Pond, Monster Munch, Little Helpers, Scoop Shop** | Compose/decompose small totals; bundle ten; explain how many more are needed | Equal groups, arrays, division with visible leftovers, equal pieces of a whole | Equivalent fractions, decimal amounts, and scaling a small order or recipe with visual models |
| **Word Monsters, Letter Trails** | Reviewed sound/word combinations, familiar word families, short labels that matter in a scene | Prefixes/suffixes, syllables, and choosing a word that fits a clue or sentence | Use word parts and context to interpret unfamiliar words; compose or revise a short message with spoken support |
| **Story Steps, Photo Safari** | Retell a sequence and match a spoken clue to an event | Infer a cause, choose an ending supported by clues, organize a short narrative | Compare two accounts, select supporting details, revise an explanation or create a branching story |
| **Robot Path, Egg Catch, Mail Carrier** | Editable routes, repeated steps, picture-map legends, planning two stops | Loops, simple conditions, reusable route chunks, debugging a visible trace | Small functions or nested loops, several constraints, comparing routes and explaining a revision |
| **Size Parade, Bug Builder, Puzzle Pals, Rainbow Fingers** | Measuring with equal units, rotation, symmetry, composing shapes | Area/perimeter, tiling, equivalent shapes, following and changing a blueprint | Packing unit cubes for volume, coordinate designs, relating a drawing to a construction |
| **Sink or Float, Peg Garden, Bouncy Launch** | Predict and observe; compare two trials with obvious differences | Change one variable, repeat a test, record outcomes in a picture table | Read graphs, compare evidence, notice limitations of a model, and improve an experiment |
| **Jelly Drums, Song Maker, Sound Garden** | Rhythm conversations, phrases, musical turn-taking | Arrange contrasting sections and layered rhythms; create a response to a phrase | Compose for a story or event, vary rhythmic patterns, revise a piece after listening; retain pentatonic pitch rules |
| **Duckling Parade, Roundup, Dot Link, Bounce Back** | Follow patterns, sort by a stated rule, cooperate toward a visible goal | Combine attributes, plan an order of actions, explain a partner strategy | Optional constraint puzzles or cooperative remixes where the original controls still feel good; otherwise keep them as relaxing favorites |
| **Weather Wardrobe, Feelings Faces, community errands** | Plan a short outing and consider what a friend might need | Read a map or simple timetable, coordinate roles, compare plausible perspectives | Plan a shared event with several needs, explain a compromise, and revise it together; avoid treating feelings or family routines as having one correct answer |

Some of these steps now exist as games of their own: **Lasso Loops** (built) bundles fireflies in fives and tens and makes equal groups, and **Block Tower** (built) reaches a simple, stated balance rule for 6–8 through stacking. Avoid making every game teach arithmetic. Language, music, spatial thinking, inquiry, coordination, creativity, and cooperation all have a place. Offer pictures, manipulatives, narration, and editable plans throughout. Reading and writing can be practiced inside a game while the interface and help remain accessible aloud.

## Zone concepts

Names and boundaries are working proposals. Each zone starts as one small place with a host and a few activities; a zone does not need a separate engine, economy, or complete subject curriculum.

| Proposed zone | Identity and recurring host | First small expansion | Later growth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Wonder Woods** | A trail of hollow trees, streams, and signs; a curious squirrel postkeeper (Hazel, who appears in Mail Carrier's map) | Seesaw Balance's take-the-same-off levels, a Robot Path loop mode and a Mail Carrier map mode (all three built); **The Woodland Picnic** uses all three | Older Peekaround Island modes, listening clues, short stories, and gentle classification puzzles |
| **Maker Harbor** | Floating workshops and a friendly ferry crew; a beaver builder | Extend Little Helpers' equal groups (built at level 6) into dock loads and Mail Carrier into dock routes; the constrained **Chain Reaction** activity is built as a standalone game | Shape Buddies, Wobble Works, area/volume blueprints, and a **Ferry Fair** project |
| **Storybook Square** | A library, puppet stage, and post office; an owl storyteller | Extend Story Steps into clue-based endings and Word Monsters into meaningful word parts; add **Silly Describer** | Story Theater, alternative viewpoints, a locally saved illustrated newspaper, and **The Mixed-Up Invitations** mystery |
| **Discovery Marsh** | Boardwalks, ponds, and an observation hut; a patient heron naturalist | Extend Photo Safari into observation records and Sink or Float into repeatable trials; add a small **Habitat Helpers** model | Compare habitat needs, chart observations, test one variable, and design a visitor garden with several valid solutions |
| **Skywatch Isles** | Hilltop observatories and model-building decks; a moth astronomer | Extend Robot Path with a reusable routine and Bug Builder with a measured blueprint; extend **Light Lab** (built at Wonder Woods) with predictions and replays | Data stories, coordinate maps, a limited sun/shadow model, and **The Lantern Observatory** project |

**Light Lab** is built (pre-K–school): tap mirrors to turn a sunbeam onto sleeping flowers on generated, rule-tested grids, with colored glass and plan-then-shine levels. A Skywatch Isles extension could ask where the beam will land before it shines, then compare two beam paths in a replay. Any scientific explanation must match the simulated model; the game does not need general optics.

**The Woodland Picnic** is a later zone-specific use of the existing Windy Picnic pilot, not a second adventure framework. Other projects reuse the same pattern: a friend has a concrete need, two or three actions help, and the resulting scene remembers what the child made.

Every available zone stays open. Its activities offer supported entry modes; neither birthdays, test results, nor collecting enough stickers become access gates. Returning to the original island remains easy. The pet treehouse and discovery journal are shared destinations, so new zones enrich one personal world.

## What an expansion should contain

Use this as a default scope, not a fixed quota. A smaller release that deepens one favorite game is useful too.

1. **Two extensions to familiar games**, each with a new decision or form of expression.
2. **At most one new interaction family**, such as cooperative shaping, beam routing, or editable rules.
3. **One place or a small addition to a place**, with a memorable host, clear landmarks, and spoken navigation.
4. **One optional project** combining a few activities, with saved steps and a satisfying ending. Free play remains directly available.
5. **One creation or known keepsake** to revisit in the home/journal. Completed game rounds retain the ordinary one-sticker reward; the project layer does not duplicate those awards.
6. **Content review and verification**, including old-save behavior and regression checks for existing play.

After each expansion, decide what the child wants to repeat, what was confusing, and whether a fresh arrangement still makes sense. Expand the successful activity before filling out an entire proposed zone. Review the roadmap at these milestones, without dates, daily obligations, or a promise to deliver every idea.

### E2 — Add middle-elementary depth and choice

- [ ] Choose **Maker Harbor** or **Storybook Square** first based on the next desired skill area; build the other in a later expansion.
- [ ] Add the relevant extensions from the progression table: equal groups/parts and design, or word meaning and evidence in stories.
- [ ] Add one new interaction and one project per expansion, with several valid solutions where appropriate.
- [ ] Support saving a work in progress (a construction, program, or story) and returning directly to it from the journal.

### E3 — Support upper-elementary investigations and projects

- [ ] Build a small **Discovery Marsh** investigation before a broader habitat simulation; use explicit observations and a limited, explainable model.

- [ ] Add projects with several short sessions' worth of optional work, saved at meaningful steps; the child can stop after any step.
- [ ] Let the child compare two results, identify a useful piece of evidence, or explain a choice through picture/word choices or optional writing. Spoken assistance remains available.

Exit milestone: a project supports planning, testing, revising, and showing the result; different valid approaches reach the ending, and the child's evidence can be revisited alongside the creation.

## Shared systems: change them when the next slice needs them

| System | Current implementation | Proposed evolution and safeguard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Places and learning ranges** | A `Place` has one `band`; places and bands correspond one-to-one, and a place lists all games supporting that band | Give thematic zones their own stable identity and explicit activity entries. Keep band/range selection separate from geography. Preserve the original trail as familiar starting destinations; do not append one giant scrolling place per grade. |
| **Routing and home** | A game's return destination is its launching band | Carry the launching zone and its position as navigation context. The same game can return to the harbor, woods, or original place without duplicating its game ID. Keep the current route behavior until that migration is implemented and tested. |
| **Difficulty and progress** | One level/pin/history per game, constrained by the played band's ranges | Begin by extending coherent ladders. If branches teach different skills, give modes stable keys and define how old levels and pins map. Never silently renumber existing saved levels, award a grade, or infer a global age from performance. |
| **Game results and projects** | `RoundResult` contains only misses and hints; the shell saves the round and awards a sticker | Add typed results only for a concrete project need: a chosen route, created pattern, or observation. Save project steps and results once so retries/reloads cannot duplicate awards. Do not embed whole game scenes inside another game's lifecycle. |
| **Creations and journals** | Saved sticker ownership and normalized placement; no general project/creation model | Store small versioned descriptions of drawings, music, programs, and observations, with limits, explicit replacement, migration, and backup/restore. Keep data local and offline. |
| **Content and support** | Metadata includes broad skill labels and spoken line IDs | For new modes, author a learning goal, prior concepts, examples, acceptable answers, misconceptions, and graduated help. These are content guides, not automatic mastery tests or hard prerequisite locks. |

Resolve the place/band separation as part of **E1**, before multiple elementary zones depend on it. The new zone layout will require updating tests that currently assume exactly one place per band; those tests should continue protecting the original trail and verify every new route. Audit parent settings, birthdays, session behavior, and backups as part of that change. No such architecture change is implemented by this document.

Longer puzzles do not need faster fingers or mandatory reading. Keep the large touch targets, palette, synthesized audio, spoken guidance, and shared shell. Add structure through plans, evidence, and consequences, while leaving undo, experimentation, free play, and support available. Do not introduce currency grinding, timed access, online accounts, or care that deteriorates between visits.

## Educational references and content review

The broad sequence is informed by a few curriculum reference points, not a claim of complete alignment. Grade 3 math emphasizes multiplication/division, fractions, area, and shape reasoning; grade 5 develops fraction and decimal operations and volume. These provide useful places to check the number-game progression when authoring each mode. ([Grade 3 overview](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/3/introduction/), [Grade 5 overview](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/5/introduction/))

For later story activities, using details from a text to support an explanation and understanding word parts are useful reference goals. Adapt the response format to touch and spoken support; listening comprehension and independent decoding are different things to observe. ([Grade 5 literature](https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/RL/5/), [Foundational reading skills](https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/RF/5/))

For investigations, plan fair comparisons, control relevant variables, and use observations as evidence. Check the content and simulation limits before presenting a game result as a fact about the natural world. ([NGSS grades 3–5 engineering design](https://www.nextgenscience.org/topic-arrangement/3-5engineering-design))

Before promoting a mode to implemented status, review its facts, explanations, language, solvability, alternatives, and behavior with help. Record automated checks, device observations, and content-review gaps separately in [VERIFICATION.md](../VERIFICATION.md). Completion with hints is valuable play; it is not a standardized assessment of the child.
