# Puddle Island games

What each game does and the design decisions behind its rules: what counts as a miss, what a hint shows, how the levels grow, and what was learned while building it. The [README inventory](../README.md#the-island-and-its-games) is the one-line summary and the game count; `src/games/registry.ts` and each game's `logic.ts` are the source of truth; the grown-up how-to cards in `src/content/howto.ts` say how to play each one. Shared systems (the shell, saves, the treehouse, couch play) are in [DESIGN.md](DESIGN.md).

Games are grouped by subject, the stable IDs in `src/content/world.ts`. Each game also has a home land on the island map (`LANDS` in `src/content/lands.ts`); each land mostly holds one subject's games (five games moved lands in RW13 slice 1), and the art subject's land is Painted Canyon. Each entry gives the bands it plays in (from the registry) and, where there is one, what inspired it. Some games also play on the couch; [COUCH-PLAY.md](COUCH-PLAY.md) lists them.

When a game changes, update its entry here. A new game gets an entry in its subject.

## Bubble Beach (senses)

### Bubble Pop

*lap–school.* Its how-to intro names and pictures each level (a mode card, with a ladder of the band's levels easiest first on the intro). Tap bubbles as they float up; some hold a friend, who drops to the sand and makes its own animal sound, and the first of each friend in a round says its name ("A duck!"). Later, one color, numbers in order, then pairs, then counting sequences. Levels 7–8 and 10 show countable dots beneath the unchanged-size numeral (dice layouts through six, then five and some more); levels 9 and 11 fade that support. Every round floats in one of four places drawn at random: the shore (the sticker and map icon stay on it), a tiled bath with foam, a night sky with twinkling stars and a sleepy moon, or the sea with light rays, seaweed and three fish. Plain bubbles get a rim that shows in each; the scenery takes no touches. Count the dots together in co-play. A wrong bubble only wobbles and gets a spoken hint. Levels 10–11 (number bonds): every bubble has a partner that makes 5 (pre-K) or 10 (early school). Tap one to hold it still with a glow, then its partner: both pop with "3 and 2 make 5!" A wrong pair is a gentle miss that says both numbers and their total; two in a row glow a pair that works. Tapping the held bubble again lets it go. A sign at the top of the screen shows the total to make (two small bubbles, an equals bar and the numeral, with the dice dots on level 10), and takes no touches. Levels 12–14 are counting sequences, not sums, in the same drifting bubbles and with the same wobble, nag and glow after two misses: 12 starts at a number from 2 to 7 and counts up six (never past 12); 13 starts at a number from 5 to 10 and counts back to 1; 14 counts by tens (10 to 70) or fives (5 to 35) in two digits. Each shows its start, or its step with an arrow ("10s"), on the sign, and the spoken line names the start. Because drifting bubbles overlap, a touch goes to the bubble whose middle is nearest the finger (a play-through found taps landing on the wrong, overlapping bubble). **Level 15, pop anywhere** is lap's first level (her order is 15, 1, 2, 3, and a game she has never finished starts at the first): the same floating bubbles, but bigger, slower and fewer (four at a time, eight pops), and a touch anywhere on the screen pops the nearest bubble that is showing, however far, so a swat counts (a hand resting on the glass with more than three touches is still ignored); there is no wrong place and no miss, a touch with no bubble showing does nothing, and the rainbow bubble at the end is also popped by a touch anywhere. Two smooth rounds step her up to level 1.

### Peg Garden

*lap–pre-K · after Peggle and pachinko.* A pearl drops through a sea garden; each flower bud it touches blooms and chimes, and nothing is lost. Later levels ask for every flower, the orange ones, then aiming a launcher at a numbered flower and at 1, 2, 3 in order. `engine/ball.ts` simulates every board, so each numbered peg is reachable by some aim. Level 8, *Big buds* (the lap and toddler entry; lap order 8, 1 and toddler 8, 1, 2): four huge buds (radius 60) sit in a diamond, every touch drops a pearl and blooms one, with no misses or hints. Otherwise it stays at one lap level. The bloom level always finishes: after two pearls in a row that wake no new flower (the same spot tapped again and again), the next tap's pearl is let go over a waiting flower instead of the tapped spot, and at that level there is no drift and the hint adds a ring on the ledge at that spot. On the island the whole screen takes touches (the sea beside the board maps to the nearest point of the ledge), and every touch at levels 1–3 makes a ring of bubbles; a touch while a pearl falls makes no second pearl (at level 1 it queues the next, one at most, never past six). Dropped pearls step at a fixed 1/240 s so play matches the simulation; aimed levels and couch keep frame-step physics. A cancelled aim fires nothing. At the numbered levels (4–5) only the asked number opens: another numbered bud chimes and wiggles, and plain buds still bloom; after each aimed shot its real path stays as faint dots until the next shot starts (feedback only, counts nothing). Finished pearls roll into a scalloped shell tray in the sand (twelve visible slots) and pour back up at the finish. Each seeded round draws one of four board shapes (rows, diamond, hourglass, waves) over one of three backdrops (shallows, reef, kelp forest); the newer shapes keep buds at least 64 units from the side walls. Couch play keeps the first look (rows, shallows) with no tray or trail, so its boards are unchanged. Known open: on the `rows` shape a pearl dropped near the wall can wedge until the 10-second cutoff ends the shot ([dive gap 12](deep-dives/peg-garden.md#the-game-today)).

### Bounce Back

*lap–school · after Pong and air hockey.* A slow, giggly ball between two big paddles: bounces are counted together toward a goal. A grown-up takes the left paddle on the play-together level, and the pet steps in when no second finger is there. A miss keeps the rally, and a hint (a slower ball and a ring) ends on her next bounce. Later levels aim through stars, count a rally of ten, and (level 6, pre-K and school) bounce the ball to the color the pet calls: the pet only calls a zone her paddle can reach, another color is a free try with its name said, and two tries without success show where to hold the paddle. School levels 7 and 8 score goals past the pet: a slow keeper (it can cover only part of the table while the ball crosses, so a good shot always gets by) and pips for each side; first to 5, then first to 7 with the ball speeding up through a rally. Level 9 has the pet call the three colors in an order, shown as dots on top, and she sends the ball to each in turn (a wrong color is a free try at the same one). Levels 10 and 11 count a rally of ten bounces by twos (to 20) and by fives (to 50), the pet and the number on the table saying each one. A win and a loss both finish with a sticker; a win counts a miss for every two goals against and a loss counts them all, so adaptation reads the margin. On the play-together and count levels the pet lobs a little, so the ball does not always return straight. Each round is on a pond, beach or snow court with its own ball. On the couch, a face-off trip's Bounce Back stop is a real face-off: goals at both ends, first to five, the ball served to whoever let it by and speeding through a rally.

### Bumper Garden

Each seeded round chooses a Meadow arch, Pond columns or Hive ring. Pond bumpers are tinted lily pads with ripples and a low croak-bell; Hive bumpers are hexagonal flowers with higher wooden notes. The same missions, collision sizes and accounting apply to every table.

*lap–pre-K · after Pokémon Pinball and 3D Pinball Space Cadet.* A ladybug ball bounces around flower bumpers on a garden table. It never drains: past the flippers a flower pot catches it and pops it back up. Tapping either half of the screen flips that side's flipper; young levels get a wider reach. At levels 2–6, 8 and 9 a pulsing ring at the nearer flipper tip shows when the falling ladybug is within its actual reach; it changes no physics and counts no hint. Levels: launch for fun (ends after six launches), flip until every flower blooms (the pet counts each bloom aloud, as it does at the color level), bloom one color (ends when all of that color have bloomed), bump numbered flowers in order (ends at the last numbered flower). Level 8, *Not this color*, asks "Bloom every flower that is not {color}!" and, 1.4 s later, the asked color's flowers fall asleep (faded, tilted, a bobbing "z", on all three tables): they are never wanted and bumping one only chimes. The plan has 8 flowers, 2 of the asked color and 3 each of two others; the asked color is always the fewest and at least 2 flowers are wanted, all reachable by an aimed launch. A launch that blooms nothing wanted is a miss and the usual two-in-a-row glow and aimed launch apply (a counted hint; hint accounting on this level is an open decision). The finale wakes the sleepy flowers. Level 6, *Count on*, starts at a seeded number from 2–6 and asks for three or four consecutive flowers. Level 9, *Count back*, starts at 5–8 and descends to 1. Both show a number strip highlighting the next target and speak the starting instruction; an out-of-order bump only chimes. Pre-K follows the explicit order 3, 4, 6, 8, 5, 9; ID 7 remains unbuilt. On goal levels a shot that blooms nothing wanted is a gentle miss; two in a row light the wanted flowers and aim the next launch at one (found by simulating the table).

### Fruit Slice

*toddler–school · after Fruit Ninja's Zen mode and Toca Kitchen's chopping board; [definition](definitions/fruit-slice.md).* Big fruit lofts from a basket and hangs near the top of a slow arc (at least 1.6 s); a swipe across it splits it with a pop, drops and a rising pentatonic note. Nothing is timed and missed fruit drifts back down, so there are no bombs, lives or clock. Levels: 1 Touch it (any contact slices; a tap works), 2 Swipe across it, 3 Only one kind (the sign shows the kind), 4 Only fruit (a balloon or flower bounces off), 5 Stop at the number (the sign counts; the bowl lids when full and a tap sends it), 6 Two kinds (a row each, with its own count), 7 One straight line (draw one line that cuts exactly N fruit; it follows the finger from touch-down and cuts on lift), 8 Pieces by twos (each slice drops two pieces and the bowl counts by twos; the last sign is a still board of three pairs cut with the straight-line tool). Toddler 1–2, preschool 2–4, pre-K 3–6, school 5–8. A miss is one refused cut (wrong kind, not fruit, a cut past the sign or a finished row, or a level 7 line through the wrong number); one stroke counts at most one miss, and the right fruit in it still count. The refused fruit bounces with a boing and the pet says why; from the second miss the right fruit glows and counts one hint, and the pause sheet's "Show a hint" gives the same. Level 7 boards always have lines through N and N−1, and through N+1 only when N is below 4. Board box 820×400 units, translated and never scaled. Strokes end on pointer up, up outside or cancel. The ghost-finger bot waits until its 180-unit stroke is clear of other fruit. Nothing is saved and there is no couch play.

### Domino Run

*toddler–school · after domino toppling videos; [definition](definitions/domino-run.md).* Drag dominoes from a tray into the sockets of a lane (at level 2 a tap on the tray domino also hops it into the glowing gap), then tap the green starter: the line clacks down and the last domino rings the bell. A domino falls if the one before it reaches it (reach equals size) and it is at most one size bigger; a bell is size 1 and a gong is size 4, so only a giant rings it. The size shows on the domino's dot face. Tapping a placed domino sends it back to the tray; dropping on another placed one swaps them. A chain that stalls is never a miss: the fallen dominoes stand back up and the pet says why (a gap too wide, or a push too big). Misses are always 0. From the second stall on a line at levels 3–8 the next placement glows and counts one hint; the pause sheet's "Show a hint" gives the same glow and counts once; a line that already rings just says "Tap the green one!". Levels: 1 Tip the line (tap any domino), 2 One missing (a glowing gap, tap-to-hop), 3 Fill the gaps, 4 Tall ones reach farther, 5 Two bells (a fork, two lines), 6 Growing dominoes (a gong), 7 Just enough, 8 Two bells, one tray. Toddler 1–2, preschool 2–4, pre-K 3–5, school 4–8. Lines are seeded and checked by a tipping-rule test (the authored first lines' exact solutions; 500 seeds across the eight plans never use the fallback). The ghost-finger bot drags the hint placement, then taps the starter. Nothing is saved and there is no couch play.

### Puddle Gobble

*toddler–school · after Hole.io, Donut County and Katamari's growing threshold; [definition](definitions/puddle-gobble.md).* A smiling rain puddle sits on the beach. She leads it with a finger (a drag, or a tap where it should go; the whole screen steers, as in Duckling Parade) and it soaks up the shells, pebbles and driftwood that fit inside it: a thing fits when its tier (1–4, shown as dots) is at most the puddle's, and the puddle grows a tier after every two soaks (levels 2–3) or three (level 4, a drop meter on its rim); level 1 does not grow. A thing that is still too big wobbles and the puddle says "Too big yet"; that is never a miss at any level. At the end every soaked thing washes up in a row, smallest to biggest. Levels: 1 Soak it up (no growth), 2 Too big yet (grow every two), 3 Bigger and bigger (grow every two), 4 Count to grow (every three, a drop meter), 5 Smallest first (a stated order, no growth), 6 Bigger number (numerals on the things, the puddle gains 1 per soak, numerals alone at pre-K), 7 Add to grow (the puddle gains each thing's number, with a sum strip along the top), 8 Just right (reach exactly the pail's number: soaking past it is refused, and tapping a token on the strip lets that thing go, free). Toddler 1–2, preschool 2–4, pre-K 3–6, school 5–8; no lap entry. Misses come only at level 5 (a bigger thing before a smaller one) and level 8 (an overshoot), one per thing per round, and only when she **aimed** at it: the finger's destination is on that thing, no fitting thing is nearer the finger, and nothing has been soaked since she last aimed (a pass-over on the way only wobbles, and a step-aside the game makes after a let-go is never her aim). This aim gate is a deliberate accounting choice beyond the definition. After two aimed too-big tries since her last soak (levels 2–4 and 6–8) or two misses (levels 5 and 8) the next fitting thing glows: free at levels 1–3, from level 4 it counts one hint until she soaks again; the pause sheet's "Show a hint" glows and counts one hint per glow. Level 8's hint glows a soak on an exact solution, or the latest strip token to let go. After a let-go the puddle steps off the thing's spot and the thing re-arms once the puddle moves 30 units away or changes size. The first round of each level is authored once per session; later rounds are seeded boards, and a rule test checks that no level 1–7 board can dead-end and that level 8's hint reaches the pail within eight steps from every reachable state. The ghost-finger bot follows the hint target, steering round things in its way and tapping let-go tokens. Co-play: name a thing before the other steers there. Off-screen: nest cups from smallest to biggest. Nothing is saved and there is no couch play.

### Sea Lift

*school (6–8) · after number-line and elevator-floor work on integers; [definition](definitions/sea-lift.md).* A glass lift runs up a pier from a cliff ledge to the sea floor, with the water line as stop zero and stops from −5 to 5; landings zig-zag either side of the shaft at an even pitch, to scale, so every stop is on one screen. A friend's request shows on a fixed card left of the shaft (an arrow and one dot per stop). She taps the landing she thinks it names and the lift rides there, ticking each stop and splashing as it passes the water, and the friend counts it out ("two below the water"; speech never says minus or negative). A round is five rides (level 3: three pick-up cases) and earns one sticker, with or without help. The first round of each level in a session is authored; the demonstration always opens on it, shortened to two rides or one case. Nothing is saved or filed in the journal.

Levels (school only, 1–3): 1 *Up or down?* five requests from the dock, n stops above or below the water; 2 *Through the water* five rides that cross zero, the request naming only a count and a direction; 3 *Deepest first* three pick-up cases, deepest or highest first with at least two friends below the water, picked up by tapping the landing (or the friend, which counts as that landing); the friend card or the car replays the request.

Misses and hints: one miss per distinct wrong stop per request; the lift rides there and the friend counts it out, then it returns. Two misses glow the right stop (one hint, once per request; "Show a hint" in the pause sheet does the same); a third distinct wrong stop sends the lift there by itself, a supported finish. Never a miss: the stop the lift is already at, an empty landing, a picked-up friend at level 3, and replays. The pause sheet offers Start again and Show a hint. Touch only; no couch play.

## Music Mountain (music)

### Jelly Drums

*lap–school.* Free play on five notes, then copying tunes of two to five notes. In the echo levels a yellow listen-again button (a music note, above the stage) replays the whole tune from the first note whenever it is her turn (slowly, with the first jelly glowing, once she has needed help); it counts no miss or hint, and is hidden in couch play. Level 10, *One big jelly* (lap only, placed first with `order`), is the easiest way in: one enormous red low jelly stands alone and a touch anywhere on the stage sings the nearest awake one; the little blue high jelly wakes after 6 touches, then the others at 10, 14 and 18, and 24 touches finish, with no miss and no hint. The island's background loop is silenced here (the jellies still bob to its quiet clock), so nothing competes with the notes she plays. In the echo levels (4–9) a round never plays the same tune twice, and tunes have shapes: a staircase up or down, a hop or a wiggle, and (levels 5–6 Hot Cross Buns; level 9 also Mary Had a Little Lamb and Jingle Bells) songs whose title is spoken first; it never draws the kind it just played. In free play (levels 1–3) after 8 seconds without a touch the last-touched jelly wiggles and glows; nothing is counted.

### Song Maker

*lap–school.* A looping grid of jelly beads; higher rows sound higher (pentatonic steps), and the playhead follows the music clock's beat (`music.beats()`). Free play at lap and toddler; then copying a song from shadow notes, then from a small card (levels 3–5 draw twelve authored motifs per shape, preserving their rests, with steps, repeats and at most one leap; the previous three motif choices are excluded within the loaded profile’s session, and demonstrations leave that history alone); continuing a repeating pattern (level 6 has ten named shapes, such as stairs, hops, zigzags, an arch and a valley, and the pet says the shape's name aloud before play); and finding a four-note tune by ear, where a wrong jelly is followed by the right note so the difference can be heard. A song from a free level with at least two lit jellies can be kept on the treehouse's tune plaque (the round finishes on its tap count either way); a copied song is not hers and is not offered. The background loop is silenced so the song is the only music; its clock keeps running at the game's tempo for the playhead.

### Sound Garden

*lap–pre-K · after Electroplankton.* Garden creatures sing when touched: each round seats a seeded five of six (bird, frog, bunny, turtle, bell, woodpecker) in a seeded order, and each sings its listening role (a high chirp, a low pitched ribbit, four quick drum beats for the bunny, two slow tocks for the turtle, the bell's tune, the woodpecker's knock), so the pictures have been heard before the questions; level 1 ends after twelve songs. Listening questions follow, with seeded sounds that differ between questions: high or low (bird or frog; the two tiles sing their notes as they appear), fast or slow (bunny or turtle; same note and span, twice the beats), does the tune go up or down (a 3–5 note tune, down being up reversed), then echoing a woodpecker's rhythm on a drum, judged forgivingly for wobble but not for the wrong rhythm. The background loop is silenced, so the garden's own sounds are the only music and a fast-or-slow question is not blurred by a tempo underneath.

Level 7 adds three pictured paths: rising steps, falling steps and a flat path. A seeded three- or four-note tune goes up, down or repeats one pitch; the instrument, duration and note times match across choices, and the same answer never appears three times running. Six supported answers finish; pre-K plays 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10. Wrong choices get a gentle retry and replay; two misses glow the right path and count one hint.

Level 5 draws a dot under the woodpecker at each knock, spaced in proportion to the heard gaps. The dots fade after the first drum tap and return on replay. Each round draws from twelve rhythms: two short patterns, one three-gap pattern and a four-gap final pattern. A good echo opens the sleepy tree's eyes and brings an answering knock flourish. After two misses, the drum lights for the first and each remaining tap, following the child's taps at the same relative gaps. Replaying is free; two misses offer one hint, and four echoed rhythms earn one sticker even with support. There is no absolute timing score or speed target. Ghost-finger demonstrations cover both levels.

Level 8, One big friend, is the lap entry before level 1 (lap order 8, 1; toddler stays 1–2). One large friend sings on touch-down anywhere in the view except the shell's top corner controls and bottom-left pet corner. Four taps bring a wave and the next friend walking in; quick taps during the handoff still sound. Twelve songs finish with no misses or hints. Each round features three friends from a seeded five-of-six cast; the cast varies across rounds. New taps replace any delayed beats of the previous call. Level 1's individual targets and tap cooldown remain unchanged. Entry ID 8 lands at the next band's easiest level on a birthday, without renumbering saved levels or pins.

Level 9, Sing Higher, adds a vertical flower stem with six pentatonic pitches (steps 2–7). Moving the bird auditions each snapped pitch; lifting commits one note. The bell, frog and bunny take turns joining on the step below. Twelve released notes finish the creative round with no misses or hints. A cancelled drag commits nothing. Preschool plays 2, 3, 4, 9; toddler remains 1–2.

Level 10, Copy the shape, plays two two-note contours (up, down), then four seeded three-note contours (rising, falling, arch and valley). The left bird sings and shows its path; tap it to replay freely. Drag the right singer and lift for each note. Any starting pitches and intervals with the same directions count. A complete wrong contour boings and replays; after two misses, one hint lights viable next pitches. A starting note with no room to continue gets a fresh try without a miss. Six shapes finish, including with support. Both levels keep the pet and corner controls clear and have drag demonstrations; iPad sound and child understanding remain unchecked.

### Little Conductor

*toddler–pre-K (levels 1–3; toddlers play 1–2) · after Peter and the Wolf and Fantasia.* Three friends on a bandstand play one seeded four-beat pentatonic tune (a hop, a slide or a wave) over and over. A turtle pad plays it slowly (60 beats a minute) and a rabbit pad quickly (90); the notes and their order never change, only the pace. The first tap starts the band at once; after that a change waits for the next beat and shows a pending ring with a clock, and tapping the pad that is already playing calls the waiting change off. Four beat marks under the playing pad fill as the tune goes by. Level 1 lets her play at either pace. Level 2 adds a check beside each pad once she has heard its tune whole. Level 3 (preschool and pre-K) adds a fourth seeded friend, the listener, who holds a 200-unit sign showing the turtle or rabbit (the pads' picture and word) for the current half of the tune; the two requests are always one of each, in a seeded order, and the band holds silent between phrases. A pad tap is her pick and plays that half once at that pace (free, repeatable, never counted); Listen again replays the half at the picked pace; the green check submits. Check with nothing picked wiggles the pads and says to pick first (not counted). A wrong pick boings, bounces the sign, names the requested pace and counts one miss, and the pick stays. Two wrong Checks on one half glow the requested pad and the band previews that pace once, counted as one hint, at most once per half; the pause sheet's Show a hint does the same. A right pick plays the half at that pace while the listener nods, then the sign turns to the second request; after the second half the band and listener bow and the round ends with one sticker. Misses and hints at level 3 feed the adaptive level. The idle turtle pulse is off at level 3. The green Done is dim until the heard gate: at level 1 a whole phrase at the pace she last chose; at level 2 both paces heard whole, then the chosen one whole since she chose it; a waiting change closes the gate at once. Done before then blinks the beats still unlit and says to hear it whole (or to hear the other pace); it is never a miss. Listen again restarts the tune from its first note and keeps the heard marks. At the gate Done plays the tune once more with claps, the band bows and the round ends with one sticker. At levels 1–2 there are no misses and no hints, so a round finishes with both at 0. After six idle seconds before the first tap the turtle pad pulses once, which is not a hint. Fast is never the better pace. The band plays at half volume while the pet is speaking, and the background loop is silenced. At levels 1–2 the ghost finger taps turtle, then rabbit once the turtle tune has been heard whole, then Done; at level 3 it reads the sign, taps that pad, waits for the audition and taps Check. Nothing is saved. Not checked on a device: whether 60 and 90 sound clearly different from an iPad speaker, whether a two-year-old reads the pads and the pending ring, whether the drum masks the melody, and how device speech says turtle and rabbit.

### Rhythm Neighbors

*lap–school · after Rhythm Heaven and call-and-response songs.* Two lap modes let taps make a bird call and frog chorus; they end by themselves after eight frog taps (the arrow still stops earlier), and after six quiet seconds a frog wiggles and croaks once, never counted. Level 7, *Knock, knock* (the lap entry, lap order 7, 1, 2), fills the left with a big tree: every knock on it sounds, and one croak answers it about 0.45 seconds later with a ripple on the pond; eight knocks finish, a tap on the frog croaks uncounted, and an idle wiggle waits. Level 8, *My turn, your turn* (toddler order 2, 8, 3), has the bird call two or three knocks, then one frog glows and waits: one tap answers, three exchanges finish, with no misses or hints. Finishing any answered round (levels 3–6 and 8) files the journal page "The frogs answered the woodpecker". Later levels alternate a two- or three-knock bird call with a different pictured frog reply drawn from twelve long/short shapes (two or three notes at levels 3–4, three or four at 5–6; the call is never the reply's pattern, and at 3–4 its knock count differs from the reply's note count), add a second frog (a green frog and a teal frog that croaks lower, alternating from a seeded first frog), then short/long rhythm gaps. The relative-gap judge is shared with Sound Garden. Explicit submission avoids any deadline; at levels 5–6 a gap over 2.5 s inside an unfinished reply just clears her taps with a spoken "take your time" (no miss). A tap past the reply's length sounds and costs nothing. Replay demonstrates both parts. After two misses, or when help is requested, guided play highlights the next frog and accepts the correct sequence at any pace. Three exchanges each end in a joinable song: the bird knocks its call, every frog plays the last reply together and her frog taps sound along un-judged, then one sticker. The background loop is silenced, so the bird and frog parts are the only rhythm.

### Beat Builder

*preschool–school · after Chrome Music Lab's Rhythm and step sequencers.* Toddlers play level 1 with a gentler Done: the green check appears once one square has sounded all the way round, a one-sound beat is a whole beat, and turning every square off takes the check away again (no misses, no hints). Level 9 shows three cards (circle, square, circle returns): turn the cards to bring the first part back, edit the parts if you like, hear the song, then check. Level 8 makes a piece of two parts (circle and square): make one, copy it, change the copy, hear both, then check. Level 7 locks the drum and asks her to move the claps to match a heard target (a clap-only replay button helps). Level 6 (mend the beat) starts a copy that differs from a small model in one square; add or remove it, then check. A step-sequencer grid with a looping playhead: rows are drum, clap and bell, columns are 4 or 8 steps. Free play reveals a green check with at least three active squares and two playhead returns to the start since the last edit; one composition finishes with that check. Visible copying completes three exact matches automatically; ear copying uses replay/check for three matches (two on the longer three-row grid); repeating completes two exact matches automatically. The how-to states these controls and counts separately for each of the five modes. Copying a visible beat leaves wrong squares off as gentle misses; copying by ear has free two-loop replay and a check. Editing/checking waits until the replay finishes; tapping the note again restarts it freely. Two wrong checks show glowing plus marks for missing sounds and minus marks for extra sounds; these update after each edit. Repeat levels give the first half and ask for the second to match, with missing-sound marks after two misses. Help counts once per target; the ghost finger can add or remove sounds. Bell taps and loop playback use the same pitch for each column. Targets at levels 2–5 come from twelve named four-step drum/clap phrases and eight named eight-step drum/clap/bell phrases, selected with the round seed without replacement. Level 4 repeats one short phrase twice. Each nickname is spoken before play; no reading is needed. Every row has sound and rests, the drum opens, and the long phrases never sound all three instruments together. Level 1 stays free composition, and sticker art uses its own unchanged seed. The woodland clearing frames the unchanged grid. A bear responds to the drum, a bunny to the clap and (on the three-row grid) a cat to the bell, during square previews and target/player playback. Trees, the wooden stage and friends take no touches. The game uses a near-silent `quiet` music style so the child's beat is the music.

## Painted Canyon (art)

### Rainbow Fingers

*lap–school.* Finger painting that grows over seven levels: rainbow or color-pot painting, paint pots that say their color, coloring pages ("paint the sun yellow", then "what color is an apple?"), mixing colors in a bowl, then remembering that a pumpkin is orange, pouring red and yellow into the bowl and painting it. Level 6 (Just a few) is the pre-K entry, placed before level 1 in pre-K's order (6, 1, 2, 3): three critters and one hoop, with one critter that fits already inside as a worked example; two are left to sort and one of them fits. After two wrong drops the hint glows the critter she just missed (in its right place), not the first unplaced one. Preschool does not offer it yet. Levels 1, 2, 3 and 5 also open with a worked example: after the pet says the rule it points out a critter already in its place ("Look, the bear is already in its place."), one in the hoop at level 1, one in each hoop at level 2, one in the middle only at level 3 and, at level 5, one that is not like the picture. Examples are never moved by the bot or the hint, and at least two critters are left to carry. Every part of the diagram gets at least one critter and exactly one offered rule sorts the guessing round that way (rule-tested). A wrong hoop is a miss with a reason ("the cow is not brown"; "the dog is brown and has floppy ears, so it goes in the middle"); putting a critter back on bare grass is never a miss. At levels 2, 3 and 5, a code-drawn leaf mat below the hoops is the explicit outside answer: critters that fit neither hoop go there at 2–3, and those that fit the crossed-out picture go there at 5. A correct mat drop gets a spoken reason; a wrong mat drop explains the hoop it belongs in. Every critter must reach a hoop or the mat before these rounds finish. The two-miss glow and ghost finger include the mat and keep the hint on the missed critter. One-hoop entries 1 and 6 still allow outsiders to stay on the grass. Level 7, **Where does the new one go?**, is school only: five critters are already sorted across the two overlapping hoops and mat; the sixth is the only movable newcomer. Each diagram retains an example of left, right, both and neither, and all four newcomer destinations occur across seeds. Place the newcomer in each of three diagrams to finish; wrong places get the usual reason and a glow after two misses. Level 8, **A new sign**, is school only: sort six critters into one hoop or onto the leaf mat, then the sign flips and the same six hop back to the grass. The pet names the new rule and all six are sorted again. Both rules have in/out examples and classify the group differently. Both passes are needed for one sticker; mistakes and hints accumulate across them, with the glow cleared when the sign changes. The ghost finger sorts both passes. Level 9, **This but not that**, adds one hoop with a paired sign: the first picture must fit and the crossed-out second picture must not. Each seeded group has an accepted critter, an A-and-B exclusion and one matching neither. Correct and wrong placements explain both parts; bare grass stays free and two misses glow the missed critter’s destination. Sort all six into the hoop or onto the leaf mat in each of two diagrams for one sticker. The ghost finger follows the same A-and-not-B rule. Level 11, **How many?**, sorts six critters into two separate hoops and the leaf mat, with groups of one, two and three. After sorting, tap any critter to count its part aloud: a ring marks it and its part’s badge grows once. Repeated taps replay its assigned number without inflating the count or recording misses/hints; counting is optional support. Choose the pictured part with more using the three buttons below. Wrong comparison choices give a spoken quantity comparison and a retry; two wrong choices reveal totals and glow the largest part, adding one hint for that diagram. Sorting retains its usual miss/help accounting. Complete two sort-and-compare diagrams for one sticker; the ghost finger sorts, counts all parts and chooses the largest. School follows IDs (2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 11), skipping reserved 10; existing saved levels and pins keep their meaning.

### Bolt Boards

*toddler–school · after screw-pin puzzle apps; [definition](definitions/bolt-boards.md).* Wooden boards hang on the barn's pegboard wall on big bolts, with a fruit or a peeking friend behind. She taps a bolt and it spins out with a ratchet click: a board with two bolts left stays put, with one it swings and hangs like a picture on a nail, with none it slides softly down to the woodpile and the fruit hops into the basket. Poses are computed exactly (no physics). A bolt under another board is covered and waits. Boards are frozen, solver-checked shapes (mirrored and re-dressed by seed); one sticker per round, with or without help.

Levels: 1 one bolt per board, all hanging; 2 two bolts hold a board; 3 boards lying on top of others; 4 swings that cover a free bolt; 5 a friend to uncover (other boards may stay up); 6 every bolt needs an open hole to be parked in, open holes lit; 7 holes and a friend, holes unlit. Toddler plays 1–2, preschool 1–4, pre-K 2–6, school 4–7. At levels 6–7 she taps a bolt, then an open hole (or drags it), putting it back is free and so is undo, and the pet says "Stuck!" when no bolt can move. No misses at 1–3 (a covered tap only wobbles the board above and names it); from level 4 a covered tap is one miss, and at level 7 a covered hole is one miss (free at level 6). The two-miss glow and, at 6–7, a wander hint show the first move of a shortest solution; levels 1–5 can never dead-end. Level 6's first board is authored: one order of taking the upper board's bolts leaves her stuck, the other opens the hole.

## Counting Cove (numbers)

### Duck Pond

*lap–school.* Count along as ducks hop in; then put N in, how many?, adding and taking away. The dots beside a number are there to count. Level 10 (make ten): 3–9 ducks swim in. "How many more to make 10?" is answered on lily pads. The missing ducks then swim in to fill the pond's ten places ("7 and 3 make 10!"). A wrong pad counts on aloud from the ducks already there. Level 11 (hiding ducks): the sign gives a whole from 5 to 10, some ducks paddle beneath a code-drawn bridge, and the child finds the hidden part on the lily pads. A wrong pad counts on from the visible ducks to the whole. A right answer brings the hidden ducks out one by one, counted aloud, into separate pond seats. At levels 8–11 the sign then shows the addition or subtraction sentence and the pet reads it; reading is not needed to answer. Level 12 (Bye-bye ducks, ID 12; lap and toddler entry, placed first in their order): five ducks swim on the pond, and tapping one makes it wave and swim off while the voice counts down ("Bye-bye! 4 left"). After the last, the mother duck calls and all five swim back, counted up. One song is one round; there are no questions, so no misses or hints are possible. A departing duck takes no further taps.

### Monster Munch

*lap–school.* A code-drawn monster gapes, chews, burps, and shows eaten food in a tummy window. It complements Duck Pond rather than repeating it: lap levels feed by tapping, toddler levels count along and give one cookie to each monster, and preschool levels ask for an exact order (cookies, or cookies and apples) that the child completes by ringing a bell, deciding for herself when to ring. A full monster refuses another of that food, which counts as a miss. Pre-K shares cookies fairly among two or three monsters; unequal shares hand the extras back to the tray, then the child says how many each monster got. Lap level 9 (*Big bites*, the lap entry before level 1) is two rounds of three big cookies: a tap on a cookie or on the monster feeds the next one, the belly grows with each bite, and nothing can be missed. Toddler level 10 (*One of each*, after level 3) gives two, then three, monsters a cookie and an apple each; a second of the same food to a monster is refused and counts as a miss. Level 8 (leftovers): the tray holds one or two more cookies than share evenly, and the bell says "I think it's fair". Uneven shares hand the extras back; ringing while everyone could still have one more is a gentle miss. Then two questions: how many each, and how many left over (those go to the pet). **Counting and answers.** Levels 1 and 2 play two rounds (4 then 6 cookies; 3 then 5). Level 1 counts each bite aloud, and a tap on the monster feeds it the next cookie; levels 4–6 count each bite of its kind ("2 cookies!"). A wrong number pad counts one monster's tummy together, ringing each icon as it is counted; at level 8's leftover question it counts the cookies on the tray. The hint line follows the glow (a cookie, the bell, a monster or a pad). A drop short of any monster is still no miss: it says "Hm?" and the nearest monster leans toward it. The level-7 card no longer mentions a bell, since the tray emptying triggers the fairness check. Whether per-bite counting keeps pace with quick feeding on device speech is unjudged.

### Little Helpers

*lap–school (lap plays levels 1–2) · after Pikmin.* Tap a fruit (or pumpkin, watermelon, acorn, pinecone, blueberries, a toy boat) and a helper runs to carry it home; bigger things need more helpers, shown by a badge of hollow rings that fill as each helper arrives (at 4 and 5 a numeral sits over the rings; extras show as orange rings outside the frame). Each round is seeded into an orchard (basket), a beach (rowing boat) or the woods (bucket), and the same thing does not come twice running where the place has another. Needs never fall into a plain 1, 2, 1, 2 swap. Later levels wait for a whistle so the child sends exactly enough: too few cannot lift, extras walk back. The level before equal groups asks how many more are needed when some are already helping: the empty rings are the gap, and a short whistle says it ("3 are here. It needs 5. 2 more!"). **Level 9 *Whistle when it's full*** (toddler after 2, preschool after 2) is the gentlest whistle: four things needing 1–3, the whistle wiggles when every ring is full; an early whistle strains and blinks the empty rings, and a helper sent past full bounces off and walks back, all with no miss or hint. Level 6 (equal groups): a bunch of two or three same-size fruits that each need the same team of two or three (2×2, 3×2, 2×3, each once before any repeats). The badge groups its dots by fruit and helpers line up in a team under each fruit. A wrong whistle counts by the team size ("2, 4, 6"), and each lift ends with "3 groups of 2 make 6". **Every touch answers.** At level 1 a touch anywhere (not only the fruit) sends a helper. While a fruit is dropping, lifting or being praised, any touch makes the nearest helper hop and squeak; at levels 2–6 a tap on the sky or grass makes the nearest waiting helper wave and chirp, and a tap on a waiting helper that cannot be sent home hops it. With no helpers left, a fruit tap makes the crowd shrug and the pet says "Everyone is busy helping!" (no boing, no miss). A whistle with nobody sent is a nudge ("Send some helpers first! Tap the fruit."), not a miss; from level 3 only a whistle with the wrong number counts. Every fruit of a bunch goes into the basket. Whether a small child notices the hop and wave is unjudged.

### Pet Kitchen

*toddler–school · after Cooking Mama and JumpStart's cafeteria.* Cut one or two sandwiches into halves or quarters, move equal pieces between plates, then serve. Both halves and quarters are accepted when they share the wholes equally. A round-arrow control restores the wholes to try another cut. The school ladder then asks for a named fraction of one sandwich (a half, a quarter or three quarters; two quarters count as a half), halves an even picture recipe for one friend, then cuts one pizza into three equal slices for three friends. The earlier two recipe levels double two kinds of fruit; every recipe has undo. Only unsuccessful serving checks count as misses; cutting, arranging and undo are exploration. A missed serve says why: serving before the cut repeats the instruction, too few pieces for the friends (halves for four) says to cut more, an empty serve says nothing is on the plates yet, and unequal plates say to move a piece to a plate with less; two misses highlight a useful next action (the working cut before anything is cut, the recut button for too few pieces). A touch on the uncut food wiggles it and repeats the instruction (at most every 3 s, never a miss), and six seconds of quiet before the cut bounce the cut buttons. At the named-fraction level a fair serve counts the pieces aloud ("one quarter, two quarters, three quarters") before the round ends. The Windy Picnic plays a Pet Kitchen round as its sandwich step.

### Frog Hop

*preschool–school · after number-line hopping games and Frogger.* A number line of lily pads. Levels: find a number; one more and one less; add by hopping; take away by hopping back; how many hops between two numbers (number cards); a line to 20 that slides to keep the start and answer in view; then split a shown sum that crosses ten by first choosing the hops to 10 and then landing the rest; level 8 is the mirror, a take-away from 11 to 16 (taking away 4 to 8) that first chooses the hops back to 10 and then lands the rest. At level 7 the sign first shows the whole sum (such as `8 + 5`), then the split (`8 + 2 + 3`); level 8 reads `13 − 5` and then the split the same way; the pet explains the total and each part. A sign shows the sum (`4 + 3`, `7 − 3`, `3 → 8`). Wrong-way hops and one-too-many or one-too-few hops get specific spoken feedback. Two misses bring a counted demonstration, the frog hopping and counting aloud, then the answer glows. Each question records at most one hint even when both stages at ten need help. On card questions, the second pad tap prompts and bounces the cards for free. Five answered questions finish the round for one sticker, including with help.

### Fruit Sums

*school · levels 1–3 · after Donkey Kong Jr. Math's* Calculate *mode (the notebook's "Make Twelve").* A hanging sign names a number and the tree drops five or six numbered fruit. Tap two fruit and they hop into the two boxes (tap a fruit in a box to put it back, or tap a third fruit to swap the newest), then tap the green check. **Any way that makes the number counts**, and a round asks for **two different ways**: the first is celebrated aloud ("7 plus 5 makes 12!") and listed under the sign, then "can you make 12 a different way?". The same sum the other way round, or the same one again, is "the same way" and is not a miss; a tray is built around two ways, so a round can always be finished. A try that makes another number is a miss and the check shows what it made ("8"), spoken as "3 plus 5 makes 8. We need 12."; the fruit stay in their boxes so she can swap one and check again, and after two misses on a sum the first fruit of a way she has not found glows (one hint). Levels: 1 add to make 4 to 10 from five fruit; 2 add to make 11 to 20; 3 a plus leaf and a take-away leaf choose the sign (the fruit tapped first is the one to start from), targets 5 to 15 from six fruit up to 20, and the tray always allows both a sum and a take-away. Taking away more than she has says so and is not a miss. Rules, tray generation and layout are in `logic.ts`: a test checks every seeded tray at every level has at least two distinct ways, stays inside the level's numbers, and keeps every target at least 100 units clear of the pet corner at five views. The second way is the whole point: many routes reach one answer. Notebook sketch's *times*, three-fruit sums and the grown-up "make 24" are not built; two people sharing the tray is a co-play prompt only, since seat modes wait on the family-table decisions.

### Orchard Rows

*school · levels 1–3.* Tap a whole empty row to grow its trees, then tap every full row to count equal groups aloud and choose the total. The row bands are unscaled 100-unit controls; individual trees are pictures. Each seeded round selects two different orchards. Level 1 uses 2×3, 3×2 and 3×3 arrays with a running addition sentence. Level 2 uses 2×4, 3×4 and 2×5: after counting by rows, turn the same orchard to count by its columns and discover that its total stays the same. Level 3 uses 3×5, 4×5, 5×4 and 5×5: one row is missing; choose its tree count, plant it, then count the completed orchard both ways. Rows can be planted or counted in any order; replay never counts a group twice and is free support. Every wrong number choice is one miss, with an explanation; two misses on a question add one persistent answer glow and one hint. There is no further hint charge on that question. Two completed orchards finish through the shell for one sticker, including with help. A ghost-finger demonstration plays every mode through the same row and number controls. Co-play: count the equal groups together; off screen: arrange blocks in rows, then view them from the side. Device speech and child understanding of grouping remain unjudged.

### Picture Graph

*pre-K–school · after classroom picture graphs and tally charts.* Critters play in a meadow; the child builds a bar graph below by tapping above a bar to add a block (tapping the bar takes one away), then checks it. A wrong bar is a miss ("count the ducks again"); two make that kind's critters glow. Then questions about the graph she built: which has the most and the fewest (tap a bar), how many more of one than another (number pads), and a finished graph to read (how many in all; which two bars are the same). Counts never tie where "most" or "fewest" is asked (rule-tested). Two more levels add a **key**: one block stands for two critters, shown on a card ("block = two dots"), on every row of the graph's side scale (2, 4, 6...) and in the meadow, where the critters stand in pairs. On level 5 she builds the bars from the pairs and then answers how many more of one kind than another; on level 6 the keyed graph is already built and she answers how many critters in all and how many more. Answers count critters, so the number of blocks (the answer read straight off the bars) is always among the choices, and picking it says "that is how many blocks, each block is two critters" (rule-tested; counts are whole numbers of blocks and no two kinds tie, so the difference is never nothing). A bar with one block per critter is a miss ("each block is two ducks; count the pairs again"). **School level 7** is one complete graph cycle: counts from four to seven make the group of five tally marks visible. Tap above a column to add a mark, or on/below it to take one back; a wrong tally check gently says the count and glows that column after two misses. Once the tallies match, she uses the meadow to build the ordinary one-block-per-critter bar graph and answers how many more. This order gives the tally notation a job before she reads a graph built from the same data.

### Lasso Loops

*preschool–school · after Montessori golden beads, ten frames and Pokémon Ranger's capture loop.* Draw a loop around fireflies and they fly into a jar. The loop always closes back to its start, so a nearly closed loop counts; a loop around none is just explained. Levels: loop any fireflies; loop exactly 2 or 3 per jar; jars of five, then of ten, with the rest resting on a leaf, followed by "how many in all?" (the choices include the swapped tens and ones); and equal groups ("make 3 groups of 4", then how many). A wrong-sized loop lets the fireflies go and is a miss; two put white rings round a close group of the right size. A wrong total is counted aloud the way the jars show it (by fives, by tens and ones, by groups). School level 6 *About how many?* shows 21–39 fireflies: freely guess from three far-apart cards, loop one benchmark jar of ten, then keep or change the guess before finishing the tens-and-ones count. The pet compares the final guess with the count; neither guess counts as a miss or hint. Only wrong-sized loops and wrong total answers count, as at level 4. School spans levels 3–6; earlier bands and level IDs stay unchanged. Drawing uses its own pointer handling with palm rejection; a cancelled touch drops the loop.

### Number Trails

*preschool–pre-K · after Letter Trails and the numeral-formation charts of the common handwriting programmes.* Follow the firefly along a numeral from the green ring, in the direction of the arrow; a numeral of two parts (4, 5, 9) takes two touches. Wandering off the corridor is a gentle miss (boing, a spoken "come back to the firefly") and a glow after two on one stroke. When the numeral is done, that many things (ducks, apples or stars, one kind a round) pop up one at a time and are counted aloud, "that is 4 stars!". Level 1 draws 1 to 5, level 2 all of 0 to 9 (zero ends with "there are none", no things); at level 3 the things are asleep, three more than the numeral, and she taps them awake and counts; the round ends by itself at the numeral, so the rest stay asleep and nothing she taps is a miss. It shares Letter Trails' stroke tracer (`advanceTrace`, `sampleStroke`, `traceWaypoints`); the digit table is `digits.ts`. **The stroke forms are an interim convention (simple common forms, every stroke starting at the top), not yet checked against Handwriting Without Tears, D'Nealian or Zaner-Bloser's printed chart:** the roadmap keeps that decision open.

### Fill and Dump

*lap–preschool · after water-table and bath-toy play.* Levels 1–4 choose one seeded set per round: fruit in a basket, balls in a bucket, blocks in a toy box or bath ducks in a tub. Tip-out uses distinct designs (at least four available in each set); counting repeats one identical design. Speech names the current container and pieces, and each set has a different pentatonic landing pitch. Tap the big container and it tips over, the pieces tumbling out one by one with a chime each and landing on the grass; tap a piece (or the container again, which scoops the nearest one) and it hops back in. At levels 1–2, open grass also tips a full basket or scoops the outside piece nearest the touch. After six idle seconds while ready for input, the container or the piece nearest it wiggles; the invitation changes no misses or hints. Counting and pouring ignore ground taps and have no idle invitation. Taps only, since a one-year-old will not drag. Levels: 1 tip out three and scoop them back (nothing can go wrong); 2 four pieces, tipped out twice (the container is full again between, and the voice says so); 3 put in the number asked, one to three, from five identical pieces on the grass; 4 three to five from seven. At levels 3–4 a card left of the container shows one hollow dot per piece asked for (up to three a row); a dot fills in the pieces’ own color as each piece lands, and a piece beyond the number hangs below the card as an extra filled dot, so "too many" shows without sound or color (decoration only; the check and accounting are unchanged). Counting levels: each piece that goes in is counted aloud, a tap on the container takes the latest back out (a free undo), and the green check says "that is enough". A wrong check is the one miss (the check with nothing in yet is only the instruction again); every second miss glows what leads somewhere (a piece to add, the container to take one out of, or the check itself) and counts one hint. Landing places are seeded, in separate grid cells at least 128 units apart (tap circles 120 across), clear of the pet, and recomputed on resize. Toddler and preschool add level 6, *Full and empty*, placed after level 2 (toddler order 1, 2, 6, 3, 4; preschool 1, 2, 6, 3, 4, 5, 7; lap unchanged): two baskets stand apart, one with three fruit peeking over the rim, one empty, with a pile of three fruit on the grass between them. Four requests alternate between "Tip out the full one!" (it tips, the fruit tumbling out in front) and "Fill the empty one!" (the pile hops in), the first and the side of the full basket seeded, each request dealing a fresh pair. The wrong basket gets a boing, a head shake and "Nothing in there to tip!" or "No room, it is full!" and is one miss; the second miss on a request glows the right basket and counts one hint (once per request). Grass and pile taps are free. Preschool adds level 5: pull a jug right to pour into three cups, first to a halfway line, then quarter and three-quarter lines in seeded order. The furthest displacement in a gesture controls the amount (240 units per full cup); lifting stops, moving back rights the jug without pouring again. A check within six hundredths of a cup of the line accepts it. Overflow is caught in the tray; tapping the cup empties it into the tray, and tapping the tray returns all its water to the jug. Spills, emptying and recovery are free experiments. An empty check repeats the instruction; other incorrect checks count misses, with a persistent glow after two misses per cup and at most one hint per cup. Three checked cups finish with one sticker. Pointer cancellation, leaving the view, resizing and lost release stop the gesture. The ghost finger pours, recovers and checks through those same controls. Preschool top level 7, *Which holds more?*, sets out three distinct pairs: full bowl → small cup first, then cup → bucket and bucket → mug in seeded order. Straight-sided capacities differ by at least 1.6×; height is volume divided by width. Tap either container to make a free prediction, pull the left one right with the same 240-units-per-cup gesture to pour it all into the right, then tap the check. A partial check repeats the instruction without scoring. Overflow in the tray means the source holds more; room left means the receiver holds more. Tap the larger container to answer. Only a smaller-container answer after evidence counts a miss; after two, the spill or empty space glows (one hint per pair). Tap the tray to return all water and re-pour freely; during pouring, the right container also returns the water. Predictions, spills, partial pours and re-pours never score. All water stays in source, receiver or tray within each experiment; each next pair sets out its own full source. Three answered pairs finish with one sticker. The ghost finger guesses, pours, checks and answers through the same controls. Quick Tricks' third show remains a proposal. Co-play: tip it out together, count the pieces aloud; off-screen: a tub of soft toys or dry beans and a big bowl.

### Ribbon Workshop

*school · after the fractions study; [definition](definitions/ribbon-workshop.md).* A gift waits on the workbench and its tag asks for a ribbon of one length of a declared whole (the spool above the bench). She taps halves and quarters in the tray and each unrolls onto the bench end to end; tapping a bench piece sends it back to the tray, and undo takes back the last place, return or snip. When she thinks the length is right she taps the bow: the pieces join and tie around the gift. A round is two gifts and earns one sticker, with or without help. Every combination that makes the length is accepted (a rule test checks the accepted compositions against brute force), so one amount shows several names.

Levels (school only, 1–3): 1 a whole from halves and quarters (two halves, a half and two quarters, or four quarters); 2 the scissors appear and gift 1 asks for a half (including a tray of only quarters), while gift 2 asks for a quarter from two halves and cannot be made without a snip; 3 three quarters, whose second gift tempts two halves (a whole, too long) until she returns one, snips the other half and uses a quarter. The scissors snip a tray half into two quarters in place. The bench holds six quarter-ticks (one and a half wholes); a place past that is refused with a shake and "no more room".

Misses and hints: Check on an empty bench is free, and so is a refused overfull place. A non-empty bench that is the wrong length is one miss, and the gap (too short) or the overhang (too long) flashes. The second miss glows the solver-led next step (a place, a return or a snip) as one hint; the pause sheet's "Show a hint" counts one unless the glow is already up. The ghost finger follows the same hint path, and the demonstration plays one gift (the snip gift at levels 2–3). Touch only; no couch play.

## Cozy Village (everyday life)

### Splish Splash

*lap–pre-K.* Scrub mud off the pet; nine levels. Level 9, *Pat the bubbles*, is the lap entry and is placed first (`order` [9, 1, 2], `entryLevels` [9]): any touch anywhere is a splash that takes a third off the nearest of four mud patches (tummy, head, two cheeks; at most 12 pats), with no misses or hints, and a patch sparkles after six idle seconds (an invitation, not a hint). The rest grow into named body parts in a shuffled order, two at once, then "first … then …". Washing the wrong part just makes the pet giggle and repeats the request; a finger that is still within reach of a patch it scrubbed in the same touch is not told off for brushing a neighbor on the way (the ears reach into the head's patch), but sliding on to another part still is. Levels 1–2 scrub with a palm-sized brush (1.6 times the usual reach, also used by the bot). Free levels 1–2 and the pat level name each part at its first touch ("My cheeks!", once per round, at least 2.5 s apart, never over the opening instruction) and as it goes clean ("Clean tummy!", except the last part, which has the finale's "Squeaky clean!"); parts levels 3–8 say "Clean ears!" before the next question. Wrong touches on a pair say "Those are my cheeks!". On levels 1–2, after 8 s with no touch the next muddy splotch sparkles and the pet wiggles (never a hint). Lines take whole phrases from a word-and-plural table, so a pair reads "Wash my ears and my nose!".

### Feelings Faces

*lap–school.* The critter gained `sad` and `calm` moods, with brows and a tear so feelings read even at sticker size. Lap play is cause and effect: four feeling bubbles change the big pet's face, with a matching sound, and tapping a sad pet gives it a hug. Level 9, *Poke a feeling*, is the lap entry (lap order 9, 1, 2): no bubbles, the pet starts calm, and each tap anywhere on it shows the next step of a fixed cycle (happy, surprised, sleepy, sad, then a hug that returns it to happy) with the feeling's sound, word and effect; the hug is hearts and its own sound. It ends by itself after six taps with a sticker, and `misses` and `hints` are always 0. Later levels match the pet's face, find a named feeling, choose what helps a need (a hug, a pillow, an apple, or a scarf), watch a small event and choose how the pet feels (the pet stays `calm` until answered, so the event carries the answer), and find which of four friends feels a named feeling. Level 6 draws four different happenings from eight (a balloon drifts away, the moon rises, a cloud rains on the window, a butterfly lands on the pet's head, a lullaby's bell notes drift up, a ball bounces in, and more); two happenings may share a feeling. Level 7 draws its friends from eight. Level 8 joins the event to a helpful choice from five scenarios (balloon drifts away or ice cream falls off its cone, then a hug; moon rises, then a pillow; snow at the window, then a scarf; an empty picnic basket, then an apple); a round shows four different happenings and at least three different helpers, and the care-only happenings never appear at level 6. The hug is said as the pet's own preference ("{pet} loves a hug!"), not a rule. A tap on a happening's prop wobbles it and replays its sound, free and never a miss. Tapping the pet repeats the question (at level 9 it shows the next feeling instead). The big pet replaces the corner guide and repeats the instruction when tapped.

Level 10, *Hug or pillow*, places two helper cards below a sad, sleepy or hungry pet; toddler order is `[2, 10, 3, 4]`. ### Lemonade Stand

*school · after MECC's Lemonade Stand and the Coolmath version.* A short market week. Each day shows a forecast (a sun, clouds or rain, and from level 2 an event: a ferry bringing visitors or a quiet day), the child taps a batch of 4, 8 or 12 cups (and from level 3 a price of 1, 2 or 3 shells), then taps OPEN. Friends come one at a time: each who finds a cup buys it, the rest watch the cups run out under a SOLD OUT sign, and spare cups go to two friends on a picnic blanket. A row (weather, price, cups made, sold and left, shells) joins a picture table; tapping a row reads it aloud. After the last day the pet points out one comparison the table can show (the same weather at two prices, two weathers at the same price, or the day that kept the most).

The market is a plain function of the day (`logic.ts`): the lowest crowd for rain, clouds and sun is 2, 6 and 10 friends (plus a fixed 0 to 2 per day from the seed), a ferry adds 4 and a quiet day takes 4 away, and each shell of price loses friends (price 2 loses 2, price 3 loses 6; at least one friend always comes). Sold is the smaller of cups made and friends who came. The three weathers never overlap at one price, so the nearest batch above a day's crowd always leaves at most two cups. A week is rebuilt exactly from its seed, always has two or more weathers, and on level 1 is exactly two weathers with one of them twice, so there is always a day to compare. A rainy quiet day is never made.

Levels: **1** three days, weather only; **2** three days with an event; **3** four days with a price (a middle price earns most on a plain sunny day, the lowest in the rain, the highest on a very busy ferry day, so no price is a dead button); **4** a five-day market week where a lemon costs one shell and makes two cups (a batch of 4, 8 or 12 costs 2, 4 or 6 shells), the purse starts at 6 and carries from day to day, a batch the purse cannot pay for is dimmed (tapping it says what it costs), spare cups still cost their lemons (a small, causal loss), and if the purse falls below the smallest batch's cost the ferry captain tops it up to exactly that, so there is no debt and no failed week. A best choice that does not lose shells exists on every day of every week (tested).

Mistakes and help: nothing is a miss, so `misses` is always 0. Running out, spare cups and a weak first batch are results the table and the pet explain ("All sold out! 5 more friends wanted a cup", "4 cups are left. They go to a picnic with friends!", "The spare cups go to a picnic, but they cost shells to make"). OPEN before choosing only nudges the cards or prices. The lightbulb asks the pet for one true thing about today, most useful first: what happened the last time it was the same weather (ran out, a smaller batch would have sold as well, or nearly everything sold), how another weather compared (levels without a price), what the event does, how the forecast works, and what a higher price does. The rows the message is about are circled; asking again steps to the next. Every message is checked against the model (`helps()`), and on day 1, with no row to point at, it explains the forecast. Asking for help counts as one hint for the day however often she asks, so a round where help was asked on two or more days counts as struggling and two of those step the level down, while two rounds with no help step it up.

Not built: a saved week or market-week chart in the journal, recipe quality and advertising, a "close the stand" button (the home button always works), the Maker Harbor tickets and fair board that this game is the first slice of, and a couch version.

### Sudoku Garden

*grown-up, on the island by touch and on the couch · after sudoku, and Garden Rows grown up.* Fill a bed so every row, column and box holds each number once: 6 by 6 beds (boxes two rows by three columns) and 9 by 9 beds. A square holds a numbered blossom whose color is its number's, so a bed reads at a glance as well as by its digits; numbers given at the start are inked, numbers placed are blue, a number that repeats in its row, column or box turns red with its twin and a soft boing (spoken once a bed). A wrong number is never refused. **By touch (the island):** tap a square to choose it (a given number can be chosen too, to look along its row, column and box), then tap a number in the tray; **Marks** pencils the number instead, **Erase** takes a number or its marks out, **Hint** and **Start over** are buttons. Every square and tile is at least 64 logical units: the bed sits beside the tray, or under it on a tall view. A wrong number counts as a miss and a hint counts as help (the rule couch play already had), so the island adapts on both; the how-to card's demonstration plays a bed with ten squares left. **Controls on the couch:** the stick or D-pad moves the cursor (hold to repeat); the bottom button on a square opens the number tray at the right of the bed (a tile per number with how many are left to place, and a pencil tile); in the tray the bottom button places the highlighted number or, on the pencil, switches marks on; the left button closes the tray, or takes a number (or its marks) back out of a square.

**Score and help.** Every number placed counts one entry (a replaced number costs another); taking one out and pencil marks are free; placing the number that is already there changes nothing. The fewest is the bed's empty squares, so a perfect bed is a bed with no mistaken or replaced number, and the score is the entries over par. The pause menu has **Show a hint** (marks a challenge run as helped for good) and **Start this bed again** (entries already made keep counting). A hint points at a number she placed that cannot be right first (a clashing one if there is one); otherwise at the next number the techniques below can place from where the bed stands, with its reason spoken and written above the bed: *only the 4 fits there* (a lone number), *the 4 has only one square left in this box* (an only place), or, on the hardest beds, the pair or pointing line that rules numbers out first, with its squares marked. A square left with no number that fits turns orange and the game says one of her numbers has to come out.

**The rules** are in `logic.ts` and carry the tests. `search` counts solutions (a bed must have exactly one) and grows random full beds. `techniqueSolve` works a bed the way a person does, easiest step first: **lone** (only one number fits a square), **place** (a number has one square left in a row, column or box), **pair** (two squares in a house can only hold the same two numbers) and **locked** (a number's squares in a house all lie along one line, or one box). A bed's *grade* is the hardest technique it needs. `makePuzzle` takes numbers out of a random full bed in random order, keeping each removal only if the bed stays unique and workable, and throws a bed away unless it has exactly the plan's blanks and grade. Six levels: 6 by 6 with 14, then 21 empty squares (lone numbers only), 6 by 6 with 24 that needs an only place; 9 by 9 with 42 (lone), 52 (only places) and 55 (a pair or pointing line). A 6 by 6 bed almost never needs a pair, so it has no third rung. Rule tests assert every plan makes exactly its blanks and grade over many seeds, one solution, every technique step sound against the solution (a placement is the solution's number; nothing ruled out is in it), and a follow-the-hints player always finishes, with and without a wrong number to start.

**By touch (the island, Lantern Lake).** Touch the pond and lift to set the line off (nothing moves before then); swipe the way the line should turn, and one stroke can turn it twice (turns wait in line and are made one a step; a swipe straight back is ignored). **Hint** (dots along the way to the next crumb, kept up until it is eaten) and **Start over** (the line goes back, steps keep counting) are buttons of at least 64 units under the pond; the pond itself is the target, so its cells may be smaller, and it keeps clear of the home and help buttons. The accounting is the couch's: a bump is a miss, a hint is help. The how-to demonstration paddles at one step a second over the first four crumbs.

**Couch.** A trip stop (face-off `twin`: each plays their own seeded bed, scored in entries over the empty squares) plays the three 6 by 6 levels. Two challenge courses of frozen beds are on the puzzle shelf with par: **Six Beds** (six 6 by 6 beds, par 124 entries) and **Three Big Beds** (three 9 by 9 beds, one of each grade, par 149); `course.ts` holds them with a `version`, and tests assert each is unique, solvable by the techniques and graded as stated. ### Lantern Lights

*grown-up couch play · after Lights Out, on a pond at dusk.* A square of paper lanterns, each lit or dark. Pressing one flips it and the lanterns above, below and beside it; the goal is every lantern lit. The cursor shows a faint glow over what a press would flip. **Controls:** the stick or D-pad moves (hold to repeat); the bottom button presses; the left button takes the last press back; the pause menu has **Show a hint** and **Start this pond again**.

**Score and help.** Every press counts, and a press taken back stays counted (taking it back is free); the fewest is the pond's par, so the score is presses over par. A hint (marks a challenge run as helped) points at a lantern in one of the fewest-press ways to light the pond from where it stands now, and the cursor goes to it; pressing it always brings the fewest down by exactly one, so a hint never leads away from the finish. A restart puts the pond back as it began, with the presses so far still counted. There are no misses to count: any press is allowed, and nothing is ever stuck, because every pond can always be lit.

**The rules** are in `logic.ts`. Pressing is its own undo and the order never matters, so a way to win is just which lanterns get pressed an odd number of times, and whether a set wins is a system of linear equations over two numbers. `solve` finds every winning set by Gaussian elimination over two numbers and picks the fewest, so a pond's par is worked out, never guessed (a 5 by 5 pond has four winning sets, a 4 by 4 sixteen, a 3 by 3 or 6 by 6 one). `makePond` lights every lantern and then presses a plan's number of different lanterns at random, so every pond can be lit, and drops one that turns out easier than the plan's `atLeast`. Six levels: 3 by 3 with two, then four presses to find; 4 by 4 with four, then seven; 5 by 5 with five, then ten. Rule tests assert the fewest against a brute force over every 3 by 3 and 4 by 4 board and over 4000 random 5 by 5 boards (including the quarter that cannot be lit, which `solve` reports as none), every winning set lights the board, each level's par lies inside its plan, hints bring the fewest down by exactly one on every level, and the same seed gives the same ponds.

**Couch.** A trip stop (face-off `twin`: each plays their own seeded pond, scored in presses over the fewest) climbs the six levels. One puzzle-shelf course of frozen ponds, **Dusk on the Pond**: a 4 by 4 pond and four 5 by 5 ponds with fewest presses 4, 5, 7, 9 and 11 (par 36); `course.ts` holds them with a `version`, and tests assert each can be lit, none starts lit, the par is the solver's, and the ponds climb. It opens in the last tier of trips and is on the shelf from the start. Not built: a 6 by 6 level, a replay of the best way, lanterns with more than two states.

**Island touch (2026-10-13).** In Lantern Lake the game plays by touch: tap a lantern to press it (a layer over the pond works out which one, so every cell is at least about 100 units), and three buttons under the pond take the last press back, show a hint (the lantern to try rings) and start the pond over. There is no cursor on touch. Misses are always 0 (a press is never wrong, and taking one back is free), each hint counts, and the round gives one island sticker. The ghost finger taps the lanterns of a fewest-press way. The buttons and tap layer are absent in couch play, which still drives the same board with `control()`. ### Pond Conga

*grown-up couch play · after Snake and Neopets' Meerca Chase, made gentle.* A line of ducklings paddles across a pond at a steady, easy pace, and the stick or D-pad turns the leader to each crumb of bread in turn. Eating a crumb adds a duckling; only the next crumb (and a faint look at the one after, so a route can be planned) is shown. **Controls:** the stick or D-pad turns (a turn is made at the next step, two can wait in line, a turn straight back is ignored); the bottom button, or any turn, sets off, because nothing moves before somebody is ready. A **bump** into a lily pad, the bank or the line itself is a **bonk**: nothing is lost, the whole conga turns about (the old tail leads), there is a breather of a second to choose, and the bonk counts as one more step. Because it turns about, the line can never box itself in, and there is no game over.

**Score and help.** Every step of the leader counts, and so does every bonk, so the score is steps over the fewest. The fewest is exact (no route can be shorter than the lily-pad-aware distances between the crumbs added up, and a pond is used only if a route with no bonk at all gets there), so a perfect run is a real thing to reach, and a bonk or a wasted turn each cost steps. The pause menu has **Show a hint** (yellow dots along the next few steps to the crumb, kept up as the line moves; marks a challenge run as helped) and **Start this pond again** (the line goes back to the start, steps so far keep counting). The pace is constant (2.4 to 3.4 steps a second, 3 on the course) so what makes a pond harder is the number of crumbs and lily pads, never speed.

**The rules** are in `logic.ts`: `step` (the whole rule of play), `distances` and `lowerBound`, `legRoutes`/`solveRoute` (bonk-free fewest-step routes with the line's own body simulated), `hintPath`, `parsePond`/`pictureOf`, and `makePond`, which places lily pads (the open water stays one piece, with a clear runway in front of the start), then each crumb at a distance from the one before within the level's range, keeping a pond only if a route as short as the crumbs allow exists. Six levels: 11 by 7 with five crumbs and no pads at 2.4 steps a second, then 13 by 9 with 4, then 8 pads, then 15 by 9 with 12, 16 and 20 pads (ten, twelve and twelve crumbs). Rule tests assert exactly that for every level on a dozen seeds each (par equals the lower bound and a bonk-free route reaches it), that a hint always leads on from wherever the line has wandered, and that a bonk keeps every duckling.

**Couch.** A trip stop (face-off `twin`: each plays their own seeded pond, scored in steps over the fewest) plays one pond at levels 1 to 5. One puzzle-shelf course of frozen ponds, **Crumb Trail** (four ponds of 6, 8, 10 and 12 crumbs among 3, 8, 13 and 17 lily pads; par 34 + 50 + 77 + 98 = 259), with **Watch the best routes** replaying each pond's fewest-steps way; `course.ts` holds them with a `version`, and tests assert each parses to a route as short as its crumbs allow. It opens in the last tier of trips and is on the shelf from the start. Not built: a timer (none is wanted), a bonk variant that costs nothing, two lines on one pond for a face-off, a wrapping pond.

### Island Bridges

*grown-up, on the island by touch and on the couch · after Hashiwokakero (Bridges).* Numbered islands sit in a sea. Join them with plank bridges, one or two between the same pair, running straight along a row or column to the next island, so that every island ends with as many planks as its number, no bridge crosses another, and all the islands are one group. **By touch (the island):** the water between two islands that face each other shows faint dots; tap it to lay a plank, tap again for a second (a third, a crossing or an island with no room bumps and costs nothing). **Take off** is a switch: while it is on, a tap on a bridge lifts one plank (free); a hint to take a plank off turns it on for her, and any other hint turns it off. **Hint** and **Start over** are buttons. Every stretch of water is a cell at least 64 logical units across and every button at least 64: the sea sits beside its buttons, or above them, bigger, on a tall view. The accounting is the couch's (a plank beyond the answer is a miss, a hint counts as help). The how-to card's demonstration plays a sea with its last six planks left. **Controls on the couch:** the stick or D-pad moves the highlight to the nearest island in the direction pushed; the bottom button *arms* the island to lay planks, and then a push toward a neighbor lays one (a second push lays another, a third bumps); the left button arms it to take planks off instead (free); pressing the same button again lets go. A faint line shows each neighbor the island can reach. An island turns green when it has its planks, and its outline turns red when its number can no longer be reached, whichever bridges were laid (judged from the numbers and the room left, never from the answer).

**Score and help.** Every plank laid is an entry, and taking one off is free, so a wrong plank costs the one entry it took to lay it; a bridge that would cross another, a third plank, or an island with no room bumps and costs nothing. The fewest is the planks in the answer, so the score is planks over par (a plank that is not in the answer counts as a miss for adaptation and is never refused). The pause menu has **Show a hint** (marks a challenge run as helped) and **Clear this sea** (planks already laid keep counting). A hint names a bridge that has more planks than the answer first; otherwise an edge one island decides by itself (its number equals the room it has left, so each open bridge must carry a plank); otherwise any plank the answer needs, with the two islands ringed and the bridge lit.

**The rules** are in `logic.ts`. `layoutOf` finds the pairs of islands that face each other and which of those bridges would cross; `solve` settles each island's number against the room it has (a bridge that must carry planks forces what crosses it to none) and searches what is left, and a finished try must also join every island. `makePuzzle` grows a layout and its planks together (each new island joined to one already there, then extra bridges where two face each other across clear water), numbers each island with the planks it got, and keeps the puzzle only if `solve` finds exactly one answer; the two gentler levels also need `bycounting` (propagation alone finishes it, which is how an easy puzzle is told from one that needs guessing). Four levels: 7 by 7 with seven to nine islands, 9 by 9 with eleven to fourteen, a busier 9 by 9 with fifteen to eighteen, and 11 by 11 with nineteen to twenty-four. Rule tests assert that the solver agrees with a brute force over every plank assignment on thousands of random small layouts, that a two-answer layout is counted as two and refused as a puzzle, that every made puzzle has exactly one answer with numbers one to eight, that the highlight reaches every island, and that a player who follows every hint from an empty sea lays exactly the answer's planks.

**Couch.** A trip stop (face-off `twin`: each plays their own seeded sea, scored in planks over the answer) plays levels 1 to 3. One puzzle-shelf course of frozen seas, **Island Hopping** (five seas of 8, 13, 18, 20 and 22 islands; par 11 + 17 + 27 + 27 + 38 = 120); `course.ts` holds them with a `version`, and tests assert each has exactly one answer, every number one to eight, and that they climb. It opens in the last tier of trips and is on the shelf from the start. Not built: a replay of the best way, a 13 by 13 size, hints that teach a named technique.


## Shape Buddies

**Fun action:** reshape two paper friends, try their construction, then carry a caught seed to a pot. **Learning:** shape and function, spatial planning, testing and revision. Pre-K and school both play levels 1–3; subject/home land Tinker Lab.

Tap a friend, choose Flat, Cup or Slope, then drag it to the bridge or catching dock. At level 1 flat and sloped bridges both work, and either friend can take either role. Try releases the seed along a fixed chute. A bridge carries it across the gap; a scoop at the receiver catches it and waits. Tap the pot to carry and tip the seed into it, grow a sprout and finish through the shell for one sticker.

A failed trial drops the seed onto a cushion and names the first unsupported role. Experiments, edits and occupied-dock drops are free: misses always stay zero. After two unsuccessful trials for the same role, or explicit Help, useful friend/tool/dock outlines glow; each role counts at most one hint across the round. Undo restores the last construction edit, including selection. Replay resets a caught seed without changing the construction. Undo and other edits also return a caught seed to the chute. A friend can be parked in its own spot to free its dock; occupied docks never evict the other friend. Input is locked during the run and carry.

**Level 2, A step down:** the far bank is one step lower (the left bank and chute, and the bridge dock, are raised 50 units). Only a Slope bridge reaches it: a Flat bridge stays up high and the seed drops into the stream (`buddies.stepFlat`); an empty, plain or scoop bridge says `buddies.stepBridge`. The cup role is unchanged. Misses stay 0, each role still counts at most one hint, and guidance suggests Slope. Spoken start is `buddies.start2`.

**Level 3, Two make one:** the gap is wide, so neither friend reaches alone. A flat friend goes to the near half (the bridge dock) and a cup friend to a 180-wide far half (the receiver); a dashed joining outline shows both places. When both halves are filled the friends hold hands, a bell clasps and they form a spoon. Try releases the seed; a failed trial names the first thing wrong through the pure `failureLine(s, level)` (level 3 uses `buddies.joinPath`, `joinSlope`, `joinShort` and `joinCup`), and the lines `buddies.start3` and `buddies.together` open and close the round. Both friends carry the spoon to the pot. Misses stay 0 and hint policy is unchanged; levels 1–2 keep their old failure lines. Planting one seed finishes the round. Co-play and off-screen suggestions got a level-3 sentence.

Rules use a finite inspectable trace, independent of collision/frame timing. The ghost finger uses selection, tools, drags, Try and the pot through the same handlers as touch play. Colors are seeded; geometry and solvability are fixed. Co-play asks which shape can hold the seed; off screen, slide a large soft toy across cardboard into a bowl. At level 2, ask why the flat one did not work this time; off screen, add a step-down ramp. No controller or second-seat mode. Future arrangements remain proposals.


## Sing-Along Barn

**Fun action:** tap anywhere and the song moves one note on; a friend comes out of the barn for each verse and answers. **Learning:** song order and animal words; making choices. Lap, toddler, preschool and pre-K; home land Barnyard (moved from Music Mountain in RW13 slice 1). No wrong answers: `misses` and `hints` are always 0, and a song she plays along to is not offered to the tune plaque.

Each round sings one of **six songs**, drawn from the round's seed (`pickTune` in `logic.ts`; a song is a `Tune`: opening, verse, answer phrase). From level 5 she chooses the song herself (see Levels); below that the seed draws it. **Old MacDonald** is sung in two forms. Levels 1–5 sing the **short Old MacDonald**: the first two lines once, then "And on that farm he had a …, E-I-E-I-O" for each friend, with the moo-moo lines and the repeated first line left out; a grown-up singing along should sing that short form, as the how-to card and co-play tip say. Level 6 (pre-K) sings the **whole verse**: after "E-I-E-I-O" comes "With a moo-moo here and a moo-moo there, here a moo, there a moo, everywhere a moo-moo" (the friend sounds on every moo; the syllable `~` is its own sound word), then "Old MacDonald had a farm, E-I-E-I-O" again (`SingPlan.full`, `OLD_MACDONALD_FULL`, `tunesFor` in `logic.ts`). The moo-moo melody (G G C C C / G G C / C C C G A G) was written from memory of the tune's shape, not a printed score (open in [DEVICE-CHECKS](DEVICE-CHECKS.md)). The tune is "Old MacDonald Had a Farm" in C (C D E G A, with a pickup on the G below), so it needs no exception to the pentatonic rule; each note is one sung syllable. A row of beads shows the line being sung (one bead per note; sung ones fill, the next breathes). The opening is "Old MacDonald had a farm, E-I-E-I-O"; each verse is "And on that farm he had a …" (the friend answers in its own voice on the last note) and "E-I-E-I-O", after which the friend settles on the bottom row and the next one comes out. The finale is the last "O": everyone answers once, confetti, and the round ends with one sticker.
