# Astronomy: stars, galaxies, telescopes and deeper sky models

**Subject:** `astronomy` · **Audiences:** L, T, P, K, S, U, M and G as [defined in the tracker](../subjects.md#the-audiences) · **Date:** 2026-10-17 · **Session:** `claude/astronomy-subject-research`, [PR #512](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/512). Requested by the developer 2026-10-16 as a follow-up to the [Space study](space.md); this file replaces that day's research brief.

**Research and proposals only.** Recommend one new identity, **Far Lights** (how bright a light looks depends on how far away it is), with a first ladder for pre-K through upper school. Everything else extends what Space already proposed: **Moon Window** gains two appended shadow levels (8–9) for eclipses; **Observatory Files** gains three case types that use its one control (seasons, a star cluster's age and a two-view telescope case); a night scene goes to **Photo Safari**; galaxy shapes go to **Critter Sort**'s rule-guessing level; galaxy and universe scale go to **Zoom Walk**. No game, band, zone, land registration or save format is added. Nothing here comes ahead of Space's first packet (Moon Window 1–3).

Science checks opened the same day:

- [NASA: Stars](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/stars/) says stars form in molecular clouds, and that a star's "mass determines how rapidly it runs through its supply". Low-mass stars end as white dwarfs after a planetary nebula. High-mass stars end in a supernova, leaving "either a neutron star or a black hole".
- [OpenStax Astronomy 2e, Star Clusters](https://phys.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Astronomy__Cosmology/Astronomy_2e_(OpenStax)/22%3A_Stars_from_Adolescence_to_Old_Age/22.03%3A_Star_Clusters) says a cluster's stars "all formed at nearly the same time, from the same cloud"; "since stars with higher masses evolve more quickly", "we can see many stages of stellar evolution among the members of a single cluster". This textbook page, not a NASA page, supports the cluster case below.
- [NASA: Eclipse types](https://science.nasa.gov/eclipses/types/): eclipses happen "only occasionally, because the Moon doesn't orbit in the exact same plane"; "lunar eclipses occur at the full moon phase"; a solar eclipse needs the Moon between Sun and Earth. Its viewing-safety guidance (eclipse glasses, pinhole projection) is noted so that **nothing here drafts viewing advice**.
- [NASA Space Place: What is a galaxy?](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/galaxy/en/) gives spiral, elliptical and irregular shapes, the Milky Way as our galaxy, and telescope deep fields as the evidence for many galaxies.
- [Chandra: 3-Color Composite teacher guide](https://chandra.si.edu/edu/formal/3color/3color_teachGuide.html) says X-ray images need "representative or 'false' colors" and that false color "is an arbitrary selection of colors to represent some characteristic". [NASA: How are Webb's full-color images made?](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/science-overview/science-explainers/how-are-webbs-full-color-images-made) says separate filter images are assigned colors in wavelength order ("the shortest wavelengths are assigned blue"), sometimes reassigned "to support the science".

**Recalled, not opened:** the inverse-square relation (a light's apparent brightness falls with the square of distance) and stellar parallax (a nearby star's apparent shift as Earth moves around its orbit, useful only for comparatively nearby stars). Both are standard physics, but no page opened here states them. Far Lights uses the first and an optional toy parallax. Both are **reviewer items** before anything is spoken as fact.

## What people can do, and what remains uncertain

- **L/T:** Space's [NAEYC finding](space.md#what-people-can-do-and-what-remains-uncertain) carries over: making things happen, noticing and sharing. Nothing here supports a claim that a toddler learns anything about stars. Youngest play is cause and effect with a light, and that is all it claims.
- **P/K:** no preschool study of brightness and distance was opened. "Nearer looks bigger and brighter" is a familiar everyday comparison (a torch walking toward you), offered as a design hypothesis.
- **S (6–8):** [Plummer's interview study](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/a-cross-age-study-of-childrens-knowledge-of-apparent-celestial-mo/) (abstract opened) covered 60 first, third and eighth graders using a small sky dome. They "hold a variety of non-scientific ideas about all aspects of apparent celestial motion", and accuracy showed "little change from third grade to eighth grade". A search summary added that over half did not think different stars are seen through a night. **Not opened**; treat it as a lead. Young school children need replayable observation before causal models.
- **U (9–12), seasons:** [Starakis and Halkia (Physics Education 49, 2014)](https://indico.unina.it/event/73/contributions/1042/attachments/359/644/2014_-_Addressing_k-5_students_and_pre-service_elementary_teachers_conceptions_of_seasonal.pdf), full text opened, asked 142 Athens fifth graders and 142 pre-service elementary teachers why summer is hotter. Both groups mainly used two schemes:
  - **Sun–Earth distance.** Their review calls it the most common notion.
  - **Orientation.** About 23% of the fifth graders said the side "orientated towards the Sun" has summer, which confuses seasons with day and night.

  Even among the teachers, only one came close to the scientific explanation. They report Baxter's and Trumper's 15–16-year-olds near 50% for tilt and Galili and Lavrik near 20%. These percentages are cited **as reported by Starakis and Halkia**; the primary studies were not opened. **Design consequence:** a seasons model must show, for one site, the Sun's height, how spread out its light is and how long the day is. It must also include an opposite-hemisphere record that defeats the distance idea. "The side facing the Sun has summer" must never be drawn or spoken.
- **U/M, stars:** [Agan (Astronomy Education Review 3, 2004)](https://istardb.org/wp-content/uploads/tainacan-items/1018/19915/Agan_2004_Stellar-Ideas-1.pdf), full text opened, interviewed 17 high-school and first-year university students, an exploratory sample by the author's own account. Students commonly held that "a star's distance relates to its apparent brightness" (assuming every star has the same own brightness) and that the Sun is not a star. Students without astronomy instruction focused on "secondary characteristics of stars, such as size and color". One student reasoned that a farther star could look as bright as a nearer one. **Design consequence:** the ambiguity between a nearer dim light and a farther strong one is the deep rung, and it is exactly what 5-ESS1-1 leaves out of the school band. [Bitzenbauer and colleagues' cross-age study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13728) (abstract opened, 366 secondary students) reports views growing more scientific through secondary school; its item-level findings were not opened. Sadler's and Lightman and Sadler's brightness and H-R results appeared only in a search snippet. **Not opened; not used.**
- **M/G:** Space's tween evidence still holds: no study here shows that 11–14-year-olds or parents enjoy these exact puzzles. [ESA CESAR's galaxy case for ages 12–15](https://cesar.esa.int/upload/202008/galaxies_sse_editable.pdf) (opened) shows the classroom pattern: invent your own classification of galaxies, then compare it with Hubble's scheme, then use colour as a clue to the stars inside. It also gives each team member a role (telescope operator, astronomer and so on). It is a lesson for 12–15-year-olds, not evidence of what they enjoy.

## What fits

Astronomy's fun, here, is **moving a light, a viewpoint or a time dial and watching what you would see change**, then using a second observation to decide what the first one could not. That fits the island's rules. Star names, planet facts, constellation trivia and dense planetarium interfaces do not earn a game.

**The Observatory Files test.** A case belongs in Observatory Files only if the player sets a time, viewpoint or position until the model's records agree with the observed ones, then picks an observation that tells the remaining explanations apart. Three astronomy cases pass:

- seasons: orbit position and site;
- a star cluster's age;
- a two-view telescope case, with the instrument choice as the distinguishing observation.

Galaxy sorting fails and goes to Critter Sort. Ordering one star's stages fails and goes to Life Story Lab.

**Model honesty, everywhere.** Every model on screen states its compressions:

- toy distances and glows;
- an exaggerated orbit tilt, labelled;
- a dial that compresses billions of years, labelled as a model;
- colours assigned by a legend.

A compressed animation is never presented as something anyone watched; astronomers infer a star's life from many stars. No live sky service, location, camera, real dates, waiting for night, or kept observation records. Real constellations and cultural sky stories need appropriate sources and review; invented patterns are labelled invented.

## A first game's ladder: Far Lights

### The model, stated once

A dark lake seen from the side (top) and the same lights as seen from a window (bottom); in landscape they sit side by side. Each light sits on one of four stops at toy distances 1, 2, 3 and 4. Each has a glow (its own brightness) of 1, 4 or 9. **How bright it looks** is `glow / distance²`. This is the inverse-square relation, recalled, not opened, and needing review. On the window view, how bright a light looks is drawn as the size and strength of its halo on a fixed small core. **Halo size shows brightness, never the light's size**; the how-to and the pet say so ("it looks brighter, not bigger"). Halos come in readable steps: two lights count as different only if one looks at least twice as bright, and as the same only if they are exactly equal. Real stars also differ in colour, size and age, which this toy leaves out; the how-to says it is a toy.

Lights are friendly, original code-drawn floating lights; the night is a quiet drawn scene; sounds are pentatonic. Nothing depends on the device's clock.

**Accounting and finish:** a finite authored round at each level (three taps, two lights, two pairs, one board for levels 4–8). Moves, previews and replays are free; only an explicit check or choice can miss. After two failed checks on the same task, show one actionable glow and count one hint; further misses on that task do not charge the glow again. Supported completion is always available and the shell's sticker always follows. Levels 1–2 are exploration: like Space's decision 5, their 0-miss rounds are not evidence of mastery, and the level-change policy for them must be set before registration.

**Co-play:** "arrange and guess" (see G play-with) uses the same board with the curtain; one device, taking turns; solo, the pet arranges.

**Rework fit (proposals only):** a Discovery Club project, *"How can everyone see the lantern-boat festival?"*, could use Far Lights 4 and 7, with the festival appearing brighter in the world when finished. A journal entry could file "a strong light far away can look like a small light nearby" only when that round showed it (`RoundResult.discoveries` files what a round showed). No new save field, currency or creature is assumed; any fictional night creature is labelled fiction.

## Experience and presentation

- **First unsupported action:** one large light (≥ 100 logical units including its touch area) that brightens on touch-down before any speech. Drags use `src/engine/drag.ts`, snapping to the four stops; taps use `onTap`. The instruction comes through `ctx.instruct` and observations through `ctx.say`; nothing needs reading.
- **Layout:** side view above and window view below in portrait, side by side in landscape. Stops at least 110 units apart, the bottom-left pet clear, the curtain a large pictured button, and the sideways step button large and labelled with a picture. If four stops do not fit at 100 units in portrait, use three stops at that level rather than shrinking them.
- **Replay:** seeded layouts vary which side holds which light, which stop is empty and which glow pairing appears. The model's laws never vary.
- **Ghost finger:** feasible: the bot taps, drags to a stop, presses the curtain/check and the step button, through the same public actions a person uses.
- **Off-screen bridge:** the page card above, reviewed before any practical step.

## Deeper models: what each extension must state

- **Moon Window 8–9 (eclipses).** Level 8, *Shadow or phase?*, gives two records: a crescent and a darkened, reddish full Moon. The player chooses the orbit-view arrangement that made each. A phase comes from the Moon's angle; a lunar eclipse needs Earth exactly between Sun and Moon at full.
  - Level 9, *Why not every month?*, adds an **edge-on view**. The Moon's path is tilted and the tilt is **exaggerated and labelled**. The player chooses which full-Moon positions around the year line up with Earth's shadow.
  - A solar eclipse appears only as a model arrangement: no sky scene, no viewing advice.
  - Rule tests list every arrangement that matches each record and check that a hint reaches one.
  - Space's levels 1–7, which draw no Earth-shadow wedge, are unchanged; the shadow appears only from level 8.
- **Season file.** One site and its opposite-hemisphere partner. The Earth's axis keeps a fixed direction, tilted and labelled. Controls are four orbit positions and the site's hemisphere. Each record shows the Sun's height at midday, how spread out a patch of sunlight is, and day length as relative bars, never clock arithmetic.
  - Sun–Earth distance is shown, and the case is built so that distance cannot explain both records together.
  - No drawing or line says "the side facing the Sun has summer", which is the orientation misreading found above.
  - Rule tests check that each record pair has exactly the stated surviving arrangements.
- **Cluster file.** An invented cluster of stars of three masses, all formed together. A model age dial is labelled "millions of years squeezed into a slide". Bigger stars change sooner: a table of invented, ordered stage durations stands in for real values and needs review.
  - The snapshot shows which stars are still steady, which have swollen and which have finished. The player sets the age so that the model agrees, and if two ages survive, picks the star whose look separates them.
  - End states use NASA's terms only after review.
- **Two-view file.** A code-drawn cloud hides young stars in the visible view. The infrared view shows them in **assigned colours with a legend always on screen**, stating that the colours were chosen in wavelength order.
  - The player chooses which view can answer "are there stars inside?". Both views stay available.
  - No real images and no "this is what it looks like" claim.
