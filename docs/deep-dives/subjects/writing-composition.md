# Writing and composition: write for a reader who was not there, and fix it when they ask

*Subject `writing-composition` · 2026-10-22 · `claude/roadmap-research-ideation-sk8axh` · [PR #824](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/824). Research and proposals only, for L/T/P/K/S/U/M and grown-ups. Base: `f336f7b7`. No game was played or implemented.*

**Recommendation:** the island has no game in which she is the *author* of something a reader needs. Story Steps asks her to order, repair and predict stories someone else wrote, and Story Theater lets her choose a cast, a weather and a prop and watch a show; nothing asks her to put a story into words for a reader who was not there, and nothing lets a reader's question send her back to improve it. That loop (plan, draft, read it back, hear the reader's question, revise, share) is the writing process, and the one writing recommendation the What Works Clearinghouse rates **strong** is teaching it "for a variety of purposes". The island cannot ask her to type or handwrite a story, cannot hear her, and never requires reading, so composing has to be **choosing and arranging picture-and-word tiles that the pet reads back**. That is also how Common Core itself frames kindergarten writing ("a combination of drawing, dictating, and writing"). **Define first a new game, Story Weaver (working name), levels 1–3, school band:** she builds a three-page story from tiles (who and where, what went wrong and what they tried, how it ended and how they felt); an owl who "can't see the pictures" hears it read, then asks about the first story part she left out; she may add it or say "that's my story", and either way the round is complete. No typed text, nothing is judged, and a whole story is six small choices, so keeping one later is a tiny bounded record. The new game needs the developer's confirmation of identity, land and whether stories are kept before it is built; the study also hands three smaller items to hosts that already exist (below).

**What people can do.** *Early writing.* Puranik and Lonigan, [*Reading and Writing* 24(5), 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3309424) (abstract opened): 372 preschoolers aged 3–5 (30 three-year-olds, 201 four-year-olds, 141 five-year-olds) wrote letters, their names, simple words and composed from a picture and a retell. Children "appear to progress along a continuum from scribbling to conventional spelling", "linear and task dependent", they wrote their names better than they composed sentences, and they fell back on simpler features (random letters) when a task got harder. *Transcription constrains composition.* Puranik, Duncan and Guo, *Early Childhood Research Quarterly* 2024 (**search summary of the listing; the article was not opened**): 282 kindergartners in 49 classrooms; "transcription had a strong constraining effect on both composition quality and productivity", and transcription moderated how oral language related to quality (negatively). **This is the reason tiles are a sound design, not a shortcut:** if getting letters down is what holds young composition back, a tile bank lets the part the island *can* teach (what to say, in what order, for whom) be practised on its own. It does not teach transcription, which stays with Letter Trails and Word Monsters, and nothing found shows that tile composing transfers to handwriting. (A search result that looked like the same finding, Balade and colleagues 2025, was opened and does not measure composition.) *Dictation.* Shanahan's kindergarten post on talk-then-scribe writing could not be fetched (HTTP 403), and a search summary reports that he describes the language-experience approach as shared experience, talk, teacher transcription, then small-group and individual dictation; **I found no study testing dictation against composing**. *Audience.* The sources found for "real audience raises quality" are teacher accounts and small studies: a three-lesson Year 7 action-research study (Sadler, Cambridge), a kindergarten teacher's year-long letters-home project and a fifth-and-sixth-grade newsletter (both practitioner reports). **The owl-as-reader is a design hypothesis**, not a finding. *Strategy teaching.* Graham and Perin's *Writing Next* (2007) ranks teaching students to plan, revise and edit first among eleven elements for adolescents; the effect size of .82 comes from **two secondary summaries and was not checked** in the report.

**Evidence limits.** Nothing opened shows that picture-and-tile story building improves handwriting, spelling or later writing, that a pretend reader raises quality, or what tweens or parents enjoy here. Completing a Story Weaver round shows she can arrange story parts and respond to a question, and nothing more.

## First game's proposed ladder: Story Weaver 1–3

**The scene.** A little book on a stage, with a page strip across the top (three pages: first, next, last) and a tile tray under it (each tile at least 140 by 120 units, a picture and a word, spoken on touch). An owl on a stump at the edge, clear of the pet, **"can't see the pictures, only hears the words"**; that is the reason the words matter. Tapping a tile puts it on the page; the pet reads the whole page back with the words lit one by one; tapping a placed tile takes it out. Text is composed from clause lines per slot ("Bunny" "at the pond" "lost the hat"), so it never needs a gendered pronoun and has no free text. The six parts, in order: **who, where, what went wrong, what they tried, how it ended, how they felt.** Problems are gentle (a lost hat, rain, stuck in mud, a wobbly tower, hungry); endings are drawn from an authored table of fitting endings per problem, so every combination she can build is a sensible story. Creative choices are never misses.

| Level | Name | Band window | First round | What she decides | Mistakes and hints | A rule test asserts | Cost and couplings |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | *Page by page* | school | The three pages open in turn. Page 1: pick a friend and a place; page 2: what went wrong; page 3: how it ended (three endings that fit). ## Experience and presentation

- **First unsupported action:** tap a tile. Every tile is spoken on touch and the page is read back with its words lit, so a non-reader hears exactly what she wrote. Reading it is support, not evidence of independent reading.
- **Touch-down feedback:** a tile dips and sounds its word; a placed tile settles into the page with a soft page-turn; Read it to the owl rings a small bell.
- **The reader's reaction is the feedback:** the owl tilts its head, asks one question, then rereads the whole story with the new part. It never sounds disappointed and never counts.
- **Replay variety:** friends, places, problems and tile order come from the seed; the endings drawn change with the problem.
- **The reveal:** the story is read once in full with the pictures; one sticker follows the finish, with or without help or an added part.
- **Off-screen activity:** WC10 Story scribe and WC11 Pass the story; WC12 sends something real to a real person.
- **Co-play:** the grown-up plays the owl: they listen, ask one question about a part that is missing, and she decides whether to add it.

## Decisions for the developer

1. **Identity, registration and land.** Lean: a new game, **Story Weaver** (working name), school band, in **Story Grove**, which lists 12 games; Puzzle Peaks lists 13, so a 13th is possible in the layout, but other proposals also target Story Grove, so the lands test decides. The alternative is Story Steps 14–15 (rejected above).
2. **Does she keep her story?** Not in the first slice. A story is six small choices (who, where, problem, try, ending, feeling), so a kept one is a tiny bounded record, not text; it still needs a new creation kind with repair and backup tests (development saves may be reset, per AGENTS). Lean: a journal entry "Stories I wrote" after level 3 plays well, not a new treehouse shelf.
3. **The owl's question and "That's my story".** Lean: the question is never a miss, declining always completes, and the sticker follows the finish. Alternative: none that fits "predictable rewards".
4. **A younger opening (WC2).** Lean: only after a child has played level 1 at school; the pet scribes one page.
5. **Review gates.** The problem, ending and feeling tiles, the clause lines and the owl's questions get an ordinary content review (gentle problems only; no frightening events, no loss, no "bad" feelings framed as wrong). **Anything on writing readiness, delay or "is my child behind?" needs a named literacy specialist and is not drafted here.** Opinion topics for WC4 avoid food as good or bad.
6. **Person checks to add:** whether a six-year-old understands that the owl cannot see the pictures; whether the owl's question reads as a suggestion and not a correction; portrait tile fit.

None of these blocks the WC1 definition: the lean in each is the default, and decision 1 gates the *build*.
