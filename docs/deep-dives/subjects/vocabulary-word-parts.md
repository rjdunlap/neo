# Vocabulary and word parts: two words make one, a part changes a word, a word can mean two things

*Subject `vocabulary-word-parts` · 2026-10-22 · `claude/vocabulary-word-parts-research` · [PR #814](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/814). Research and proposals only, for L/T/P/K/S/U/M and grown-ups. Base: `de8c3ef6`. No game was played or implemented.*

**Recommendation:** the island teaches many words by showing them (Photo Safari, Opposites, Teddy Doctor, Goodnight Room, Read & Do). Most word-part rungs already have an owner, all of them proposals waiting on [developer decisions](../../WAITING-ON-DEVELOPER.md):

- [Opposites](../opposites.md) level 9 owns **un-**, and its level 10 *Two answers* plans a word with two meanings.
- [Word Monsters](../word-monsters.md) G2 owns **re-, -ing, -ed and -s** (its decision 7).

**Nothing covers the compound word.** The grade-2 standard names it (L.2.4.d: "birdhouse, lighthouse, housefly; bookshelf, notebook, bookmark"), and preschoolers coin compounds themselves (studies below). A compound is also the only word part the island can say safely: its parts are whole words, so device speech never has to say a bare "un" or "-ed".

**Define first a small new picture game, *Word Glue* (working name): drag one picture onto another and they snap into one thing.** Sun plus flower makes a sunflower, and the pet says "sun… flower… sunflower!". Its deeper rungs ask:

- what the new word means from its parts (a birdhouse is a house *for a bird*);
- which part names the thing (a *goldfish* is a fish; a *houseboat* is a boat, and a *boathouse* is a house).

No built game joins two pictures into a third, so this is a new game rather than a mode. Its home land is a decision, because Story Grove already holds twelve games.

**Development.** Sources are labelled; only search summaries were seen for the compound studies, and the classic findings are recalled.

- *Compounds come early.* A single-child case study ([St. Cloud State Working Papers](https://repository.stcloudstate.edu/stcloud_ling/vol9/iss1/3), **search summary**) found novel compounds of one meaning type at 2;6 and all four types by 2;9. An English–Polish elicitation study ([Haman et al.](https://journals.pan.pl//Content/107622/PDF/50.pdf), **search summary**) found American preschoolers coined new words mostly by compounding, the dominant pattern of their language, and were still far from adult mastery. Clark (1993, *The Lexicon in Acquisition*; **recalled**) reports compound coinages from about two and agentive -er from about three to four.
- *Which part is the thing is harder.* Children of 4 to 6 were not yet adult-like at compound versus phrase stress ([Shilling](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/576736/1/Shilling%20Birmingham.pdf), **search summary**). French 3–5-year-olds named modifiers more often than heads ([Nicoladis and Krott 2007](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/Documents/college-les/psych/CogDev/NicoladisKrott2007.pdf), **search summary**); French puts the head first, so this does not transfer directly to English. Hence *which part names the thing* is a school rung, not a preschool one.
- *Inflection before derivation.* Berko 1958 ("one wug, two wugs"; **recalled**): 4–7-year-olds apply -s and -ed to nonsense words, and derived words and compounds come later. Carlisle (**recalled**): morphological awareness grows through grades 1–5, and transparent forms (enjoy → enjoyment) are easier than changed ones (heal → health).
- *Instruction.* Bowers, Kirby and Deacon 2010 ([*Review of Educational Research*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654309359353), 22 studies, **search summary**) found effects largest on word-part tasks themselves and smaller on comprehension, and instruction woven into wider literacy beat isolated drill. Goodwin and Ahn 2010 ([ASHA evidence-map summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/93cb3843-8a27-4b52-887b-32781be13e92), **search summary**) report an overall d = 0.33 and a vocabulary d = 0.40. The 2013 paper's finding of larger effects from preschool to grade 2 is **recalled**.
- *Categories.* Markman 1989 (**recalled**): young children expect a new word to name a kind of thing, and thematic sorting (dog with bone) gives way to sorting by kind. Anglin 1993 (**search summary**; the figures are recalled): school-age vocabulary growth is increasingly in words worked out from their parts.

**Evidence limits.** No opened study shows that joining pictures on a tablet improves a child's morphological awareness or reading. The instruction meta-analyses are classroom studies, and their gains on word-part tasks themselves do not establish comprehension. Word Glue's completion shows she can join pictured parts and pick a pictured meaning, nothing more. Device speech for invented compounds ("snail hat") and for the whole words is unchecked.

## Experience and presentation

- **First unsupported action:** drag a picture. Both pictures name themselves on touch-down, and the dragged one wobbles toward its partner.
- **Touch-down feedback:** the picture lifts and speaks its word; the target glows faintly when the dragged picture is near it, never hinting which partner is right on levels 2–5.
- **The payoff is the new thing alive:** a sunflower turns to the sun; a snowman waves; a dog curls up in the doghouse (Endless Reader's acted word).
- **Replay variety:** the round's compounds and positions come from the seed; level 3 rotates its shared heads.
- **Off-screen:** the word-inside-a-word card; Silly Compounds; find real compounds at home (toothbrush, doorbell, bedroom).

## Decisions for the developer

**Decided 2026-10-23 (on the developer's delegation):** Decisions 1–2 as in the [Word Glue definition](../../definitions/word-glue.md#open-decisions): a new game, the shared Story Grove allocation with Treehouse as fallback, and the session's source check for the compound list. Decisions 3–4 keep their leans.

1. **A new game, and its land.** Lean: yes to a new game (no built game joins two pictures into a third); home land Story Grove if its capacity test passes, else Puzzle Peaks or Treehouse. The working name *Word Glue* is a placeholder.
2. **Compound list review.** The list, its distractors and the deny list need an ordinary content read (an adult literacy reviewer, not a specialist): no opaque compound on meaning rungs, no distractor that is a real compound, regional words (US/UK) flagged.
3. **Family languages.** English puts the thing last (gold*fish*). Spanish, French and Portuguese often put it first or join a verb and a noun (*guarda-chuva*, *paraguas*). Arabic builds families from consonant roots (k-t-b, write: *kitāb*, *maktab*), which suits Root Garden more than Word Glue. Lean: Word Glue levels 1–3 can take another language's reviewed table later, while levels 4–5 are English-only and say so on the grown-ups' card.
4. **Reading at U and M.** Root Garden and Root Words probably need printed words, with spoken support always present. The decision is whether the school top reads.
5. **Person checks to add:** device speech for each whole compound and for invented two-word names (said with a pause); whether a three-year-old reads the join as "making a new thing"; drawing legibility of combined pictures at 140 units.
