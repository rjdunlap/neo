# Algebra and balance: what goes in, what comes out, and what keeps a seesaw level

**Subject:** `algebra-balance` · **Audiences:** L, T, P, K, S, U, M and G, as defined in the [subject tracker](../subjects.md#the-audiences) · **Requested:** 2026-10-16 by the [standards audit](standards-audit.md) (its Box Balance seed: "Seesaw Balance already leans here; a session can decide mode vs. new game before anyone builds") · **Researched:** 2026-10-19 · **Session:** `claude/subject-algebra-balance-finish`, [PR #621](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/621), resuming the claim in [PR #604](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/604).

**Research and proposals only. Nothing built** (slice 1, the [Rule Machine 1–4 definition](../../definitions/rule-machine.md), landed 2026-10-31, docs only; the builds are batches rule-machine 1 and 2). This study sits beside three neighbours and does not repeat them: the [Seesaw Balance dive](../seesaw-balance.md) and the [level 11 definition](../../definitions/seesaw-two-boxes.md) own **equations on a balance** (one unknown, the same off both sides, two steps, substitution; built to 10, defined to 11, a ladder to 17); the [Pattern Train dive](../pattern-train.md) owns **sequences** (repeating units to 17, stairs and steps at 18–20, *Add three, add four* at 21 "after a child plays 19–20"); and the [ratio study](ratio-proportion.md) owns proportional relationships. This study owns what none of them has: **a rule that turns an input into an output** ("what does the machine do?"), a rule read from a table, the inverse ("what went in?"), two rules in a row, a letter standing for any number, whether a log of pairs is a rule at all, and the one idea that ties all of it to the seesaw: the equal sign means *the same as*, not *the answer comes next*.

**Recommendation.** Define **Rule Machine** levels 1–4 first: a new game whose fun is feeding things into a machine and seeing what comes out, then saying what will come out *before* it does. The machine never refuses an input and never says no; the prediction is the only thing it checks, and a table of what went in and what came out fills as she plays, so the record is the help. Its pre-K opening is a hat machine (a rule you can see: everything comes out with a hat; 1.OA has nothing to say and that is the point); its `school` rungs add one more, two more, twice as many and a covered machine known only from its table; upper school runs the machine backwards and chains two machines; the 11–14 rung reads a letter card ("*n* goes in, 2*n* + 1 comes out"), finds the rule from two rows and judges whether a log is a rule at all (8.F.A.1). The grown-ups' form is **Box Balance**, a Lantern Lake puzzle: lettered boxes on both trays of Seesaw Balance's seesaw, a fewest-moves par found by search, the DragonBox shape the audit named. Seesaw Balance gets one more rung, *Can it ever be level?* (one answer, none, or every), through its own dive.

## What people can do, and what remains uncertain

- **Sourced (abstract and search summary, paper not opened):** [Knuth, Stephens, McNeil and Alibali, *Does Understanding the Equal Sign Matter? Evidence from Solving Equations*](https://www.nctm.org/Publications/journal-for-research-in-mathematics-education/2006/Vol37/Issue4/Does-Understanding-the-Equal-Sign-Matter_-Evidence-from-Solving-Equations/) (JRME 37(4), 2006; full text hosted at [Wisconsin](https://labweb.education.wisc.edu/knuth/taar/papers_rep_pub/JRME2006.pdf), not read this session). Many middle-school students "lack a sophisticated understanding of the equal sign", reading it "as an announcement of the result of an arithmetic operation", and that understanding "is associated with performance on equation-solving items". A follow-up ([Alibali, Knuth, Hattikudur, McNeil and Stephens 2007](https://mathed.net/wiki/Alibali,_Knuth,_Hattikudur,_McNeil,_%26_Stephens_(2007)), 81 students over three years, read through a wiki summary) found improvement "gradual, with many students still showing weak understanding and poor performance at the end of grade 8". **This is the study's central design fact:** a child who has only ever seen `3 + 4 =` followed by the answer learns that "=" means "now write the answer". So the island never shows an equal sign as a prompt for an answer. Rule Machine writes *in → out* with an arrow; Fruit Sums says "makes"; the equal sign first appears at the 11–14 rung on a card drawn as a level seesaw and spoken "the same as".
- **Sourced (search summary; PME proceedings PDF not opened):** [Blanton and Kaput 2004](https://emis.univie.ac.at/proceedings/PME28/RR/RR033_Blanton.pdf), urban elementary students: covariational thinking ("as this grows, that grows") "can appear by kindergarten" and children "can describe how quantities correspond by first grade", with teacher support; a later grade-4 study found functional thinking before any instruction in variables or notation. Treat as a direction, not a measurement: the children had teachers asking the questions. It licenses a **pre-K rule you can see** (every creature comes out with a hat) and a school *what does it do?* with counts, not a letter.
- **Sourced (page opened):** [DragonBox Algebra 5+](https://dragonbox.com/products/algebra-5) claims "children as young as 5" learn "the process needed to solve basic linear equations", with drawn cards "slowly replaced with variables and numbers" over ten chapters. That is a vendor's claim. **Recalled, not opened:** a Norwegian classroom study (Dolonen and Kluge, about 2015) reported strong in-game progress with weak transfer to written equations; a definition that cites it must open it and may find the direction differs.
- **Recalled, not opened:** Carraher, Schliemann, Brizuela and Earnest (2006) on third graders using letters for unknown amounts in a measured-quantity context; McNeil and Alibali (2005) on "operational patterns" (why the equal sign is misread after years of `a + b = c` practice); Carpenter, Franke and Levi, *Thinking Mathematically* (true/false number sentences as the classroom route to relational thinking). Recalled only.
- **Inference, not evidence:** that a 4–5-year-old enjoys predicting what a machine does to a creature; that a 6–8-year-old reads a two-row table as "the machine adds two"; that a 9–12-year-old chooses to run a machine backwards or chain two; that a 12–13-year-old reads a letter card or judges a broken log; that parents enjoy a fewest-moves balance puzzle. Device and person checks.
- **Not established:** that a machine toy produces the relational view of "=" or transfers to written equations. Rule tests prove the toy consistent (one rule fits the shown rows, every target is reachable), nothing about learning.

## What fits

- **Fits as a new game: Rule Machine.** The action (feed the machine, watch, then say what will come out before it does) exists nowhere on the island: Pattern Train asks what comes next in a line, Seesaw asks what keeps two trays level, Secret Code asks which code the marks allow. The learning is specific and visible: *a rule acts on whatever you give it, the table records it, and the same rule run backwards finds what went in*. It passes "a fun action with a clear learning purpose" only if the **prediction stays cheap** (one tap, and a wrong one is answered by the machine, not a buzzer), so the first thing a definition must fix is the accounting below.
- **Fits as modes:** Seesaw Balance 18 *Can it ever be level?* (U–M, through its dive); Pattern Train *Two trains* and *The car's number* (U–M, through its dive); Fruit Sums *Both trees* (S, a Fruit Sums dive). Box Balance for grown-ups is a new Lantern Lake puzzle only if the lake's capacity test passes (twelve games today); otherwise it is Seesaw Balance's 13+ level with a par.
- **Does not fit:** symbol-pushing drills ("simplify 3(x + 2)"); a typed-answer box; exponents, inequalities, negative coefficients and fractions below 11–14; "solve for x" wording to a child; a balance with lever distance (Seesaw's decision 4); any level whose equal sign appears before a child has met "the same as" on the seesaw.

### The equal-sign rule, stated once

- **Below the 11–14 rung the island never writes "=".** Rule Machine shows *in → out* (an arrow, a picture of the machine between); Fruit Sums says "makes"; Seesaw says "level" and "the same". Fruit Sums' sign is unchanged.
- **At the 11–14 rung the equal sign is drawn and spoken as a level seesaw:** the letter card at Rule Machine 7 shows `2n + 1` beside the machine, and the pet says "*n* goes in; two *n* plus one comes out", never "equals". If a definition ever writes `2n + 1 = 7`, the card draws a level seesaw between the sides and the pet says "the same as".
- **"=" is never a prompt for an answer.** No `3 + 4 =` with a blank after it, anywhere, at any band (Knuth's finding above). A missing number sits where it is missing: `8 + ? → 11` on a machine, a bag on a tray on the seesaw.

**For M.** The tracker's tween evidence favours real challenge and autonomy. A 12–13-year-old would plausibly choose *find the rule from two rows*, the broken log and Box Balance's par over the feeding levels; that is a design bet. Recommendation: Rule Machine 7–8 at `school`'s top levels, Box Balance for 13+. Placement and the 12/13 entry are the developer's.

**Creature-world fit (rework).** The hat machine's outputs are a natural **wearable**: a hat the machine put on a creature at level 1 could be a permanent unlock the pet keeps (RW02b's wearables fit decides whether such parts exist); the journal files each *rule she found* as a card with its table (only what a round actually showed); a rework project might ask "the festival needs twice as many lanterns as last year", where the premise is fiction and the rule is the same. Nothing accrues, wears out or waits. The machine is not a creature.

## A first game's ladder: Rule Machine

Working ID `rule-machine`, **not registered**. The decision is **what the machine will do to the next thing**, then **what the rule is**, then **what went in**. Overlap checked against Pattern Train (what comes next in a line), Seesaw Balance (what keeps trays level), Secret Code (which code the marks allow), Robot Path (a program she writes), Chain Reaction (a machine she builds) and Critter Sort (where a thing goes): only Rule Machine has a rule that acts on an input and a prediction about it.

**Interaction.** A machine across the middle of the screen with a **hopper** on the left and a **chute** on the right, at least 100 units each; a **tray** of inputs along the bottom right (the pet keeps the bottom left). Drag an input to the hopper (or tap it: it hops in), the machine chugs for half a second with a rising pentatonic phrase, and the output rolls down the chute; the pair is written into a **table** above the machine as *in → out* (pictures at level 1, counters and numerals from 2, numerals alone from 4). From level 2, before the machine runs, three **prediction pads** appear under the chute: tap what will come out, then the machine runs and the real output lands beside the prediction. From level 3 a **rule shelf** of three cards appears after three rows; tap the card that fits every row. The machine never refuses an input, never runs out, and is never wrong. **Undo** is not needed (feeding changes nothing); **Peek** (from level 4) lifts the cover for one input, a requested hint.

**Accounting and help.** Feeding the machine, watching, lifting nothing and plotting are exploration. A **first wrong prediction on a new rule is never a miss** (the machine's answer is the lesson, as Chain Reaction 8's bell and Seesaw 15's guess are unscored); a wrong prediction after the table holds two rows, a wrong rule card, a wrong backwards car and a chain that does not fit are one miss each. After a miss the pet names one row ("4 went in and 6 came out: two more"); after two misses the right pad, card or car glows; a supported finish then runs the ghost's input and the round completes with its sticker. **Peek** (level 4) is a requested hint. Assisted completion is not mastery evidence.

**Rule tests a definition must name** (by enumeration): every level's rule pool keeps outputs within its pads (0–12 at 2–4, 0–30 at 5–8) for every tray input; at levels 3, 4 and 7 the shown rows (any three at 3, the player's three at 4, the two given at 7) are fitted by **exactly one** of the three shelf cards, and the distractor cards each fit at least one shown row (so the table, not luck, decides); at level 4 every authored rule pool contains a three-input set that separates every pair of rules; every level-5 output has exactly one whole-number input in the tray; every level-6 target is reachable by at least one two-card chain from the shelf, and in at least one case per round the reverse order fails; every level-8 case has exactly one log with a repeated input and, among the rest, exactly one straight-line log; every hint names a row, pad, card or car that some solution changes; the hint after two misses leads to a solvable state. These prove toy consistency, not algebra learning; the letter-card and "rule at all" wording wants a mathematics educator's read.

**Cost.** One M game across three build slices; no new engine, physics or persistence beyond journal entries and (if the rework takes it) a wearable from the hat machine.

## Experience and presentation

The first unsupported action is level 1's tap on a creature at the hopper; touch-down squashes it, it hops in at once, and the machine's chug starts before the finger lifts. The ghost finger is feasible: the same taps and drags, with inputs and predictions supplied by the rule solver, and a card tap. Replay variety comes from seeded rule pools per level (the rule, the inputs and the distractors vary; the interaction does not), seeded chains at 6 and seeded logs at 8. Sounds: a chug that rises a pentatonic step per row, a thunk as the output lands, a chime on a right prediction and a soft "huh?" boing on a wrong one; the island's loop plays. All instructions go through `ctx.instruct`; "what will come out?", "two more" and "*n* goes in" are spoken, counts and cards are pictures, and no letter is required reading before level 7. The pet watches from the bottom left and holds the table up on a hint. Off-screen: the *Guess my rule* conversation card (any card that hangs weights at home is a reviewed practical card and waits for that reviewer).
