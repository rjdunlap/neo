# Food and fiber systems: where a thing began, who changed it, and which way it can go

**Subject:** `food-fiber-systems` · **Audiences:** L, T, P, K, S, U, M and G, as defined in the [subject tracker](../subjects.md#the-audiences) · **Requested:** the tracker's [follow-up additions](../subjects.md#additional-gaps-from-the-follow-up-review) row (where food, cloth and paper come from; growing, transforming, moving and using resources) and the [follow-up audit's](additional-subjects.md) boundary: the distinct question is how a source, a transformation and a delivery connect · **Researched:** 2026-10-31 · **Session:** `claude/research-food-fiber-systems`, [PR #1067](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/1067)

**Research and proposals only; nothing here is built.** The island already grows flowers (Garden Grow), blends pretend food (Recipe Workshop), shares it (Pet Kitchen), weaves a scarf (Little Loom), sells cups (Lemonade Stand) and carries letters (Mail Carrier); the medieval study has queued *Sheep to cloth* and the Silk Road study *The silkworm's thread* as Story Steps stories. What it never asks is the question the strongest evidence below points at: **a finished thing hides where it began.** In an Australian survey only a quarter of Grade 6 students classed cotton socks as coming from a plant, and most thought cardboard came from a factory. Ordering pictured steps does not reach that; seeing the *same* source change form at each maker's hands, and trying a wrong beginning to watch what it would have made instead, might.

**Recommendation.** Define **Maker Lane 1–3** first (FS1), a new game: a lane of island makers' stalls (shearer, washer, spinner, dye pots, loom; a field, a mill and an oven; a tree, a chipper, a pulp vat and a press). She carries a bundle whose **form changes** at each stall, and a maker can only work on the form in front of them. Level 1 is a cause-and-effect walk where the next maker waves; level 2 *Who can use it now?* asks her to carry the bundle to the maker who can work on it (a maker who cannot shows, in a thought bubble, the form they need: "Not yet. I need it clean first"); level 3 *Where did it begin?* starts from a finished thing (a cotton sock, a cardboard box, a loaf, a scarf) and asks for its beginning, and a wrong beginning is answered by running *that* source down the lane to the different thing it makes. Later levels add a real decision with more than one right route: **stripes or plain** (dye the fleece, the yarn or the cloth; a plain scarf accepts all three, stripes need the colour in before the loom) and **two ways to a box** (new wood chips or old paper). The supply-chain puzzle for older players and grown-ups (FS5) and a clothing-label card for the grown-ups' page (FS3) follow.

## What people can do, and what remains uncertain

- **A processed thing hides its source.** ACER's [survey for the Primary Industries Education Foundation](https://www.acer.org/news/article/student-understanding-of-food-and-fibre-origins) (**opened**; news article of 30 March 2012 on 213 Grade 6 and 687 Grade 10 students and 53 teachers from 71 schools, data June–October 2011; the full Hillman and Buckley report was not opened) found most students could name wheat and wool products, "trace the stages of getting fruit from an orchard to the home" and classify products as plant or animal, but only **25% of Grade 6 and 58% of Grade 10** classed cotton socks as a plant product, and most in both grades thought **cardboard** came from a factory or laboratory rather than from plants. **Consequence:** the gap is not ordering steps (they could) but connecting a heavily changed thing back to a living source. Level 3's wrong beginnings are exactly *an animal* for the sock and *a factory* for the box.
- **The same pattern in UK surveys.** British Nutrition Foundation surveys of 2014 and 2017 (**search summaries only**, press coverage; the BNF pages not opened) are reported as finding a third of 5–8-year-olds saying pasta comes from animals and a fifth of primary children never having visited a farm. Advocacy-commissioned polls, not developmental research; recorded as consistent, not as evidence of an age norm.
- **No developmental study of when children understand transformation chains was found;** recorded as a gap. Naive-biology research on living and non-living things is adjacent but was not opened. The curricula agree on *where from* at about 5–7 and *how it was processed* at about 7–11, so the levels follow that order, with *Where did it begin?* first offered at pre-K as a picture choice and its explanation (the replay) carrying the meaning.
- **Middle steps are the hardest to order** (Solé 2019, opened for the [river study](ancient-river-civilizations.md#anchors-and-evidence-limits), *recalled* here). **Consequence:** Maker Lane shows every middle form as a distinct object (lumpy fleece, clean fleece, a fluffy roll, yarn, cloth) and never asks her to order steps from memory; each maker's thought bubble shows the form they need.
- **Real processes, simplified honestly.** The Maine State Museum's [*Wool production in depth*](https://mainestatemuseum.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Wool-Production-In-Depth.pdf) (**opened**, PDF text extracted locally) gives scouring, picking, carding ("the combing, lining up, and blending of fibers into one loose piece"), spinning, weaving, fulling, napping and shearing; washing "until it is clean and ready for carding". Wikipedia's [Dyeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyeing) (**opened**, an encyclopedia) gives fibre (stock) dyeing "before they are spun into yarn", yarn dyeing "before the fabric manufacturing stage", piece dyeing "after producing fabrics with undyed yarns", and garment dyeing; a trade summary (search only) adds that yarn-dyed fabrics suit stripes and checks, since the pattern comes from the yarn. Paper's steps (debark, chip, pulp, press and dry; recycled paper re-pulped) come from classroom pages found by search (Montana Agriculture in the Classroom, Project Learning Tree, Wonderopolis; **search summaries, not opened**). **Consequence:** the island keeps three or four makers per chain (fleece: shear, wash, card-and-spin, weave; cotton: pick, clean, spin, knit; wheat: cut, mill, bake; paper: chip, pulp, press), says so on the grown-up card, and a reviewer checks the pictures (decision 5).
- **The mill history includes children's labour** (the Maine PDF: machines needing "multiple children and one adult"; "Kids often started working in mills at ages 8 or 9 during the 1800s"), and NALO T5.3-5.c names the cotton gin, whose history is bound to enslaved labour. Decision 4.

## What fits

- **Fits:** a bundle that changes form at each maker; makers who can only work on one form and say what they need; a finished thing traced back to a living beginning, with a wrong beginning shown making something else; two right routes; plants, trees, wool, milk and eggs as sources; makers as island friends (T5.K-2.e).
- **Does not fit, and where it goes instead:** ordering steps from memory (Story Steps' *Sheep to cloth*); routing to a matching door (Mail Carrier); planting needs and rain (Garden Grow, life science); recipes and measuring (Recipe Workshop, Pet Kitchen); prices, profit and swaps (Lemonade Stand, Market Stall, Swap Meet); reuse and recycling places (Second Chance Shed). Healthy-food judgements, food safety, storage and calories never. Meat and slaughter not depicted (decision 3). No perishability, idle earnings, crops that die or resource debt, as the audit rules out.

## A first game's ladder: Maker Lane 1–3

The scene is a short lane of three or four stalls, each a maker friend (a creature from the round's seed) at a large picture of their tool: shears, a wash tub, a spinning wheel, a loom; or a sickle, a millstone, an oven; or a chipper, a pulp vat, a press. The bundle sits in a basket at the lane's start. Every form is a distinct, large picture (lumpy fleece, clean fleece, a fluffy roll, yarn, cloth, a scarf).

| Level | First round | What she decides | Mistakes and hints | Rule test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1 · Along the lane** · toddler–preschool | The fleece chain; the next maker waves and glows | Nothing to get wrong: tap the waving maker (or drag the bundle there) and the bundle changes; the finished scarf goes to the pet | No misses. A tap on another maker makes them wave and point at the glowing one, free | Every chain reaches its product in exactly its maker count |
| **2 · Who can use it now?** · preschool–school | Three makers in a shuffled order along the lane, no glow | Which maker can work on the bundle as it is | Tapping a maker without the bundle is free (they show what they make). Carrying it to a maker who cannot use it yet: they shake their head and their thought bubble shows the form they need ("Not yet. I need it clean first"), one miss. Two misses glow the right maker (one hint) | The bundle's form has exactly one maker who accepts it at this level; the thought bubble names a form reachable from the current one |
| **3 · Where did it begin?** · pre-K–school | A finished thing (a cotton sock, a cardboard box, a loaf, a scarf) and three beginnings (four at school), always including the evidence-based tempting wrong one: *a sheep* for the sock, *a factory chimney* for the box | Which beginning it came from | A wrong beginning runs down the lane and becomes something else beside the asked thing ("Wool makes a woolly sock. This sock is smooth cotton, from a plant"), one miss; the factory makes nothing and the pet says "The factory changes it, but it began somewhere growing". Two misses glow the right beginning (one hint). Right: the beginning runs down the lane and becomes the asked thing | Each thing has exactly one right beginning among its choices; every wrong living beginning makes a different, drawn product; the factory card appears only for processed things |

**Cost:** M–L (a new game). The maker graph and the beginnings table are in `logic.ts` and tested; the replay reuses the lane walk.

## Experience and presentation

- **The first unsupported action** is dragging the lumpy fleece onto a waving maker and watching it come back clean; at level 3 it is choosing a beginning and watching it walk the lane.
- **Touch-down feedback:** the bundle lifts under the finger (40 units above, per the pitfalls), the touched maker leans forward and their tool twitches.
- **Replay variety:** a seeded chain per round (fleece, cotton, wheat, paper), maker creatures and their stall order, and the colour of the scarf.
- **Off-screen:** the FS3 card's label hunt.
- **Speech:** "began as a plant", "began on a sheep", "began as a tree" in speech; *fibre*, *processed* and *raw material* on the grown-up card only.

Maker friends are a natural place for collected creatures to work, and a finished scarf could later become a wearable through the rework's customization; neither is required, and nothing here depends on rework infrastructure.

## Decisions for the developer

Under the [October 23 standing rules](../../archive/DECISIONS-2026-10-23.md#standing-rules) the defining session adopts each lean; only the person checks and named reviews wait.

1. **Identity.** A new game rather than an extension: the action (carrying a bundle whose form decides which maker can work on it, and tracing a thing back to a beginning) is not Story Steps' ordering, Mail Carrier's matching or Little Loom's weaving. **Lean: new game `maker-lane`, working title *Maker Lane*;** home land by standing rule S8 at definition time (Cozy Village first, Painted Canyon beside Little Loom as the fallback), paging rather than shrinking.
2. **Chains.** Fleece to scarf, cotton to sock, wheat to loaf, tree to box, each three or four makers. Milk and eggs only as level 3 beginnings, never a maker step. **Lean: as stated;** silk stays with the Silk Road study's story.
3. **Animals.** Shearing is drawn as a haircut that leaves a happy, lighter sheep; no meat, leather or slaughter is depicted or named. **Lean: as stated.**
4. **Labour history.** Child mill labour and the enslaved labour behind cotton are not depicted in any child's band. **Lean: not on the child's screen;** a reviewed grown-up-card sentence only if FS6 or a history rung ever names mills or the gin.
5. **Accuracy review.** The makers' pictures and spoken lines (especially "the factory changes it, but it began somewhere growing", true of cardboard and cotton but not of plastic or glass, which never appear in level 3) are checked by a named reviewer (an agricultural-literacy or textile educator) before release. **Lean: build with the lines marked for review,** as earlier studies did.
6. **The grown-ups' card.** Conversation and looking only: no paper-making, dyeing or cooking instructions. **Lean: as stated.**

**Device and person checks (for DEVICE-CHECKS once built):** does a four-year-old pick the sheep for the sock because it is soft, and does the replay change her next pick; does the thought bubble tell her which maker comes next, or does she try every stall; does a grown-up use the label card?
