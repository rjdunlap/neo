# Informational reading: a book that tells you, a page that answers, a picture that explains

*Subject `informational-reading` · 2026-10-22 · `claude/sharp-cannon-xdrzdf` · [PR #815](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/815). Research and proposals only, for L/T/P/K/S/U/M and grown-ups. Base: `1d142eb0`. No game was played or implemented.*

**Recommendation:** the island already reads several kinds of information text, each with its owner:

- **Maps and keys:** Mail Carrier (picture map and key at 6–8; 13 and 15 proposed) and Treasure Map (grids, directions).
- **Graphs:** Picture Graph (build, read, keyed graphs).
- **Picture procedures:** Recipe Workshop's card and Teddy Doctor's check-up card.
- **Printed signs:** Read & Do (its game dive is unstarted).

**Nothing teaches using a book's features to find an answer.** The grade-1 standard names exactly that: "Know and use various text features (e.g., headings, tables of contents, glossaries, electronic menus, icons) to locate key facts" (RI.1.5). Its grade-2 form adds captions, indexes and finding the fact "efficiently" (RI.2.5).

**Define first a small new game, *Look It Up* (working name).** A friend arrives at the picnic with a question. She opens the island guide with its picture tabs and finds the friend's page. On that page a fact box always sits in the same place. She finds the answer there, then makes it happen in the scene: the cow gets its hay, the duck naps by the pond. The guide's facts are about the island's own friends, so a rule test can keep every page true to the scene and no fact reviewer is needed.

**Development.** Sources are labelled as the subagent reported them.

- *Print in context comes first, decoding later.* Masonheimer, Drum and Ehri (1984; **secondary only**, via the replication) found that pre-readers named environmental print in context but not the same words in plain type. [Zhao et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3899066/) (**opened**) found 3–5-year-olds "did not easily learn words by extracting their visual form information"; colour and logo carried the meaning until about five. **So no rung below school tests word reading**: a label speaks and points, and the picture carries the answer.
- *Pictures as symbols.* DeLoache's scale-model work ([APS profile](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/25at25/judy-s-deloache.html), **opened**) found 2½-year-olds failed to use a model of a room and 3-year-olds succeeded. That pictures work earlier than models is **recalled**. Liben and Downs on map symbols (**secondary only**): young children grasp that a map stands for a place while misreading its symbols. This supports picture tabs that look like the friend, not abstract icons, at the youngest rungs.
- *Little informational text in early classrooms.* Duke (2000), 3.6 minutes a day in first grade ([Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/articles/researchbytopic/4921), **search only**). Duke and Kays (1998), kindergartners pick up information-book language from read-alouds (**recalled**). Kelley and Clausen-Grace's *text feature walk*, a look at headings, captions and pictures before reading ([metadata](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/facultybib2010/346) opened; method **recalled**), becomes the grown-ups' page card.
- *Locate and retrieve is its own process.* PIRLS gives "focus on and retrieve explicitly stated information" 20% of its grade-4 items. PISA splits locating into scanning one text and choosing among several by their headers ([PIRLS](https://pirls2021.org/frameworks/home/reading-assessment-framework/pirls-framework-emphases-in-pirls-and-epirls), [PISA](https://cmec.ca/docs/pisa2018/PISA2018_Highlights_EN.pdf); **search only**). That gives the ladder: one page, then the right page among several, then two pages together.
- *Older children skip diagrams.* [Jian 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02263/full) (**opened**) reports 10-year-olds looking at illustrations for about 6% of reading time (Hannus and Hyönä 1999). It also reports that brief strategy teaching raised fourth graders' time on illustrations from 16% to 27% and their scores from 61% to 71%, mostly on items that needed text and picture together. Cromley's diagram-convention lessons (**search only**) teach one convention at a time: arrow, label line, cutaway, inset. Hence Diagram Detective asks only what the diagram can answer.
- *Picture procedures:* **no age norm was found**. Picture recipes are standard early-years practice without a study behind an age.

**Evidence limits.** No opened study shows that tapping tabs on a tablet guide improves a child's use of real books. Look It Up's completion shows she can find a pictured fact with spoken support, nothing more. It is not evidence of independent reading. Device speech for the page sentences is unchecked.

**Why a new game rather than a Read & Do mode** (decision 1). Read & Do's action is *read a sign, tap its meaning*: one text, already in view. Look It Up's action is *find the right page among several, then the right place on it*. Searching a book is the skill, and the book is the object on screen. A Read & Do level would keep its one-sign layout and lose the tabs, so the meaningful action is not the same.

**What does not fit:** timed reading; a reading-speed or "pages per minute" score; scored page flips (RI.5.7's "quickly" means fewer looks, and looking is free); streaks or a daily page; testing word reading below school; a growing guide that fills from discoveries (that is collection and shell work in the rework's territory, and needs a save field); real-animal facts without the zoo-animals reviewer.

## A first game's ladder: Look It Up

**The guide.** A picture book on a stand beside the picnic blanket. Each page is about one island friend: a big picture, the friend's name (spoken at every band), and a **fact box in the same place on every page** with three icon rows. *Eats* shows a food picture, *Plays with* a toy, *Naps at* a spot. Picture tabs down the side show each friend's face. The facts are about these island friends, not about all cows: *Eats* comes from Animal Snack's `FAVORITE` table (cow hay, bunny carrots, dog bones, cat fish, duck seeds, pig apples, bear honey), so the two games can never disagree. *Plays with* and *Naps at* are authored island facts in the game's `logic.ts`.

| Level | Name | Bands | First round | What she decides | Mistakes and hints | A rule test asserts | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Talking labels** | toddler, preschool | The guide lies open on Cow's page. Three label tags (a name tag, the food, the toy) each have a line to their picture. Touch a tag or its picture: the tag lights and speaks ("hay"), the picture wiggles. When all three have spoken, Cow walks in and eats. Three pages a round | Nothing to get wrong: a label belongs to a thing and says what it is | No misses | every label's line ends on its own picture; every tag speaks a line in `voice-script.ts`; the round ends only after each tag was heard | M: the guide, page flip, label tags; critters from `CRITTERS`; foods from Animal Snack's art |
| 2 | **Find the page** | preschool, pre-K | The guide opens on another friend's page. Cow walks in, hungry. Three picture tabs (cow, duck, bear). Tap Cow's face: the page flips and speaks, "Cow eats hay". Three foods sit in the basket; drag or tap the hay and Cow eats. Three visitors a round | Which tab, then which food the page shows | Flipping any tab is free and speaks its page. Giving the wrong food is one miss: the friend shakes its head kindly and the pet says "Let's look in the guide". Two misses glow the friend's tab, then the food on its page (one hint) | the opening page is never the visitor's page; each food in the basket is some tab's *Eats*, and exactly one is the visitor's; page facts equal Animal Snack's `FAVORITE`; the bot taps the tab, then the food | S: authored round tables; reuses level 1's book |
| 3 | **The fact box** | pre-K, school | Duck asks: "Where do I like to nap?" Three spots in the scene (by the pond, under the tree, in the barn). Find Duck's page, touch the *Naps at* row (it speaks: "Duck naps by the pond"), then lead Duck to the pond. Next visitors ask *Plays with* or *Eats* | Which page, which row, which thing in the scene | Flipping and row taps are free. A wrong spot or toy is one miss: the friend says that is not its place, "the guide knows". Two misses glow the row (one hint) | within a round no two visitors share an answer in the asked row, so another page's fact never works by accident; every row's answer is drawn in the scene; the fact box layout is identical on every page | S: about seven toys and three spots drawn; authored fact table |

**Accounting (decided by the definition):** level 1 has no misses. At levels 2–3, flipping pages, touching labels or rows, and replaying speech are free; only an answer acted out in the scene can be a miss. Two misses on one visitor add one glow hint. One sticker per round, with or without help; the adaptive rules follow AGENTS. Completion with spoken support is not evidence of independent reading.

**Bands (proposal):** toddler 1, preschool 1–2, pre-K 2–3, school 3 (IR1). IR2 later extends school to 3–6.

**Not a quiz of known facts.** At level 2 she may already know a cow eats hay from Animal Snack. That is deliberate: the first use of a guide confirms something familiar. Level 3 asks facts that only the guide holds, so looking is needed.

## Experience and presentation

- **First unsupported action:** touch a label. It lights and speaks on touch-down, and its picture wiggles.
- **Touch-down feedback:** a tab lifts and the page begins to turn as the finger lands; a fact row lights and speaks; a food or toy lifts and names itself.
- **The payoff is the fact coming true:** Cow munches the hay; Duck settles by the pond and tucks its head; Bear hugs its drum. Read & Do's way of bringing meaning into the scene.
- **Replay variety:** the visitors, their order, the opening page and the asked row come from the seed. Tab order is fixed in a round so the book feels like a real book.
- **Off-screen:** the text feature walk with a non-fiction book at home; the sign walk outdoors; Ask me, I'll look it up with any picture book.

## Decisions for the developer

4. **Person checks to add:** device speech for each page sentence; whether a three-year-old understands that the tabs are faces of pages; tab legibility at 100 units in portrait.
