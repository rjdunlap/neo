# Inventions: meeting a need, testing and improving, how an idea spreads

**Subject:** `inventions` · **Audiences:** L, T, P, K, S, U, M, G ([definitions](../subjects.md#the-audiences)) · **Date:** 2026-10-15 · **Session:** `claude/inventions-research`, [PR #392](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/392).

Research and proposals only; no implementation. Recommend a new game, **Gadget Bench**, first. A neighbor on the island has a pictured need ("carry my apples home over the bumpy path and keep them dry"). She fits large parts into the sockets of a little gadget, **tries it on the bench test path**, sees exactly where it fails, changes one part and tries again, then gives it to the neighbor. The ladder follows the strongest evidence found: young children rarely invent a tool unaided but readily adapt one they have been shown. So it starts with **choosing between two built gadgets**, then **adapting one the pet has just built**, and reaches unaided design, constraints and a **new kit for the same need** only at school levels. Its top case, **Workshop Cases**, is the upper-school, early-teen and parent answer: three tested prototypes, a pictured results table, and one design that combines their strongest features. "An idea spreads" is the payoff, shown by the island's neighbors adopting her gadget, not by a quiz. Real inventions (wheel, printing, bulb, first flight) wait for a reviewer and join [Timeline Trail's](time-change.md#build-slices-in-order) reviewed real-object rung. The [roadmap packets](../../ROADMAP.md#inventions-recommendations) are definitions to write before builds, not a scheduled queue.

**What people can do with inventing (opened unless marked):**

- **Beck, Cutting, Apperly et al. (2014)**, "Is tool-making knowledge robust over time and across problems?", [*Frontiers in Psychology* 5:1395](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01395/full), full text read. Before any demonstration, 0% of 4–5-year-olds and 16% of 5–6-year-olds bent a pipe cleaner into a hook to retrieve a bucket from a tube. After seeing one made, almost all children, even 3-year-olds, made and used one, and kept that knowledge for three months. Handling the materials first and verbal prompts did not help. The 4–7-year-olds **did not transfer** hook-making from pipe cleaners to a dowel. The paper summarizes: "tool innovation is difficult for young children, whereas learning from others how to make new tools … is easy." It describes **Beck et al. (2011)**, "Making tools isn't child's play", *Cognition* 119(2), which was not opened (search excerpts: most children succeed only around age 8). **Implication:** choosing and adapting before inventing; a demonstration is a fair rung, not a giveaway; transfer to a new kit is a real, later decision.
- **German and Defeyter (2000)**, "Immunity to functional fixedness in young children", *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 7 (search excerpts only). Reported that 5-year-olds were less fixed on an object's designed use than 6–7-year-olds. A 2014 Minnesota dissertation (Nehring; search excerpt) found fixedness at every age when the typical use was shown first. **Implication:** "use a part in a new way" is a hypothesis to observe, not a rung to promise.
- **The Wright brothers' testing**, [Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum, "Testing at Kitty Hawk"](https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/wright-brothers-kitty-hawk), opened. Their 1901 glider, with **larger wings, performed worse** than the 1900 one. They questioned the published data, built wind tunnels in Dayton, tested dozens of wing shapes, and the 1902 glider flew well. **Implication:** the method (test, find the failure, change, test again) and the honest fact that a "bigger, better" change can fail. Used as a precedent for the game's loop, not as quiz content.

**Limits.** No study was found of a game that teaches design iteration to under-sixes, or of tween or parent engagement with these proposals; the [tracker's tween evidence](../subjects.md#what-the-search-found-about-the-older-range-2026-10-14) gives hypotheses only. The Museum of Science (EiE) design-process page returned 404; Spark!Lab and the Exploratorium Tinkering Studio returned 403. **Printing history was not opened at a museum or scholarly source:** search excerpts agree that Gutenberg combined several existing techniques, that metal movable type had earlier Korean and Chinese roots, and that credit is contested. That is the reason the real-history rung needs a reviewer and must not tell a single-inventor story. No Head Start ELOF or preschool engineering standard was opened.

## What fits, and what does not

- **Fits as a new game:** Gadget Bench, because meeting a stated need with a designed object, testing it across conditions, and revising it is an action no built game or proposal owns. **The test before building:** if level 1 reduces to placing a ramp for a marble, connecting gears, or picking three pieces for three pictured needs with no position, condition or constraint, it becomes a level of Chain Reaction, Gear Garden or Habitat Helpers instead.
- **Fits as its top case:** Workshop Cases, a results-table deduction for U, M and G.
- **Fits as a payoff, not a game:** "an idea spreads". After a round, the neighbor uses the gadget and two other neighbors build their own copies in the background. A version strip shows her tries (version 1 tipped at the bump; version 2 made it).
- **Fits the grown-ups' page:** a "What problem does it solve?" walk and a cardboard fix-it card, both needing review.
- **Later, after review:** **Better and Better**, real inventions as version chains (sledge, rollers, wheel; candle, lamp, bulb; the Wrights' gliders), shared with Timeline Trail's real-object rung. **Print Shop** stays a sketch (below).
- **Does not fit:** a single heroic inventor, dates or names below school, a quiz of who-invented-what, races or rivals, explosions as fun, earned-part gates, a "best design" score, timers, or a real tool-use instruction without review.

## First game's proposed ladder: Gadget Bench

**Scene.** A workbench by the water with the pet as helper. On the left a neighbor holds up a **request card** with two or three pictured criteria (three apples, a bumpy path, a rain cloud). In the middle sits the gadget: a sturdy box body with **three or four large sockets** (bottom, top, front, back). A tray below holds parts, each at least 100 units: small wheels, big wheels, sled runners, a float, a basket, a lid, a handle, a long hook-pole, an umbrella, a lantern. On the right a **test path** shows the conditions in order (smooth, bump, puddle, dark). **Try** runs the gadget along the path; it stops at the first condition it cannot meet and shows why (the apples hop out at the bump, the cart sinks in the puddle) and the responsible socket glows. **Give** hands it to the neighbor, who runs the same path for real.

**Model.** Each part has one allowed socket and grants fixed capabilities per condition (big wheels: bump; float: puddle; lid: rain and bump-spill; lantern: dark). A part in the wrong socket does nothing for that condition (a pole at the back cannot reach; wheels on top do not roll), which is K-2-ETS1-2's shape-and-function. No physics: the path is an authored, deterministic check, so every assembly can be enumerated.

### Rules, support and completion contract to define

- **Rules in `logic.ts`.** Parts, sockets, conditions and requests are authored tables. `runPath(gadget, path)` returns the first failing condition and the socket responsible, or success. Rule tests enumerate every assembly for each seeded round and check: at least one passes; levels 5–7 have at least two passing designs where stated; level 3's distractor fails only because of its socket; level 6's tray contains no part from the shown solution and still has a pass; level 1's two gadgets differ in exactly one part; every hint installs a part that belongs to some passing design and keeps correct work.
- **Accounting (Chain Reaction's precedent).** **Try is free:** a bench test that fails is an experiment, never a miss. **Give is the claim:** giving a gadget that fails the neighbor's path is a miss, with a gentle boing and the failure shown and spoken ("the apples hopped out at the bump"). After two misses, or two Trys that fail at the same condition without changing that socket, the responsible socket glows and "Show me" installs one useful part, counted as one hint. Level 2's demonstration and level 7's choice between valid designs count nothing. Adaptation therefore reads Give misses and Show-me hints.
- **Completion.** A passing Give ends the round: the neighbor uses the gadget, two neighbors copy it in the background ("an idea spreads"), and a version strip shows her tries. One sticker, help or not. A how-to card per level, a ghost-finger `autotouch()` that drags a part to its socket and taps Try then Give, voice lines in `voice-script.ts`, and 100-unit parts and sockets in portrait (the path folds below the bench).
- **No real inventions, inventors or dates** in levels 1–8.

## Workshop Cases: the older and family case

Three prototypes already built by island neighbors sit on the bench, each with a pictured results row across four conditions (Fox's cart: bump ✓ puddle ✗ dark ✓ gate ✗). She reads the table, inspects any prototype freely, and assembles one gadget that passes every condition by combining the parts that made each prototype succeed. **Every case has a built-in conflict** so that copying the winning part for each condition is not enough: two of those parts need the same socket, or the gate constraint rules one out, and one must be swapped for a different part that covers two conditions. A rule test, like Timeline Trail's progress-heuristic test, proves that the naive "take every part that passed" assembly **fails** in every case while at least one passing assembly exists. The checker accepts every passing assembly; an optional comparison after success shows how many parts it used. Undo, free bench Trys and a supported finish (the socket of the first unmet condition glows after two misses) as above. Its appeal to 12- and 13-year-olds and parents is untested.

## Print Shop: a sketch only

A press with a frame of six slots. Each neighbor's invitation is a row of picture tiles (a cake, a sun, a boat). She sets tiles in the frame and prints; the next neighbor's invitation differs by one picture, so she swaps one tile instead of carving a new block. It teaches reusable parts. It needs a definition that keeps it a picture game rather than a reading one, a check against Stamp Studio's "no copying a model" rule, and a reviewer before any real printing history (Gutenberg, earlier Korean and Chinese movable type) is said.

## Experience and presentation

A part lifts and sways the moment a finger lands on it (touch-down feedback), then clicks into its socket with a wooden thunk on a pentatonic step; Try plays a little run along the path with a cheerful toot at each condition passed and a soft wobble at the failure. The version strip is a row of small pictures, never a score. The island's music keeps playing. The first unsupported action is level 1's Try on either gadget: there is no wrong first touch. Replay variety comes from seeded neighbors, requests and paths, not from new rules each round.
