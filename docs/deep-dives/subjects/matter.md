# Matter: warm it, cool it, mix it, and see what it does

**Subject:** `matter` · **Audiences:** L, T, P, K, S, U, M and G, as defined in the [subject tracker](../subjects.md#the-audiences) · **Date:** 2026-10-14 · **Session:** `claude/matter-subject-research`, [PR #324](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/324).

**Research and proposals only. Nothing built.** Recommend **Melt and Freeze** first: warm and cool a tray of things, watch them change, and learn which changes can be undone. Follow with **Stir and See**, a mixing game where a spoon decides whether something dissolves, settles or floats, and an older **Particle Lens** mode that shows the same changes as moving dots. Keep floating and sinking in Sink or Float, fractions of food in Pet Kitchen and colour mixing in Color Garden. These are recommendations for docs-first definitions, not registrations, zones or a scheduled build. [Roadmap packets](RECOMMENDATIONS.md#matter-recommendations).

**Follow-up requested 2026-10-16:** a separate [chemistry deep-research session](chemistry.md) will investigate atoms/molecules, reactions/conservation and older extensions, with SpaceChem as a requested puzzle reference. That session was researched on 2026-10-17 (Bond Beads first; reactions stay review-gated in Weigh It and Stir and See); this study remains the starting point for phase changes and mixtures.

## Anchors opened at their source

These are curriculum destinations, not developmental deadlines, and none shows that a screen game teaches a standard. The NGSS pages opened for this session gave the performance expectation and, where present, its clarification statement and boundary; several pages carry no boundary text, so none is claimed for them.

| Official anchor | What this study takes from it | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [2-PS1-1](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/2-ps1-1-matter-and-its-interactions) | Describe and classify materials by observable properties such as colour, texture, hardness and flexibility | A sorting mode, not a vocabulary quiz; properties are seen or felt, not measured |
| [2-PS1-3](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/2-ps1-3-matter-and-its-interactions) | A thing made of a few pieces can be taken apart and rebuilt into something new | Already covered by blocks and Bug Builder; no new game for it |
| [2-PS1-4](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/2-ps1-4-matter-and-its-interactions) | Argue with evidence that some heating and cooling changes can be reversed and some cannot. The clarification gives water and butter as reversible, and cooking an egg, freezing a leaf and heating paper as not | The first game's spine. Choose pretend examples that need no fire, no hot stove and no real food advice |
| [5-PS1-2](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ps1-2-matter-and-its-interactions) | Measure and graph to show total weight is unchanged by heating, cooling or mixing; the boundary excludes the difference between mass and weight | The scale level must be a sealed jar. | [5-PS1-4](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ps1-4-matter-and-its-interactions) | Investigate whether mixing two or more substances makes a new substance (the page shows no clarification) | Dissolving, settling and a fizz are different outcomes; the first mixing slice shows only dissolve, settle and float |
| [MS-PS1-2](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ps1-2-matter-and-its-interactions) | Compare properties before and after to decide whether a reaction happened; the boundary lists density, melting point, boiling point, solubility, flammability and odour | The grown-up mystery puzzle may use melting point, solubility and density. Flammability and odour stay out of a children's game |
| [MS-PS1-4](https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ps1-4-matter-and-its-interactions) | Model how particle motion, temperature and state change when thermal energy is added or removed. The emphasis is qualitative, with solids, liquids and gases; water, carbon dioxide and helium are the examples | Dots are a model and not a picture. Never draw "cold" as a substance that flows in |

**Precedents found.** [PhET States of Matter: Basics](https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/states-of-matter-basics) lets a student heat, cool and compress atoms and molecules and watch them change phase. PhET's own launch post describes it as designed for a middle-school audience, and a New York State listing tags it for grades 4 to 7 (the PhET page itself returned only navigation text, so this comes from [search summaries](https://blogs.ubc.ca/dennisprattetec533/?p=65) and was not read at source). **Toca Lab: Elements** is the cautionary precedent: reviewers praise its nothing-to-read, drag-and-poke play, and [Common Sense Education](https://www.commonsense.org/education/reviews/toca-lab-elements) says children will not learn detailed science from it. [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2014-01-07-toca-lab-offers-completely-unscientific-unrealistic-fun.html) reports user reviews that it could mislead children into thinking freezing an element changes it into another. The lesson for the island is that a warm-and-cool toy must keep a thing the same substance when its state changes. Doodle God-style discovery (combine anything with anything) is the genre to avoid for the same reason: it rewards arbitrary recipes.

## What people can do, and what is still an inference

- **Sourced:** the standards above say what a school sequence asks of children. The island's own Sink or Float and Ramp Race already show that a prediction can be unscored and a fair test can be taught with two lanes.
- **Inference, not evidence:** that a one-year-old will notice a puddle after a cube; that a 4-year-old can predict "will it melt?"; that a 9-year-old will enjoy a scale puzzle; that a 12-year-old will choose a particle toy over the grown-up shelf. These are design bets for the tracker's open question about a middle band, to be tested with a person or device.
- **Not established:** any claim that the particle view produces a correct mental model. Research on particle diagrams commonly finds learners drawing particles that expand, melt or take on the substance's colour; this was not opened for this session, so treat it as a known risk to check (the model must say "the dots move faster" and never "the dots melt").

**Unplugged ideas for the grown-ups' page, not yet written as cards.** Race two ice cubes, one in sun and one in shade. Freeze a tray and watch it melt in the bath. Sort things in a drawer by soft, hard, bendy and see-through. Stir a spoon of sugar and a spoon of sand into two clear cups and see which one vanishes. Each of these needs a reviewer before it becomes a printed card, see the safety note.

**Safety flag (record, do not resolve).** Ice cubes are a choking risk for the youngest children, hot water scalds, and mixing household products can be dangerous. A card must never suggest warming a drink or mixing cleaning products, and food-related lines wait for the named reviewer in [subjects.md](../subjects.md#decisions-for-the-developer). The in-game sun, ice and fizz are pretend and need no such advice.

## What fits

Matter is a strong fit for the island: the action is a verb (warm, cool, stir), the result is visible at once, and it can be replayed. It is a poor fit for any "predict the chemical product" quiz and for the periodic table (a real subject, but a recall exercise rather than play). Chemistry beyond mixing and phase changes (reaction types, balancing) is out of scope until a science reviewer is named.

## A first game's ladder: Melt and Freeze

Working ID `melt-freeze`, **not registered**. The decision is **which tool, how much, and whether the change can be undone**. It must be checked again against Sink or Float, Pet Kitchen and the weather study's Drop's Journey before it is frozen.

### Model

Each authored substance (ice, butter, chocolate, bread, cake batter, popcorn kernels, a stone, a spoon, a candle) has a few discrete states, a heat step that changes it and a **reversible** flag. A dial has three settings: off, warm and hot. Warm melts ice and butter; hot melts chocolate and also makes bread toast. A cool tray (a snowflake) removes heat. A toast or a popped kernel never goes back, and the voice says so ("It can't un-toast. Try ice."). Every state change is authored, deterministic and rule-tested. The same substance stays the same substance in every state.

### Levels

| Level | Band | Action | Misses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | lap, toddler | Tap the sun: a cube becomes a puddle. Tap the snowflake: it freezes | None possible |
| 2 | lap, toddler | Three things in turn (ice, butter, a stone); the voice names what happens | None |
| 3 | toddler, preschool | "Make the ice into water": choose sun or snowflake | A wrong tool shrugs and shows the other |
| 4 | preschool | A tray of four things; warm all of them; some change, some do not. A discovery is filed for each thing that changed | Free exploration, none |
| 5 | preschool, pre-K | "Will it melt?" The child guesses first (unscored), then warms | Predictions never count |
| 6 | pre-K, school | Put the changed thing back: ice and butter freeze; toast and a popped kernel do not. Sort into "can go back" and "can't", testing before filing | A wrong sort is tested, not scolded |
| 7 | school | The three states of one substance: make a cloud, a puddle, and ice, in a named order | An out-of-order step is shown, then undone |
| 8 | school, upper school | Sealed jar on a scale: predict, then melt; the scale stays level | Predictions never count |
| 9 | school, upper school | A dial and a row of substances: choose a heat that melts only the chocolate, not the ice | A wrong dial is shown and named |
| 10 | upper school, middle (future) | Particle Lens: match a scene to its dots, then predict the picture after heat is added | Wrong match tested against the scene |
| 11 | middle, grown-up | Plan a sequence to reach a target state with spare tools; or read a melting-point clue to name a mystery | Counted as in other puzzles |

**Hint ladder** follows the island rule: a gentle spoken hint after a miss, a glow after two, and a supported finish. **Accounting.** Only a wrong sort or a wrong dial counts as a miss; exploring, predictions and undo never do. A level finished with a supported ending is not evidence of independent mastery.

## Experience and presentation

The sun, the snowflake and the dial are big, code-drawn Pixi shapes at least 100 units wide. Objects are drawn as pictures, never words; a puddle, a steam puff and an ice cube must be readable at sticker size. Sounds: a drip, a soft chime when a thing melts, a crystal chime when it freezes, using pentatonic steps. Steam is drawn as a **puff with a small caption in the grown-up card**, never a visible stream of "vapour", matching the weather study's limit (the visible cloud is tiny drops). The music is the island's loop. The pet sits bottom left and watches, repeating the instruction. A portrait layout stacks the tray above the dial. No hot object is ever drawn near the pet's hands, and no flame appears.

## Decisions for the developer

- **Is Melt and Freeze a new game or a mode of Sink or Float?** Lean: a new game, because the action (warm and cool) and learning (states and reversibility) differ from density. Recheck at definition.
- **Where do the particle levels go?** Top of this game (lean) or a separate older game once a middle band exists.
- **Who reviews the science and the safety lines?** A named science reviewer for the dot picture and steam, and the food and hot-water reviewer already asked for in the tracker, before any card is printed.
- **Does the grown-up Substance Mystery belong in Lantern Lake?** Decide after Forecast Detective's first case.
