# Number structure: equal rows, what is left over, the numbers that make only one row, and where place value stops

**Subject:** `number-structure` · **Audiences:** L, T, P, K, S, U, M and G, as defined in the [subject tracker](../subjects.md#the-audiences) · **Requested:** the tracker's [follow-up additions](../subjects.md#additional-gaps-from-the-follow-up-review) row (equal groups and division, remainders, factors, multiples, primes and common factors; a place-value and decimal progression audit) · **Researched:** 2026-10-31 · **Session:** `claude/research-number-structure`, [PR #1060](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/1060).

**Research and proposals only; nothing here is built.** The island already counts equal groups (Little Helpers 6, Lasso Loops' *make 3 groups of 4*), shares with a leftover (Monster Munch 8), asks *how many friends can have two each* (Monster Munch 11, the quotative meaning of division) and reads arrays both ways (Orchard Rows 1–3). What it never asks is the next question about a number's **structure**: *in how many ways* can a number be arranged in equal rows, which numbers make *only one* row, what a leftover *means* for the answer, and where two counting-by patterns *meet*. The place-value audit below finds a second, plainer gap: place value stops at tens and ones below 40, and there is **no hundreds and no decimal** anywhere on the island.

**Recommendation.** Define **Orchard Rows 4–6** first (NS1), extending the built game rather than adding one: a basket of saplings, and she chooses **how many rows** to plant them in. The saplings are dealt into the rows one at a time, so every row gets the same, and a row count that does not divide the number leaves the **leftover saplings in the basket**, where she can see them. Level 4 *Equal rows* asks for any arrangement with every row full (several right answers); level 5 *Every way* fills a page of orchard shapes until every factor pair of the number is found (a turned orchard is the same shape, the turn she learned at level 2); level 6 *Only one long row* sets two or three baskets side by side and asks which number can only be planted in one long row, and the pet names it a **prime** number on the grown-up card and once in speech (decision 3). The rows-of-trees picture is the same picture the game already draws, and every row count is a free experiment; only planting with saplings still in the basket counts.

## What people can do, and what remains uncertain

- **Two meanings of division, both from early on.** Squire and Bryant (2002) are cited (MERGA 2023 and Downton 2009, **search summaries; the paper and both citing papers not opened**) as concluding that both sharing (*partitive*: how many in each share) and grouping (*quotitive*: how many shares) experiences matter for young children, that teaching usually starts with sharing, and that the inverse relation (more shares, smaller shares) is hard. **Consequence:** the island already has both meanings at 5–8 (Monster Munch 7–8 and 11); Orchard Rows' row count asks the unknown factor, a third reading of the same structure, rather than re-teaching either.
- **Remainders are answered without being interpreted.** The well-known NAEP bus item, in which many students answered "31 remainder 12" buses, and Silver, Shapiro and Deutsch's 1993 *JRME* study of sense-making with remainders are **recalled, not opened** (searches found only classroom material describing the bus problem). Keep them marked recalled; the defining session for NS2 opens them or leaves them out. **Consequence:** a leftover is always a thing on screen that has to go somewhere (another box, the pet, back to the basket), never a number after an "r".
- **Factors and primes are grade 4 to 5 work** in both the US and England sources above (about 9–10). No developmental study of when children first see that some numbers make only one rectangle was found; recorded as a gap. The orchard picture makes the idea visible earlier, so *Equal rows* sits at about 7–8 and *Only one long row* at the top of `school` with an "about 9 and up" note.
- **Decimals: "longer is larger".** The University of Melbourne decimals project's [incidence table](https://extranet.education.unimelb.edu.au/SME/TNMY/Decimals/Decimals/tests/incidenc.htm) (opened; Steinle and Stacey's sample of Australian students, Years 4 to 10) reports longer-is-larger thinking (2.352 judged bigger than 2.4) in 74% of Year 4, 46% of Year 5 and 33% of Year 6, falling to 5% by Years 9–10, and shorter-is-larger rising from 9% to a peak of 19% in Year 6: "Most of the younger students think like this, but this misconception quickly disappears." **Consequence:** a decimal rung (NS7) compares amounts the child can see filling, never digit strings alone, and offers the longer-digit answer as the tempting wrong choice with an explanation.

## On the island today, and the place-value audit

- **Built equal groups and division:** Little Helpers 6 (a team in equal groups, a skip count when wrong), Lasso Loops' *make 3 groups of 4*, Monster Munch 7–8 (fair shares, then a leftover that goes to the pet), 11 (*How many friends?*, two each) and 12 (two kinds), Orchard Rows 1–3 (2×3 to 5×5 arrays, rows then columns, a missing row), Picture Graph's key of two (each block two critters). Each is in [GAMES.md](../../GAMES.md).
- **Built multiples, faintly:** Bubble Pop 14 counts by tens and fives; Frog Hop's landmark flowers sit on 5, 10, 15 and 20. Nothing asks which numbers are in two counts at once.
- **Notebook rows:** *Number Munch Grid* (munch the numbers that fit a rule: even, then multiples; [IDEAS](../../IDEAS.md)) and *Pen Planner* (Shikaku: fence a grid into rectangles, each holding its area; arrays and multiplication facts). The [area study](area-volume-space.md) owns area and the unknown side; this study owns *how many rectangles a number makes*, the arrangement rather than the measure.
- **Place-value audit (counted from GAMES.md, 2026-10-31):** ones and tens appear in Duck Pond's make ten, Frog Hop 6–8 (a line to 20, then splitting through ten), Bubble Pop 14 (two-digit counting by tens) and Lasso Loops 4–6 (jars of ten and loose fireflies, totals 21–39, with swapped tens and ones offered as a wrong choice). **Nothing reaches 100**: no bundle of ten tens, no three-digit number, no hundreds. **No decimal or decimal fraction appears in any game, study or definition**; the fractions study stops at fraction notation and the money and measurement studies do not use decimal notation. The roadmap's earlier note that "place value past 39 is a known gap" ([IDEAS](../../IDEAS.md)) still holds.
- **Audiences already served:** K and S have equal groups and arrays; U and M have nothing on factors, primes, multiples, remainders as decisions, hundreds or decimals.

## What fits

- **The arrangement is the learning.** A fixed basket and a free choice of rows makes divisibility something she sees (the basket empties, or some saplings are left in it) rather than something she is told. Many right answers at level 4; a complete set at level 5; one special kind at level 6.
- **Extend Orchard Rows.** Its rows, its 100-unit row bands, its turned orchard and its counting by rows are exactly this action; a new game would draw the same orchard. Orchard Rows' own game deep dive is still queued; the definition numbers levels 4–6 now and the dive numbers its own from 7, as Inchworm's did around a built level.
- **A leftover is a decision.** Lunch boxes for a picnic: how many boxes do we need for 14 cookies, four to a box? The leftover cookies need a box of their own (round up); how many full boxes can go to the friends (round down); how many are left for the pet (the remainder). That is Monster Munch's tray and dealing, so NS2 is a Monster Munch candidate handed to its dive.
- **Where counts meet.** Two frogs hop along the lily pads by 3 and by 4 from 0; on which pad do they first land together? A Frog Hop mode (NS3), U.
- **Does not fit:** multiplication-table drills, timed fact recall, any score for knowing primes by heart, the word *factor* as a thing she must read, and long division as a procedure (6.NS.B.2).

## A first game's ladder: Orchard Rows 4–6

The scene is the built orchard. A basket of saplings sits beside it; above the basket a large picture of the row count (a stack of short bars, one per row, with up and down arrows, each at least 100 units) changes the plan. Changing the row count deals the saplings at once into that many equal rows, one at a time down the rows, and any that cannot make another full round stay in the basket, so she sees the leftover before planting; **Plant** grows the trees (the basket must be empty). The numbers are spoken and shown as tree pictures; no numeral has to be read.

| Level | First round | What she decides | Mistakes and hints | Rule test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **4 · Equal rows** · school | 12 saplings, the plan opening at 5 rows (5 rows of 2, two saplings left in the basket); then 15, 16, 18 or 20 in later rounds | How many rows makes every row full | Changing the row count is free. Plant with saplings left in the basket: they wobble, the pet says "Two are left in the basket. Every sapling needs a place in a row", one miss; two misses ring every row count that works (one hint). Any equal arrangement with at least two rows and two in a row is right | For every basket, the equal row counts are exactly its divisors from 2 to N/2; the opening row count is never one of them; every basket has at least two right answers |
| **5 · Every way** · school | 12 saplings and a page of empty shape frames, one per factor pair (1 and 12, 2 and 6, 3 and 4) | Which row counts she has not tried yet | Plant with saplings left over is one miss, as at 4. A shape already found replays its frame's sparkle and is free ("We have that one. Turn it and it's the same orchard"). After two misses the row counts still missing ring (one hint). Done when every frame is filled | Frames are the factor pairs; one row and N rows are the same pair as turned; frames are never more than four (N to 24) |
| **6 · Only one long row** · school, "about 9 and up" | Three baskets (for example 12, 13 and 15); try any of them | Which basket can only be planted in one long row | Trying is free and every arrangement is shown. Tapping a basket that makes another shape: the pet plants that shape beside it, "This one makes rows of 3", one miss; two misses glow the one-row basket (one hint). Right: "13 only makes one long row. Numbers like that are called prime" | Exactly one basket in each set is prime; the others each have at least one equal shape; primes from 5 to 23 |

Levels 4–6 are the first slice. **Cost:** M. A row-count control, the equal deal with its leftover, the shape page and the prime basket set; the bot reads the rule in `logic.ts` (a divisor at 4, the next missing pair at 5, the prime basket at 6). No new save field; nothing kept.

## Experience and presentation

- **The first unsupported action** is pressing the up arrow and watching the saplings jump into a new shape; the saplings left in the basket are the consequence she sees before anything counts.
- **Touch-down feedback:** the arrow darkens and the saplings hop as they move; Plant's trees grow row by row, then the familiar count by rows.
- **Replay variety:** a seeded basket from a pool per level, the sapling kind (apple, pear, plum) and which end the basket stands.
- **Off-screen:** the grouping card's spoons.

## Decisions for the developer

Under the [October 23 standing rules](../../archive/DECISIONS-2026-10-23.md#standing-rules) the defining session adopts each lean; only the person checks wait.

1. **Identity.** Orchard Rows levels 4–6 rather than a new game: the action is still arranging and counting trees in rows. **Lean: extend Orchard Rows**; the queued Orchard Rows game dive numbers its own levels from 7.
2. **Basket sizes.** Up to 24, so every factor pair fits on the orchard at the game's current tree spacing (a single row of 24 trees is pictures, never a target). **Lean: 12 to 24.**
3. **Words.** "Equal rows", "only one long row" and "the same shape turned" in speech; *factor*, *multiple* and *composite* on the grown-up card only; *prime* spoken once, at the level 6 answer. **Lean: as stated.**
4. **The leftover's home.** Lunch boxes go to Monster Munch's dive rather than Pet Kitchen, which keeps its fractions and recipes. **Lean: Monster Munch.**
5. **Hundreds and decimals.** Sketches only until a definition picks a home game (Lasso Loops' jars could grow crates; a decimal model may belong to the fractions study). **Lean: sketch.**

**Device and person checks (for DEVICE-CHECKS once built):** does a seven-year-old use the leftover saplings to choose the next row count, or press arrows at random; does "every way" feel like a collection to complete or like a test; does the word *prime* stick, or does "only one long row" carry the idea on its own?
