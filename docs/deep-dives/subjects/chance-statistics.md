# Chance and statistics: what happens most often

**Subject:** `chance-statistics` · **Audiences:** L, T, P, K, S, U, M and G, as defined in the [subject tracker](../subjects.md#the-audiences) · **Requested:** 2026-10-16 by the [standards audit](standards-audit.md) (its Spinner Fair seed) · **Researched:** 2026-10-19 · **Session:** `claude/subject-chance-statistics-finish`, [PR #623](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/623), resuming the claim in [PR #605](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/605).

**Research and proposals only. Nothing built.** This study sits beside three neighbours and does not repeat them: [Picture Graph](../../GAMES.md#picture-graph) owns building and reading a bar graph of things she can count (the Common Core K–3 data standards and England's Year 2–4 statistics); the [data and AI literacy study's Sample Safari](data-ai-literacy.md#sample-safari-a-bounded-older-investigation) owns *where to look* before claiming something about a whole meadow (7.SP.A sampling as evidence); the [ratio study](ratio-proportion.md) owns "for every" and the ratio table. This study owns **chance**: a spin, a roll or a draw whose result nobody can know in advance; which of two spinners gives the better chance; sure, maybe and no way; fair and unfair; what a long run of spins settles toward; why some sums of two dice come up more often than others; and the one number that stands for many (the mean) with how far they spread (the range). It also states a rule for the whole island: **chance is a subject here, never a reward mechanic.** Stickers, unlocks, creatures and anything she keeps stay predictable, as [AGENTS](../../../AGENTS.md#rules-for-every-activity) and the [idea notebook's boundaries](../../IDEAS.md#family-experience-review-2026-10-12) already require ("no paid or opaque random rewards, or gacha collection pressure").

## What people can do, and what remains uncertain

- **Sourced (infants):** [Xu and Garcia, *Intuitive statistics by 8-month-old infants*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2278207) (PNAS 105(13), 2008; the PMC record reached through a search excerpt, the paper not read): across six experiments, eight-month-olds looked longer when a sample drawn from a box did not match the box's contents, and could go the other way, from a whole small population to the sample it should give. [Denison and Xu (2010)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.16059) (reported there through a later modelling paper, **secondary**, the primary not opened): 12-to-14-month-olds crawled to the cup holding a lollipop drawn from the jar that was mostly their preferred colour (4:1 against 1:4), about 78 per cent of them. So a one-year-old already expects *more of this colour, more likely this colour*. The lap and toddler answers below rest on this, and on nothing about what she would *do* with a spinner.
- **Sourced (the review):** by about age ten many children understand that randomness is linked to fairness and that randomising can make an allocation fair; **children and adults both find independence hard** ("after a run of heads, tails is more likely": negative recency); children have particular difficulty with a sample space where some outcomes are equally likely and others not (two dice: 36 equally likely outcomes but only 11 sums, not equally likely); **there is no research on children's ability to build an exhaustive list of outcomes**; children understand proportions as **ratios before fractions**, so probability may be easier introduced as ratios ("3 blue out of 4" before "3/4"); conditional reasoning goes better with **absolute counts** than decimals; and in judging correlation, intuitive reasoners show confirmation bias. The Foundation was funding a controlled intervention study with 9-to-10-year-olds; its result was not found this session.
- **Sourced (classroom practice, through search excerpts, pages not opened):** a [Vanderbilt kindergarten class](https://pty.vanderbilt.edu/2018/02/05/spring-savy-2018-day-2-probability-and-prediction-kindergarten/) played a fair four-section spinner, then designed **unfair spinners to favour a number**, and "all of the students achieved their desired rigging results", with a discussion of possible against likely; the [ASA's *Spinners at the School Carnival*](https://www.amstat.org/asa/files/docs/stew/SpinnersattheSchoolCarnival(UnequalSections).docx) has pairs predict, spin an unequal spinner and tally; [HowToSmile's *Fair spinners*](https://www.howtosmile.org/resource/fair-spinners) (ages 8–14) plays a spinner that "isn't very fair", then has the learner design a fair one and play again; a [Twinkl](https://www.twinkl.com.mt/resource/spinner-fun-fair-vs-unfair-au-n-1744759445) sheet compares a fair and an unfair spinner at Year 2; a [New York lesson](https://www.nylearns.org/module/content/pyb/resources/9883/view.ashx) has students design a carnival game with outcomes of 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and 1/8 and write rules that give both players an equal chance. **Design, then play, then compare** is the shape every one of these shares.
- **Recalled, not opened:** [Piaget and Inhelder, *The Origin of the Idea of Chance in Children*](https://www.routledge.com/The-Origin-of-the-Idea-of-Chance-in-Children/Lombard-Inhelder/p/book/9781848724549) (1951; English 1975; only bibliographic pages surfaced) treat chance as what cannot be deduced, understood in stages through to combinatorial reasoning in adolescence; Fischbein's "primary intuitions" of probability (recalled, no page found). A definition that quotes either must open it.
- **Inference, not evidence:** that a two-year-old enjoys tapping a spinner and watching a creature hop; that a four-year-old reads "which came up most" off the bars she made; that a seven-year-old chooses the spinner with more blue *and* accepts that blue can still lose; that a ten-year-old finds a settling bar chart satisfying; that a twelve-year-old chooses to rig a spinner or to argue with the pet about "red is due". Device and person checks.
- **Not established:** that spinning on a screen produces an idea of chance that transfers to dice, draws or weather; that any level reduces the gambler's fallacy, which the review says adults keep. Rule tests can prove a spinner's shares and a grid's counts, not the learning.

## What fits

- **Fits as a new game: Spinner Fair.** The action (tap a big spinner, watch a creature hop and a block drop on its bar; then pick the better spinner, say sure / maybe / no way, make a spinner fair, rig one on purpose, and watch a long run settle) exists nowhere on the island: Picture Graph counts what is already there, Egg Catch predicts what the gates decide. The learning is specific and visible: *more of a colour means a better chance, and a better chance can still lose; a fair spinner gives everyone the same share; a long run settles toward the shares.* It passes the "fun action with a clear learning purpose" test only if **the spinner's picture decides every miss and the spin itself never does**, so the first thing a definition must state is the accounting rule below.
- **Fits as modes:** Peg Garden *pearl bins* (which bin fills most; through its dive, which widens its bands); Lemonade Stand *a typical day* (the week's mean; through a dive it does not yet have); the two-dice grid is Spinner Fair's own level, not Memory Match's dots. Lucky Columns for grown-ups is a new Lantern Lake puzzle only if the lake's capacity test passes (twelve games today, and the ratio study's Mixing Room wants the same thirteenth place); otherwise it is a couch face-off or waits.
- **Does not fit:** gambling of any kind, bets, stakes, winnings or a bank; loot boxes, mystery eggs, random creature drops, a daily spin or any reward whose odds are a mechanic; fortune telling and "lucky" objects; a race the child loses on a spin; the gambler's fallacy taught as a trick question; median, quartiles, box plots, trees and random-digit simulation below 11–14; a spinner whose sections are hidden before upper school; comparing two populations (7.SP.B); anything that reads a real random source (the device's sensors, the clock) as the lesson.

### The accounting rule, stated once

- **A spin, roll or draw is never a miss.** Its result is shown and recorded; the pet names it; nothing she chose is judged by it.
- **A prediction is never a miss** (the island's existing rule), and the pet says afterwards what the prediction got right ("you picked the spinner with more blue, so blue had the better chance; this time it landed on red: that's chance").
- **A miss comes only from a question the picture decides:** which bar is tallest after the spins stop; which of two spinners has more of the wanted colour; whether a result is sure, maybe or no way on *this* spinner; whether a spinner is fair (equal sections); which sum has the most squares in the grid; how tall the levelled bars are. Two misses glow the answer, as everywhere.
- **"About how many" is a band.** At the long-run level the accepted answer is a range (for a half-blue spinner and 100 spins, 40 to 60), chosen so that the seeded run lands inside it almost always; a run that falls outside is announced as unusual and the pet spins 100 more. Only an answer outside the band is a miss.
- **A run is seeded** (`ctx.rng`), so a round replays the same and a rule test can check that each level's seeds produce the run its question needs (a unique tallest bar at level 2, a run inside the band at level 6). Fairness and chance are properties of the *picture*, which the tests check exactly; the run is checked only for the properties the question needs.
- **Help she asks for** (the pause sheet's Show a hint, or the `?` card) highlights the larger section, the tallest bar or the longest row of the grid and counts one hint; nothing about a spin's outcome is ever a hint.

## A first game's ladder: Spinner Fair

A big spinner (about 420 logical units across) with a pointer at the top, sections coloured **and** marked with a creature so colour is never the only cue; below it, Picture Graph's bars, one per section. Tapping the spinner starts a seeded spin (`onTap`; the spin lasts about two seconds with clicks and slows to its result, a pentatonic chime on landing); the creature of the winning section hops and a block drops onto its bar. The spinner editor at level 5 uses fixed wedges (six on a two- or three-creature spinner, eight at level 9) dragged between sections with `src/engine/drag.ts`; a wedge can only move to a neighbour's section, so the wheel is always whole. Spoken lines through `ctx.instruct` and `ctx.say`; the sticker is one per round, including with help.

## Experience and presentation

The spinner is the toy: big, bright, with a creature on every section, and a spin that is satisfying on its own (clicks that slow, a bounce at the end). The bars underneath are Picture Graph's, so a child who has built graphs recognizes them. Nothing counts down, nothing is lost on a spin, and the pet celebrates every result ("Sprout again!"). Colour names and "sure, maybe, no way" are spoken; the card's words are labels. The two-dice grid at level 7 appears a row at a time with its sums spoken, so it is seen being built rather than read. Fast-forward at level 6 is a visible button that runs the spins quickly with the bars growing, never a jump to a finished chart. Sound: a tick per section passed, a pentatonic chime on landing, a short fanfare when a race finishes; the island's music keeps playing.

## Fit with the creature-world rework

Her creatures are the sections: whichever she owns can stand on the wheel, and the racing pair at level 5 is two of hers. A race won by a spin is a cheer, never a possession, a rank or a loss. **Chance is a subject, not a reward:** no creature, egg, sticker, unlock or home item may arrive by a spin or a draw, and no "lucky" mechanic may enter the collection, which keeps the [rework brief's](../../rework/README.md) permanent unlocks predictable. A kept thing from this game is the two-dice chart in the journal (a discovery, filed once level 7's grid is complete). Spinner Fair 5's fair spinner could be a toy in the creature home later; this study does not define it.
