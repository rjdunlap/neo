# Oceans: the shore, the tide and the water column

**Subject:** `oceans` · **Audiences:** L, T, P, K, S, U, M and G as [defined in the tracker](../subjects.md#the-audiences) · **Date:** 2026-10-23 · **Session:** `claude/confident-gates-lvnhu9`, [PR #849](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/849). The [zoo-animals study](zoo-animals.md) hands this session ocean habitats, depth zones and tides, and asks that the Tide Pools and Reef kit be built from this plan, "not twice".

**Research and proposals only.** Recommend one new identity, **Tide Line**: the sea comes in and goes out, again and again, and she uses *evidence on the shore* (the line of seaweed and shells the last tide left) to decide where things stay dry, then reads the sea's rise and fall as a pattern, then as a chart. Tides are the ocean idea a child can watch and act on from lap age, nothing else on the island teaches them, and the weather-water, space and earth-materials studies all leave them unowned. Everything else extends what exists. **Salty or Fresh** goes to Sink or Float as a mode. **Beach Day Pack-up** goes to Second Chance Shed, and a **Beach Tally** to Picture Graph. A **Rock Pool** scene goes to Photo Safari and **shore zones** to Habitat Helpers. A depth-and-light game (**Down We Go**), an upper-school currents game (**Drift Cards**) and a grown-up **Tide Tables** puzzle are recorded as later candidates. No code, art, audio or registry change is made here.

## What people can do, and what remains uncertain

- **Evidence about young children and the ocean is thin.** One search for developmental research on preschoolers' ocean understanding found programme descriptions, not studies. One article noted "little research" on benefits of beach learning in early childhood settings (an Australian "beach kinder" of three to five hours a week); this is from a search summary. So the age ladder below rests on the island's own conventions (one goal, one tool; a prediction followed by a visible result) and on what the sea shows, not on measured ages.
- **Tides are poorly understood well past childhood.** The only studies the search surfaced were of older learners and teachers: a Spanish study of secondary and university students (about two-thirds said the Moon's attraction, about 7.5% drew two opposite bulges, [*Enseñanza de las Ciencias*](https://revistas.uca.es/index.php/eureka/article/view/3365), summary only) and a Korean case study of eight elementary teachers who held three common misconceptions ([Jeju repository](https://oak.jejunu.ac.kr/handle/2020.oak/22288), summary only). I found no study of young children and tides. Consequence: Tide Line's early rungs claim only the **pattern** (in, out, in, out) and the **evidence** (a line on the shore). The cause (Moon and Sun) is a school-band explanation, behind a reviewer, and the game never draws two bulges.
- **A repeated cycle is something a child can read as a pattern.** The island's Pattern Train and Day by Day already teach sequence from toddler up; Tide Line borrows the *form* (alternation) without claiming a measured age.
- **Light fades with depth is a good small model.** The zone vocabulary varies by source: a K-2 lesson puts the sunlight zone at the surface to 200 m, twilight at 200 to 1,000 m and midnight at 1,000 to 4,000 m (summary only); the [Smithsonian pre-K ocean-zones lesson](https://naturalhistory.si.edu/sites/default/files/media/file/231215prek-group-exploration-lesson-3-ocean-zones-accessible.pdf) (read) says only about 25% of the ocean "sees the sunlight" (its top two zones, sunlight and twilight) and the midnight zone has none, while a National Geographic activity (summary only) puts 90% in the midnight zone. Because sources disagree, Down We Go uses **ordered stops with a light meter, never metres or percentages** until a reviewer picks one source.
- **Fear first backfires.** David Sobel's essay on ecophobia ([*Connect*, Nov/Dec 2007](https://brandwein.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/David_Sobel_Climate_Change_meets_Ecophobia.pdf), read) quotes a parent worried that hitting kids "(before 6th grade) too hard with environmental problems" leaves them overwhelmed, cites Finger (1993) that heavy environmental knowledge "can even be counter-productive", and says the path to responsible behavior runs from a sense of agency and control to knowledge and then action. The "no tragedies before fourth grade" phrase circulates but I only found it second-hand (an NSTA blog report), so I do not repeat it as Sobel's. Consequence: the sea is shown **well and bright**; any care action is something she does and sees work. No dying animals, no polluted water, no entangled creatures anywhere on the child's island.
- **Touch-pool etiquette is a real, simple, widely shared rule.** Aquariums teach touching with two wet fingers and not lifting animals off rocks (Seattle Aquarium and others, search summaries; a Leave No Trace tide-pool page credits Monterey Bay Aquarium for the advice). It is a **real-world instruction**, so it waits for a reviewer. The game's version is only that a quick poke makes a sea anemone close and it opens again by itself (a reviewer must confirm this is real behavior and fine to show; it is not asserted here).
- **Safety at the shore is its own topic.** Rip currents, rising tides that cut off a cove, slippery rocks and cold water are body-safety content; the [safety](../subjects.md#decisions-for-the-developer) row waits on a named reviewer. Nothing here drafts any of it, and Tide Line shows the sea rising only against a pail and a picnic, never against a person.

## What fits

- **Fits:** one large draggable "sea" (a big wave handle on the beach, not a slider the size of a fingertip), a few large things to place (pail, flag, picnic blanket, at least 100 units), and creatures that react gently. All feedback is visible: wet or dry, water up to a line, a pool uncovered.
- **Fits the rework:** the sea star, crab and shell friends can be **collectible**, with permanent unlocks earned by finishing a round or a project step, and a "rock pool" project could end with the creature visiting the pool it uncovered. The tide-pool creatures are habitat-kit art: the kit is a backdrop and a cast, not a zone.
- **Does not fit:** fishing or overfishing as a score; an aquarium to manage; any predation as the fun; oil or plastic spills as a scene; coral-bleaching tasks for young bands; scary deep-sea monsters; a timer on the tide; anything that shows the sea endangering a person or an animal; imported whale, gull or wave recordings (audio is synthesized); metres or percentages before a source is chosen.
- **Sound:** wave and gull sounds do not exist yet. They are short noise-based synthesis in `src/audio/sfx.ts` (a filtered noise sweep for a wave, a two-note whistle for a gull) and are justified by the first game that uses them.

**Identity test.** Tide Line earns a separate game only if its central decision is **where the sea will reach and when**, read from evidence on the shore or a chart. If the definition finds the rounds are only "move the water", they become a Sea Lift or Day by Day mode instead (decision 1).

## A first game's ladder: Tide Line

### The model, stated once

The sea level moves between a low and a high along a sloped beach, in a fixed repeating pattern. The high-water mark of the previous tide is left as a visible line of seaweed and shells (the wrack line). Anything above the line stays dry and anything below it can get wet. A tide pool is a hollow whose water is covered by the sea at high tide and shows at low tide. Nothing in the model names a cause. The tide generator uses one simple periodic curve (two highs and two lows per day) and says nothing about places where the pattern differs; that is a reviewer item for level 7 and Tide Tables.

### Proposed levels and bands

| Level | What she does | Bands (proposed) | Misses / help |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Come in, go out | Pull the big wave up the sand and let it slide back. Shells wobble, the sea star waves | lap, toddler | None: exploratory. The round finishes after the sea has come in and gone out twice. Replayable |
| 2 Stop at the line | Drag the pail above the seaweed line before the sea comes in | toddler, preschool | A pail below the line floats and the pet fetches it; counts as a miss, one retry. Glow after two |

### A rule test

`logic.ts` holds the tide curve and round plans. Tests: heights are monotone between each low and high; the wrack line equals the previous high; every level-2 and level-3 round has at least one dry position and at least one wet one; the level-4 missing picture is unique; level 5 has a window where the pool shows and one where it does not; every level-6 job has at least two valid windows and at least one clashing window; and the hint always points at a valid window or position. Rules are checked over 500 seeds per level, as in the other games.

### Cost

One new game with three to six levels: wave and gull synthesis (shared), four code-drawn creatures and a shore backdrop (shared with the habitat kit), a `logic.ts`, a how-to card and a ghost-finger bot (drag the wave, drag the pail). No save, navigation or shell change. Placement uses the existing land layout test.

## Experience and presentation

- **First unsupported action:** pulling the sea and watching the whole shore answer.
- **Touch-down feedback:** foam and a soft whoosh on touch; the wave handle swells under a finger; each stop on the way has a pentatonic step.
- **Replay variety:** seeded beach shapes, three line heights, shuffled things to place, a different creature in the pool each time.
- **Pet clearance:** the bottom-left pet is kept clear; the pet sits beside the beach and fetches wet things.
- **Off-screen:** the reviewed Find the Line card. Nothing practical ships before review.
- **Presentation:** bright and calm; the sea never rises against a person; the creatures react with curiosity, never fear.
