# Ratio and proportion: for every one of these, so many of those

**Subject:** `ratio-proportion` · **Audiences:** L, T, P, K, S, U, M and G, as defined in the [subject tracker](../subjects.md#the-audiences) · **Requested:** 2026-10-16 by the [standards audit](standards-audit.md) (its Paint Mixer seed) · **Researched:** 2026-10-19 · **Session:** `claude/subject-ratio-proportion-finish`, [PR #616](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/616), resuming the claim in [PR #599](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/599).

**Research and proposals only. Nothing built.** This study sits beside two neighbours and does not repeat them: the [cooking study's Pet Kitchen extension packet](cooking-recipes.md#pet-kitchen-extension-packet) owns scaling a *recipe's ingredient amounts* (Measure and Remix), and the fractions study (`codex/fractions-subject-research`, [PR #480](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/480), unmerged) owns fractions as numbers. This study owns the **comparison between two quantities**: "for every one red, two yellow", the same mix at a different size, which of these is the same, a rate per one, a part per hundred, and whether a relationship is proportional at all.

**Recommendation.** Define **Paint Mixer** levels 1–4 first: a new game whose fun is pouring drops from big paint pots into a bucket until it is exactly the colour of a small sample, and then making *more* of that colour. Colour is the point: an equivalent ratio is the same colour, and the additive mistake (one more of each) is a visibly different colour, so the mix judges itself without a worksheet. Its `school` (6–8) opening is a copying and doubling puzzle that claims no ratio language; the ratio table, "for every" speech, any-amount answers, three-pot mixes, which-jar comparisons and per-hundred vats come at upper school and the 11–14 rung (6.RP.A.1–3, 7.RP.A.2). The grown-ups' form is **Mixing Room**, a Lantern Lake puzzle: reach a target colour exactly by pouring whole jars of known mixes, with the fewest jars as a par found by search. Lemonade Stand gets a **best buy** rate rung and Pixel Pictures a **scale factor** mode as later packets.

## What people can do, and what remains uncertain

- **Sourced:** [Boyer, Levine and Huttenlocher, *Development of proportional reasoning: where young children go wrong*](https://europepmc.org/abstract/MED/18793078) (Developmental Psychology 44(5), 2008; abstract read through Europe PMC, the paper not opened). Earlier work put proportional reasoning with **discrete** units at about 10–12, while children solve **continuous**-quantity proportion problems by about 6. Their two studies (kindergarten to fourth grade; then first and third grade) found children "performed significantly worse" when both the target and the choices were discrete, with errors "only when a numerical match was possible": "an overextension of numerical equivalence concepts to proportional equivalence problems". **This is the study's central design fact:** when the pieces can be counted, a 6–9-year-old matches counts instead of proportions. So the young rungs judge a mix by its **colour** (continuous), the counts appear only when the ratio idea is the lesson, and the game must make the count-matching answer *look wrong* rather than only say so.
- **Sourced (standards as proxies):** England puts integer ratio problems at Year 6 (10–11) and the full ratio apparatus at KS3 (11–14); Common Core puts ratio language and tables at grade 6 (11–12) and proportional relationships at grade 7 (12–13). Nothing below age 10 in either document is called a ratio; the precursors are equal groups, multiplication as "times as many" (4.OA.A.1, not opened this session) and scaling by simple fractions (England Year 5).
- **Recalled, not opened:** Piaget and Inhelder placed proportional reasoning in formal operations (about 11 and up); Lamon's work on ratio describes a build-up (additive) strategy that children use before multiplicative reasoning, and the common error of adding the same amount to both quantities. Both are recalled; a definition that quotes them must open them.
- **Inference, not evidence:** that a 6–8-year-old enjoys copying and doubling a colour; that a 9–12-year-old reads "same colour, bigger bucket" as the same ratio; that a 12–13-year-old chooses a which-jar deduction or a per-hundred vat; that parents enjoy whole-jar mixing. Device and person checks.
- **Not established:** that mixing paint produces ratio understanding that transfers to recipes, maps or prices. A matched colour proves a matched ratio inside the toy, nothing more. Rule tests can prove the toy consistent, not the learning.

## What fits

- **Fits as a new game: Paint Mixer.** The action (pour drops from big pots into a bucket until it matches, then make more of the same) exists nowhere on the island: Rainbow Fingers mixes two whole pours for a page, Pet Kitchen doubles fruit on a plate. The learning is specific and visible: *the same mix at any size is the same colour; one more of each is not*. It passes the "a fun action with a clear learning purpose" test only if the **colour judges the ratio**, so the first thing a definition must prove is the colour rule below.
- **Fits as modes:** Lemonade Stand best buy (U; a rate per cup); Pixel Pictures scale factor (S–U; England Year 6); Pet Kitchen's scaling stays in the cooking packet. Mixing Room for grown-ups is a new Lantern Lake puzzle only if the lake's capacity test passes; otherwise it is Paint Mixer's top level at 13+.
- **Does not fit:** ratio drills and flash-card "simplify 6:9"; speed-and-distance rates (no clock on the island, and constant speed needs one); interest, tax, tips and commission; decimal ratios (1:1.3) and fractional drops before 11–14; a mixing game with real paint or food instructions (reviewer); any "balance the taste" mode that judges a child's food choices.

### The colour rule, stated once

Paint Mixer is a toy model of mixing, and the game says what it leaves out.

- **Pots:** a small enumerated set, red, yellow, blue and white, each with a fixed colour, a **word** and a shape mark on the pot (dot, stripe, star, ring) so colour is never the only cue.
- **A mix** is the drop-weighted mean of the pots' colours in linear RGB (the same arithmetic Rainbow Fingers would use if its named recipes were replaced by a formula). The mean depends only on the *proportions* of the drops, so **equal ratios give exactly the same colour** by construction, and 2:3 and 3:5 differ by a small, visible step. A definition states the perceptual check: the smallest pair of authored ratios must differ by a visible margin on an iPad (device check), and a swatch comparison is always side by side.
- **Authored pairs:** red + yellow (orange), red + white (pink), yellow + white (cream), blue + white (sky), red + blue (purple). **Blue + yellow is not authored** until an art check: the RGB mean is olive, not the green a child expects, and a toy that says "that's green" would be lying. Three-pot mixes come from those pairs plus white.
- **The rule for Check:** two mixes match when their drop counts are in the same ratio (cross-products equal, as integers). The eye is the help; the count is the rule. A near colour is *not* accepted at levels 3 and up (Rainbow Fingers 12 owns near matches by eye).
- **Measured 2026-10-27 (the definition):** with the palette's pots and this mean, one more of each (1:2 against 2:3) is about 2 Oklab units apart (a just-noticeable step side by side; cream 1.1) and a whole step (1:2 against 1:1) about 5, so "visibly wrong" is not available from an honest mean. The [definition](../../definitions/paint-mixer.md#the-colour-rule-measured) keeps equal-ratio-same-colour as the reward and makes the **drop rows** (repeated copies of the sample's row, with gaps and loose drops visible) the on-screen judge; cream is withheld from levels 1–4 until the art check, and blue + yellow averages to grey.
- **Left out, and said so on the how-to card from level 5:** real pigments do not mix by averaging; paint thickness, drying and brand; the colour wheel; "more paint makes a stronger colour" (it does not, in this toy or in a well-mixed bucket). Colour words on the card are labels, not required reading.

## A first game's ladder: Paint Mixer

Working ID `paint-mixer`, **not registered**. The decision is **how many drops of each pot** make the sample's colour at the bucket's size. Overlap checked against Rainbow Fingers (mixing by eye, pages), Pet Kitchen (doubling fruit), Picture Graph (additive comparison) and the cooking packet (recipe amounts): only Paint Mixer compares two mixes and has an exact count rule.

**Interaction.** Pots at least 100 units across along the bottom right (the pet keeps the bottom left). A **sample jar** (small, its recipe shown as a row of drop icons per pot, a tape diagram; the colon label from level 3) and a **bucket** (large, a fill line when the level sets a size, its colour updating with every drop). Tap a pot: a drop flies into the bucket with a soft pentatonic plip and the colour changes at once. **Undo** takes the last drop back; **Tip out** empties the bucket (a glug; free); **Check** holds the bucket beside the sample. From level 3 a **ratio table** above the bucket shows a row per pot and a column per "batch" as she pours (1 | 2 | 3 over 2 | 4 | 6), filling only when the counts are in ratio, so the table is the proof and the colour is the feel. **Drop counts capped at 12 per bucket** so the drop icons stay readable at 100-unit pots in portrait with the pet clear; the definition audits this with `view` in both orientations, and level 7's vat of 100 shows a gauge, not 100 icons.

A tap plots a log's dots on a grid; a straight line through zero means proportional (7.RP.A.2.a) | Proportional or not | A wrong log is one miss with the odd row circled; two misses plot the dots |

**Accounting and help.** Pouring, undo, tip-out and opening a lid (level 6) are exploration; only a **Check** that doesn't match counts, once per Check. After a miss the pet names one fact ("Too red: for every 1 red there should be 2 yellow"); after two misses the pot the mix needs glows (or, with the bucket full and wrong, Tip out glows and the hint says the plan); a supported finish then pours the rest as a ghost and the round completes with its sticker. Level 2's "right colour, not full" and level 4's "the same way" are never misses. Assisted completion is not mastery evidence.

**Rule tests a definition must name** (by enumeration): every authored sample has a bucket size that is a multiple of its drop total, so every level is solvable with whole drops; two mixes match iff cross-products are equal; the mean-colour function gives identical colours for equal ratios and a stated minimum distance for the closest authored pair; every level-6 case has at least one matching and one non-matching jar and exactly one correct set; every level-7 vat and can size gives whole drops at the stated per-hundred; every level-8 case has exactly one proportional log; every hint names a pot, jar or row that some solution changes; all equivalent answers at level 4 are accepted; the hint after a full wrong bucket leads to a solvable state. These prove toy consistency, not ratio learning; the per-hundred and "proportional" wording wants a mathematics educator's read.

**Cost.** One M game across three build slices; no new engine, physics or persistence beyond journal entries and (if the rework takes it) a palette unlock.

## Experience and presentation

The first unsupported action is level 1's tap on a pot; touch-down squashes the pot and the drop leaves it at once, and the bucket's colour moves before the plip ends. The ghost finger is feasible: the same taps, with counts supplied by the solver, and a Check. Replay variety comes from seeded samples drawn from the authored pairs per level (sizes and pots vary, the rule does not), and from seeded jar sets at level 6 and logs at level 8. Sounds: a plip per drop rising a pentatonic step per batch completed, a glug on tip-out, a chime on a match; the island's loop plays. All instructions go through `ctx.instruct`; "for every" is spoken, counts and colon labels are pictures, and a colour word is never the only cue. The pet watches from the bottom left and holds the sample jar up on a hint. Off-screen: the conversation card "If we made twice as much, what would we need?" (any card that mixes paint, squash or dough is a reviewed cooking-study card and waits for that reviewer).
