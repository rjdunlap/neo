# Media literacy: who made this, who saw it, and why it was made

**Subject:** `media-literacy` · **Audiences:** L, T, P, K, S, U, M and G, as defined in the [subject tracker](../subjects.md#the-audiences) · **Requested:** the [tracker](../subjects.md#languages-arts-thinking-and-the-natural-world) row (Real or made up? Who made this and why? Ads, edited pictures, checking a claim) · **Researched:** 2026-10-31 · **Session:** `claude/research-media-literacy`, [PR #1053](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/1053).

**Research and proposals; Notice Board 1–3 (ML1) was built 2026-11-01, the rest is not built.** Media literacy for children has four strands the tracker names: *authorship* (someone made this), *purpose* (why: to tell, or to sell), *techniques and omissions* (what the picture shows and leaves out, including edited pictures) and *checking a claim* (who saw it, what others say). The island already owns pieces of the third and fourth: [Photo Safari](../../GAMES.md#photo-safari) 8 (*What changed?*) compares a scene with her own photo, and 9 (*Is it true?*) judges a spoken claim against a photo; [Movie Night](../../GAMES.md#movie-night) shows that order and sound change what a viewer learns; [Not Always](../../definitions/not-always.md) (philosophy-reasoning, build ready) tests a claim against visible cards, and its proposed level 5 picks a *reason* over confidence. What no game has is the first two strands as actions, or the source question as an action: **a poster goes up on the village notice board, and she finds who made it, who actually saw what it says, and whether it is there to tell or to sell.** That is the gap this study fills.

**Recommendation.** Define **Notice Board** levels 1–3 first: a new game at a village notice board where island friends pin up picture posters (a pictured headline, a big picture and the maker's stamp in the corner; the pet reads each one aloud). Level 1 is *Who made it?*: match the poster's stamp to the friend wearing that badge (authorship, RI.K.6). Level 2 is *Who saw it?*: a poster says something happened; of three friends, one was there (shown in a little memory picture beside them), one was somewhere else and one only heard about it; tap the friend who saw it and their photo pops up and settles it, true or not (knowledge access, which the selective-trust evidence puts within reach at 4). Level 3, at school only, is *To tell or to sell?*: sort posters into *Here's what happened* and *Come and get some!*, then the maker says why they made it, without any judgement that selling is bad (persuasive intent, which the advertising evidence puts at about 7–8). Edited and cropped pictures go to [Photo Safari](../photo-safari.md) as two proposed modes, because the act there is the same as its levels 8 and 9. Everything is fictional and closed-world: island friends, island posters, no real brands, news, people, feeds, or anything about AI-made pictures on screen.

## Anchors opened at their source

Standards are curriculum destinations, not gates, and none shows that a game teaches them.

## What people can do, and what remains uncertain

| Age | What the evidence suggests | Source status and caveat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **About 1–2** | No evidence found for anything media-literacy-shaped at this age | A gap. Nothing on screen; the grown-ups' card has a lap line |
| **3–4** | Both 3- and 4-year-olds trusted a *knowledgeable* speaker over an *ignorant* one; only 4-year-olds reliably tracked a speaker's past *accuracy* (Koenig and Harris 2005, *Child Development* 76(6), 119 preschoolers, three experiments; Pasquini et al. 2007 and Corriveau and Harris 2009 report the same age pattern) | **Search summary** of the abstracts and replications; the papers were not opened. Supports *who saw it?* (knowledge access) at pre-K, pictured, not past-accuracy tracking |
| **Under about 7–8** | The APA Task Force on Advertising and Children (2004, Kunkel and colleagues) concluded that children below about 7–8 lack an understanding of advertising's persuasive intent; mature comprehension arises no earlier than 7–8 on average | **Search summary**: the APA report page was found but returned no text when fetched, so the wording comes from the search tool's summary of it and of APA's press release. Puts *to tell or to sell?* at school, never pre-K |
| **Under about 7, and after** | The AAP policy statement *Digital Advertising to Children* (Radesky and colleagues, *Pediatrics* 2020, doi 10.1542/peds.2020-1681) reports that children up to about 7 have limited ability to understand an advertiser's intent, and that **recognizing an ad does not reliably protect a child**, especially from appealing products | **Search summary** of secondary coverage and a republished PDF; sources disagree on the volume number (145(6) or 146(1)). The second finding is why no line may claim the game protects her from ads |
| **About 11–14** | In the Stanford History Education Group's 2016 assessments (7,804 responses, middle school to college), 82% of middle-school students failed to identify an item clearly marked "sponsored content" as an advertisement | **Opened** (the *American Educator* article). An assessment result, not a teaching result; it says the *who's behind it?* move is hard well into the teens, so level 5 sits at the top of school |
| **Teens and adults** | Playing *Bad News* (players write misinformation in a sandbox) lowered the rated reliability of fake headlines in a large self-selected online sample (Roozenbeek and van der Linden 2019); effects lasted up to 13 weeks with reminders (Basol et al. 2020, *Journal of Cognition*); a junior version for 8–10 exists, with no outcome data found | **Search summary.** Its design (the player *creates* deception) is left out for these bands; see below |

**Gaps.** No study found tests a touch game in which a young child picks the friend who saw something, or sorts telling from selling; no study found measures a family "who made this?" card at home. Both are person checks.

## What fits

- **Fits, as a new game:** a notice board of island posters whose maker, witness and purpose she finds. The action is on the *poster and its maker*: the stamp lights to the maker's badge, the witness's photo settles the claim, the maker says why they made it. No game has a maker or a poster; a level of Photo Safari would turn a camera game into a reading-the-board game (decision 1).
- **Fits, as Photo Safari modes (handed to its dive):** *Outside the frame* (a friend holds up a cropped photo and says "Duck is all alone!"; she widens it and judges with the thumbs, as level 9 does) and *Pasted in* (two friends' photos of one moment from two sides; one has something stuck on, a giant fish with no reflection; find it by comparing, as level 8 does).
- **Fits, off-screen:** the grown-ups' **"Who made this?" card** and the **hand-frame** co-play role.
- **Does not fit:** real brands, adverts, news, people, sites or feeds; a child *authoring* deception (Bad News's design); "fake", "scam" or "ads are bad" framing (*who made this and why* instead); any on-screen pressure to buy, or a seller who is a villain; a claim that the game protects her from adverts (the AAP finding); AI-made or "deepfake" pictures on screen (the grown-ups' card may mention them); a judged rule about which feeling an edit gives (declined above); phishing and strangers (the `safety` row); reasons versus confidence (Not Always 5).

## A first game's ladder: Notice Board

The scene: a big wooden notice board in the village with room for one poster at a time, large and centred; below it, two or three island friends each wearing a round badge with a simple glyph (a star, a leaf, a wave). A poster is a picture with a pictured headline and a stamp in its corner; the pet reads it aloud, and tapping the poster reads it again, free. Every poster is about something on the island, and everything that settles it is shown on screen.

| Level | First round | What she decides | Mistakes and hints | Rule test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1 · Who made it?** · pre-K 1, school 1 (preschool by pin, two friends) | A poster with a leaf stamp ("Come see my garden!"); three friends with star, leaf and wave badges. Three posters a round | Which friend made the poster | A friend whose badge does not match: they shake their head and hold up their badge ("Not me, my badge is a star"), one miss. Two misses: the matching badge glows (one hint). Success: the maker steps up, the poster gets a pin with their face, they say why ("I want you to see my flowers!") | Every poster's stamp matches exactly one friend's badge; badges are visually distinct at the smallest layout |
| **2 · Who saw it?** · pre-K 2, school 2 | A poster: "Bunny found a carrot as big as a tree!"; three friends, each with a small memory picture beside them: one at the garden (was there), one at the beach (elsewhere), one with an ear-and-whisper glyph (only heard it). Tap the one who saw it | Which friend can tell | Tapping elsewhere or heard-it: "I wasn't there, so I can't tell" or "I only heard about it", one miss. Two misses: the witness glows. Success: the witness's photo pops up beside the poster; sometimes it matches (a big carrot), sometimes not (an ordinary carrot) and the poster gets a gentle correction sticker ("A big carrot!") | Exactly one witness per poster; across a round, at least one poster true and one exaggerated; the memory pictures are unambiguous |
| **3 · To tell or to sell?** · school 3, about 7–8 | Two baskets under the board: *Here's what happened* (an eye) and *Come and get some!* (an open hand). Posters: "Lemonade, two shells!" (sell), "The ducklings hatched!" (tell) | Which basket a poster goes in | A wrong basket: the maker says what they want ("I want you to buy my lemonade"), one miss; two misses glow the basket. Success: the maker says why, never that selling is wrong | Every poster has one authored purpose with a clear pictured cue (a price, a stall) or none; no poster is both |
| **4 · What did it leave out?** · school 4 | "Biggest cups on the island!" with a giant cup; tap *Go and see* and the stand shows its real cups | Which real cup the stand sells, or the part of the poster that is not like the stand | A wrong pick: the pet points to the poster's picture and the stand side by side, one miss | The poster differs from the scene in exactly one pictured attribute |
| **5 · Who's behind it?** · school 5, about 10 and up | A poster from "The Happy Hat Club": "Fox's hats are the best!"; tap the club's name and walk to its door: it is Fox. A second poster about hats from Owl, who sells none | Whose poster to trust about hats, after finding who is behind each | Choosing before visiting is allowed and never a miss; choosing the maker's own club: "Fox made both. Who else could tell us?", one miss | Each case has one maker hidden behind a name and one disinterested witness |

Levels 1–3 are the first slice. **Cost:** M. A poster model, badges and memory pictures drawn from existing critter and prop art, the board and two baskets; no new save field; nothing kept. A bot finds the maker by stamp, the witness by memory picture and the basket by purpose from the model.

## Experience and presentation

- **The first unsupported action** is a tap on a friend, guided by the spoken poster and the stamp. Touch-down: the friend lifts and looks at the poster at once.
- **The payoff** is the maker's or witness's reaction and the board changing (a face pin, a photo beside the poster, a correction sticker), not a score. Nobody who made a wrong or exaggerated poster is shamed; they laugh ("It *felt* as big as a tree!").
- **Replay variety:** seeded posters over an authored set of makers, places and claims; badges and friends rotate.
- **Off-screen:** the "Who made this?" card and the hand frame.

## Fit with the creature-world rework

The makers and witnesses are friends from the current cast, and later her collected creatures could pin posters about what she did; nothing here depends on rework infrastructure, and the game keeps nothing (decision 6).

## Decisions for the developer

Under the [October 23 standing rules](../../archive/DECISIONS-2026-10-23.md#standing-rules) the defining session adopts each lean; only the person checks wait.

1. **Identity.** A new game, Notice Board, rather than Photo Safari levels: there is no camera act in finding a maker or a purpose. Edited and cropped pictures *are* Photo Safari's act and go to its dive. **Lean: new game plus the hand-off.**
2. **Land.** Cozy Village (the island's village, where the sellers already are; nineteen games, so a twentieth pages, never shrinks) or Tinker Lab (the tracker's row; twenty games). **Lean: Cozy Village**; the defining session checks `landLayout` either way.
3. **Selling is named, not judged.** *To sell* is ordinary: the maker says what they want, and no line says adverts are bad, tricks or scams. **Lean: yes.**
4. **Story Stamp Checker.** Absorb data-ai-literacy's proposal as a Notice Board rung (5 or a later 6) so there is one board, or keep it separate. **Lean: absorb**, recorded in both studies when ML2 is defined.
5. **Exaggeration, not lies.** Wrong posters are cheerful exaggerations or mix-ups ("as big as a tree"), never a friend lying on purpose, for pre-K and school 1–3; a maker with a reason to say something is only at level 5. **Lean: yes.**
6. **Nothing kept.** No saved posters or journal pages for 1–3. **Lean: nothing kept.**

**Device and person checks (for DEVICE-CHECKS once built):** does a four-year-old pick the witness from the memory picture, or the friend nearest the poster; does a seven-year-old's basket choice follow the price and stall cue or the maker's words; does a grown-up use the "Who made this?" card with a real box or advert?
