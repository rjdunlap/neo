# Speaking and listening: say enough for a listener, ask when it is unclear, keep the talk going

*Subject `speaking-listening` · 2026-10-22 · `claude/loving-gates-tzccpn` · [PR #810](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/810). Research and proposals only, for L/T/P/K/S/U/M and grown-ups. Base: `498852d6`. No game was played or implemented.*

**Recommendation:** the island already gives her a lot of *listening* (follow a spoken request): Photo Safari's requests grow by exactly the elements a speech-therapy direction ladder uses, and Teddy Doctor, Goodnight Room, Adventure Walk, Pet Says, Treasure Map and Peekaround Island all ask her to act on spoken words. It gives her nothing to *say*, and nothing in which a listener depends on her. The island cannot hear her, and it should not try (no microphone, no speech scoring). Picture chips let her speak without a voice. **Define first, inside Photo Safari, two levels called *Tell me which* and *Tell me both*** (IDs: the next free numbers after the dive's reserved 8 and 9, fixed in the definition). The pet holds the camera and cannot tell which friend to photograph. She can see which one a visiting friend wants, and she taps picture chips (an action, a place) to tell the pet. The pet photographs whatever her description fits. If it fits two friends, the pet asks "Which one? Two are jumping!" and her chips stay so she can add one. This is the referential-communication task. Its best-evidenced training form is the child's own attempt at reference with feedback from a listener, which is exactly this design (Matthews, Lieven and Tomasello 2007, below). *Tell me both* reuses level 7's scene rule; *Tell me which* adds a sibling rule (three animals, one feature of the target unique). Both reuse the art and tap resolution and need no new game or land. Its first level does not depend on any open decision.

**Development.** *Saying enough for a listener.* Matthews, Lieven and Tomasello, [*Child Development* 78(6), 2007](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1458200) (abstract opened): 224 children aged 2½, 3½ and 4½ asked for a sticker from a crowded array before and after training. Four training conditions all helped. The most effective was the child asking an adult herself, with feedback on her own attempts (the abstract does not say what form the feedback took). Four-year-olds transferred the skill, and "young children's communication skills develop best in response to feedback about their own attempts at reference". Pynte, Girotto and Baccino, [*Journal of Child Language* 1991](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/abs/childrens-communicative-abilities-revisited-verbal-versus-perceptual-disambiguating-strategies-in-referential-communication/F7D1EE4C4B0E7A404718A82805B25032) (abstract opened): seven-year-olds, nine-year-olds and adults resolved ambiguous descriptions of blocks, and the younger children were swayed by the article ("the" versus "a"). The claim that children under about seven do worse on referential-communication tasks comes from an Edinburgh undergraduate thesis citing earlier work (**search excerpt**). Robinson and Robinson (1982, "Knowing when you don't know enough") and Robinson and Whittaker (1986) are the studies usually cited for children missing ambiguity in messages; **only their titles were found, and neither was opened.** *Asking questions.* Ruggeri and Lombrozo, *Cognition* 143 (2015) (**search summary of the abstract**): from 7–8 to 9–11 to adults, questions shift from guessing one answer to narrowing the set, and every age shifted more when guessing was least useful. That supports [Mystery Friend](../../IDEAS.md#how-the-research-lands-on-rows-already-here)'s ladder. *Conversation.* Romeo and colleagues, [*Psychological Science* 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5945324) (opened): in 36 children aged 4½–6, more adult–child conversational turns went with Broca's-area activation and verbal skill, independently of SES and of words heard. The study is **correlational**. *Shared reading.* The What Works Clearinghouse found positive effects of dialogic reading on preschool oral language (**search summary**; the report was not opened). *Barrier games.* Bunch 1989 (42 upper-elementary children; **abstract excerpt only**) is the only controlled trial found. The [Barnsley NHS key-word sheet](https://www.southwestyorkshire.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Barrier-games-Key-word-levels.pdf) (opened) describes the practice: the adult directs first, then they swap, and the levels grow from one key word to four.

**Evidence limits.** No opened evidence shows that tapping picture chips on a tablet improves how a child talks to people, that the pet's "Which one?" transfers to asking real people, or what tweens or parents enjoy here. The island cannot hear her, so nothing it records is evidence about her speech. Completing *Tell me which* shows she can pick distinguishing features in a drawn scene, and nothing more.

## First game's proposed ladder: Photo Safari *Tell me which* and *Tell me both*

Photo Safari today: levels 1–7, school window 4–7. Level 7 (`clues`) places four animals at two shared places, so each clue matches two and both together match one; taps resolve to the nearest body centre; `describe()` and `matches()` already speak and test an action-and-place description. Levels 8 and 9 are reserved in the dive and unbuilt. These levels take the next free IDs, and the definition fixes them, appending to school so no saved level or pin moves.

**The round.** A visiting friend (one of the island critters, standing at the edge clear of the pet) holds up a thought bubble, and one animal in the scene twinkles: the one the friend wants a photo of. "Cleo wants a photo, but I can't tell which one! Tell me." The pet stands by the camera on its tripod. Below the scene is a tray of chips: one per action present and one per place present (pictured, spoken on touch, at least 140 by 120 units). Animal names are not chips, as at level 7 ("no name"). Tapping a chip puts it in her speech bubble and the pet repeats it ("in the pond"); tapping it again takes it out. The large **Tell** button (a speech bubble with the camera) sends it.

## Experience and presentation

- **First unsupported action:** tap a chip. Every chip is spoken on touch, and the pet echoes it, so a non-reader hears what she is saying.
- **Touch-down feedback:** a chip dips and plays its word; Tell puffs like a speech bubble.
- **The listener's reaction is the feedback:** the camera swings to what her words fit. Two candidates mean a visible wobble between them and a question, never a buzzer. The pet sounds curious, never disappointed.
- **Replay variety:** scenes, animals, actions and the friend who asks come from the seed; level 7's generator already varies places and actions.
- **The reveal:** the friend receives the polaroid and the album fills as it does now.
- **Off-screen activity:** the *Describe it, find it* card (SL8), and Photo Safari's existing "I spy" line turned around: she says "I spy…" and the grown-up finds it.
- **Co-play:** the grown-up plays the friend: they whisper which animal, she tells the pet. The existing co-play setting carries it as a tip.

## Decisions for the developer

1. **Accounting below school.** Lean: an under-informative Tell counts one miss at school and is unscored if a pre-K window ever opens (younger children do worse on these tasks: a thesis excerpt citing earlier work, not an opened study). A pre-K opening waits until a child has played A at school.
2. ***Which one?* accounting.** Lean: never a miss. Asking is praised. A guess on an ambiguous request is followed by the pet saying which it meant and that she could have asked. Or count a guess on an ambiguous request as a miss at school top. The further-gaps row forbids a compliance score, which favours the lean.
3. **Fewest Clues' home.** A Lantern Lake puzzle after its capacity test (twelve games), defined after Jar Clues and Weights Case so the grown-up shelf's logic puzzles do not repeat.
4. **Review gates:** the talk-about wording rules and the *Describe it, find it* card get an ordinary content review. **Anything about speech delay, milestones or "when to worry" needs a named speech-language pathologist** and is not drafted here.
5. **Person checks to add:** whether a six-year-old understands that the pet cannot see the twinkle (the pretend premise); whether the camera's wobble between two friends reads as "I'm not sure" rather than "wrong"; chip size and tray fit in portrait.

None of these blocks the A–B definition: decision 1's lean is the default, and the school window needs no new decision.
