# Medieval life: castles as homes, guilds, markets and monasteries

**Subject:** `medieval-life` · **Audiences:** L, T, P, K, S, U, M, G ([definitions](../subjects.md#the-audiences)) · **Date:** 2026-10-19 · **Session:** `claude/subject-medieval-life`, [PR #581](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/581).

Research and proposals only. **Define Keep House first:** a castle is a home where a household works, so she places its people and rooms where their work needs them, then reads a plan the way a historian does. Its ladder follows what a castle actually was (a lord's fortified *residence*, an estate office and a workplace for cooks, grooms, a chamberlain and a treasurer) rather than the knights-and-battles picture that research shows children already carry before school. Markets, guilds and monasteries extend games that already own paying, weighing, clocks, patterns and sequences: a loaf against the fair weight, a bell with no face, three fields in rotation, sheep to cloth, a split tally. The youngest bands get cause and effect at a castle door with no claim that an infant learns history. Older play adds a plan read from its clues, a stone-or-timber cost decision, two sources that disagree and a grown-up market-charter puzzle. No runtime band, zone, game or system is added here.

| [Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle) | "the private fortified residence of a lord or noble"; the keep "main living quarters of the castle"; the bailey held "barracks, stables, workshops, and storage" with a well or cistern; the great hall hosted feasts, meetings and trials; a chamberlain ran the household and "a treasurer kept the estate's written records"; kitchens were busy when the lord was in residence. | [Arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch) | A true arch's elements operate "under compressive forces"; thrust "caused the need for heavy abutments"; voussoirs are wedge-shaped and the keystone is "the top block in an arch"; a pointed arch has "a lower horizontal reaction at the base", which Gothic building used for taller openings. The centering (the wooden form the stones rest on until the keystone is in) is *recalled*; the page's construction section was not reached. **Consequence:** *Keystone* is a Block Tower mode with an explicit stated rule (the ring stands only when complete and held at both ends), not a physics claim about every arch |
| [Guild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guild) | Craft guilds passed on skills through "formal systems of apprenticeship, journeymanship and mastery"; regulations could set minimum or maximum prices, limit trading hours and cap apprentices; charters gave "monopolies on local production"; guilds ran welfare funds and feasts; most were male-dominated, women entering through marriage, widowhood or family and usually barred from office, with exceptions (Rouen); Adam Smith and others saw them as monopolies hindering innovation, other historians credit training and quality control. **Consequence:** a guild is the warden who checks the work (fair weight) and the apprentice-to-master story on the grown-up card; "who may sell" and price rules belong to the money and civics studies; the exclusions get one honest U/M line |
| [Market town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_town) | Market rights came from custom or a royal charter; markets were held "one or two days a week"; a 13th-century movement moved them off Sundays; a chartered market's fixed days barred a nearby rival from the same days; new markets were barred within "a day's travel, about 10 km (6.2 mi)" of an existing one, a rule the article says still applies in England; a market cross stood in the centre; local goods (grain, livestock, cloth, yarn, cheese) with towns known for a product (Stroud's cloth, Banbury's cheese). **Consequence:** *Market Days* is a seeded constraint puzzle (towns within a day's walk take different days) for grown-ups, with a small school rung |
| [Assize of Bread and Ale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assize_of_Bread_and_Ale) | A 13th-century English law: the price of a loaf stayed fixed while its *weight* moved with the price of wheat; manorial courts enforced it; "aletasters" or ale-conners sworn each year tested the ale; the assize "likely led to" the term *baker's dozen*, bakers adding a loaf to stay inside the law. **Consequence:** *Fair weight* is a Seesaw Balance mode (the loaf against the fair-weight stone; too light, add a roll) and the warden's check in Keep House's market rung; it is a rule as a public solution (D2.Civ.12), not a lesson in punishment |
| [Tally stick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tally_stick) | A split tally is "a bilateral record"; notches cut across the full width, then the stick split lengthwise so each party keeps a matching half (the longer *stock* to the lender, the shorter *foil* to the receiver); an added notch on one half shows at once because "the absence of those notches would be apparent on the other party's tally stick"; in the English Exchequer the notch *width* encoded value (a palm £1,000, a thumb £100, a swollen barleycorn £1, a cut with no wood removed one penny); Henry I introduced it about 1100; the 1834 burning of old tallies set the Houses of Parliament alight. **Consequence:** *Stock and foil* is a record that checks itself, so it is a River Tablets cross-reference (level 5–6) and a one-case Keep House rung, never a second records game |
| [Three-field system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-field_system) | One field sown in autumn with wheat or rye, one in spring with "peas, lentils, or beans", one fallow and grazed; "rotated every year"; spread in Europe between the 9th and 11th centuries, replacing a two-field system that left half the land idle; legumes fix nitrogen; spring oats fed horses. **Consequence:** *Field, field, fallow* is an ABC cycle, so it is a Pattern Train mode, and the two-field comparison is its one decision |
| [Canonical hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_hours) and the [Salisbury Cathedral clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salisbury_Cathedral_clock) | The monastic day ran by named hours from Vigil (about 2 a.m.) through Lauds (dawn), Prime, Terce, Sext ("noon"), None, Vespers (sunset) and Compline; Benedict's "seven daytime hours and one at night"; bells "are tolled at the fixed times of these canonical hours". The Salisbury clock, "Thought to date from about 1386" (with doubts), is "a large iron-framed tower clock without a dial", a verge-and-foliot escapement driven by two stone weights, whose main purpose was "to strike a bell at precise times" for services. **Consequence:** *The bell with no face* is a Clock Tower mode: count the strikes, set the hour. The monastery appears as the place that kept time and rang it for the town, never as a place of belief |
| [Scriptorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scriptorium) and the Getty's *The Making of a Medieval Manuscript* ([Medievalists.net report](https://www.medievalists.net/2026/07/how-medieval-manuscripts-were-made-new-getty-exhibition/) on the Getty exhibition, 2026-08-04 to 2027-03-14, curator Nancy Turner; the Getty news page returned only its title) | Skins "soaked in lime, stretched on frames, and repeatedly scraped"; ruling with a stylus or lead point; quills "made from goose or swan feathers"; pigments "from minerals, plants, insects, or chemical processes", gold "used sparingly"; gatherings sewn onto supports through wooden boards. In the scriptorium monks and lay scribes copied, an *armarius* supplied materials, Cistercians kept silence and later placed scriptoria near the warm kitchen; a tenth-century prior: copying "dims your eyes, makes your back ache"; Cassiodorus had copies checked against "ancient, trustworthy exemplars". **Consequence:** *Big letter* is a Stamp Studio mode (decorate a huge initial); the copying-and-checking step is a cross-reference to River Tablets' disagreeing records, and the order of making a page is one Story Steps story after review |
| [Stained glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stained_glass) | Colour from "metallic oxide powders" (copper green, cobalt blue, gold wine-red); flashed glass for reds; pieces held in lead "cames or calms"; the medieval cartoon "drawn directly on the surface of a whitewashed table"; windows told stories "for a largely illiterate populace" and were "illuminated wall decorations". **Consequence:** a Light Lab note (the beam through coloured glass already exists); a stained-glass *picture* that lights up is content, not a rung |
| [Medieval English wool trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_English_wool_trade) and the [Luttrell Psalter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luttrell_Psalter) | Wool went to Flanders to become cloth; the pedal loom and spinning wheel spread; England "innovated mechanised methods for fulling cloth in the 13th century"; Edward I's permanent wool duty of 1275; raw-wool exports fell from 40,000–45,000 sacks a year to 9,706 in 1476 as cloth exports rose. The Luttrell Psalter (c. 1320–45, made for Sir Geoffrey Luttrell of Irnham) is "one of the richest sources for visual depictions of everyday rural life in medieval England" (harvest "both men and women", servants, a feast, a taborer), with the caution that much of its margin is "pure fantasy" and the scenes serve the patron's aims. **Consequence:** *Sheep to cloth* is a Story Steps story; the Psalter is the model for the island's own "pictures of work" and for the U/M rung on what a patron's picture can and cannot show |
| [Domesday Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domesday_Book) and [Serfdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serfdom) | The 1086 survey recorded each manor's plough teams "each reckoned at eight oxen", meadows, mills (5,624), peasants by class and value, sworn by "Twelve local jurors, half English and half Norman"; it lists 28,000 slaves, omitted the far north, never filled the space left for London, and "Its clerks made errors". | [Rule of tincture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_tincture) | "metal should not be put on metal, nor colour on colour" (or and argent against sable, gules, azure, vert, purpure), so arms stay "easily recognisable"; furs and "proper" charges are exempt; famous violations exist. **Consequence:** a two-colour contrast rule for the visual-art study's shield or banner painting; no heraldry rung here, since arms are a nobility's war-and-rank system |
| [Nine men's morris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_men%27s_morris) | Twenty-four points, nine men each, place then move, a mill of three removes an opponent's man, flying at three, a win at two men or no move; it "peaked in popularity in medieval England" with boards "carved into cathedral seats"; three men's morris and six men's morris (obsolete by 1600) are the smaller forms. **Consequence:** a family card with the three-men form for small children; removal is a mild capture, so the card says so, as the Ur card does |
| [Carcassonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcassonne_(board_game)) | 72 tiles; "roads must connect to roads, fields to fields, and cities to cities", "corners do not connect"; one follower per tile as knight, monk, farmer or thief; completed cities, roads and cloisters score during play, fields at the end; Spiel des Jahres 2001; *My First Carcassonne* (2009) is "a short game for younger children" (its age is not stated on the page). **Consequence:** edge-matching tiles are the family card's physical precedent and the model for a Keep House plan of large tiles; the island borrows the matching, not the scoring race |

### Real-history boundaries

- **No battles, sieges, weapons or conquest.** The castle is drawn with walls and a gate because that is what a castle is, and nothing on them is ever used. No archers, no portcullis to drop, no trebuchet, no dungeon as a place to put anyone. The Conquest, the Crusades, the Hundred Years War and the Wars of the Roses stay off the island; a grown-up card may name them in one sentence as "what the school years cover".
- **The Church as a workplace, not a belief.** Monasteries appear as the places that kept time, rang bells, kept sheep, copied books and fed travellers. Prayer, saints, the Crusades, Church-and-crown and the Reconquista (California 7.6.4/6/9) are outside the island's scope. Religion in daily life is a reviewed grown-up line at most.
- **Unfree people.** Most of the people who worked a castle's lands were not free to leave them, and Domesday counted 28,000 slaves. Younger rungs show people at work and say nothing. The U/M case states it in one reviewed sentence and never concludes that service was voluntary or that the castle was kind.
- **The plague** (KS3, California 7.6.7) is not depicted. If the wool or market rungs ever reach a "fewer people" change, it is a grown-up-card sentence, not a child's picture.
- **An island castle, not a real one.** Keep House's castle is the island's own, "like the castles people built long ago", with island names. Beaumaris, Dover and the Luttrell family appear only on the grown-up card and in the 11–14 rung's sources, with the figures above and their uncertainty.
- **Every historical line spoken or printed needs a named reviewer** (a medievalist, an English Heritage-style education officer, or a KS3 history teacher) before it ships. Rule tests check placement, consistency and solvability; they cannot check history.

## What fits, and what does not

- **Fits:** a castle as a home where people work; rooms placed by need; a plan read from its clues; a loaf against the fair weight; a bell with no face; three fields in a cycle; sheep to cloth; a record that checks itself; stone or timber with both costs visible; market days that must not clash; two sources that disagree; a painted initial. Each is a fun action whose consequence is the learning.
- **Fits as a note only:** a stone wall (Block Tower), a stained-glass picture (Light Lab), the pictorial shop sign (Market Stall), the tincture rule (visual art), the Domesday count and the copyist's check (River Tablets), the market charter as a rule (civics), guild prices and who may sell (money).
- **Does not fit:** sieges, knights' combat, archery, dungeons, a drawbridge to raise against anyone; the Conquest, the Crusades, the plague, the wars; religion as belief; a king's laws as a quiz; "princess" as a job; a tournament; dates as answers; any real castle shown as something she "built". No conquering, no armies, no points for a kingdom.

**Tentative windows:** toddler A; preschool A–2; pre-K 1–3; school 3–6; grown-up 7 (or Market Days registered separately). `school`'s upper rungs are selected by level, not a new U/M band. A first implementation can be A–3 only. Never silently add a level below a saved one; use the `order` contract if a younger rung is appended later.

**First-round packet to define:** one island castle (a hall, a gate, a well, a wall with nothing on it), six figures with tools, three open rooms, a plan of large tiles (at least 100 logical units after scaling), the door that opens on touch-down with its sound, the walk-and-steam ending, two seeded plans for level 3 with the needs list and its solver, voice lines ("like the castles people built long ago"), a how-to card per level and a ghost finger that taps doors, drags figures and drops rooms. No free drawing, no physics, no reusable museum engine.

**Mistakes and accounting:** levels A and 2 count nothing (a placement she can change is an experiment). From level 1 only a wrong drop or, at 3–7, a *commit* (the Finish bell, a label, a mark) can count a miss; moving rooms, re-reading needs and asking the pet to repeat one are free. After two misses on one plan, one hint glows the room or clue that helps most and counts once; asked-for help counts at most once per plan. Assisted completion still finishes for one sticker. A "can't tell" at level 6 that is the right answer is a correct answer, never a dodge.

## Experience and presentation

The first unsupported action is tapping a door, not answering a question. Touch-down opens the door and the figure steps out at once; the pet names the job as the figure starts it. Palette colours for stone, thatch, straw and the hall's fire; no sepia filter and no "olde" lettering. Rooms are big tiles with one drawn feature each (a hearth, mangers, barrels, a window seat), so a plan can be read without words; the needs are spoken and shown as two-picture pairs (a pot and a well). Portrait keeps the plan centred, the figures above it and the pet bottom-left; the Finish bell is at least 100 units and off the pet. This is not a music game: pick an existing compatible island style in the definition; the castle's own bell is a sound effect.

Replay varies the plan's shape, which needs are asked, which rooms share a clue and which towns are chartered, never the rules. Nothing accrues; nothing is owed. Round completion uses `ctx.finish`; a kept plan in the treehouse is a later creations question, not part of the first slice. Cleanup releases drags, taps and timers.

**Co-play:** steward and mason (above). **Off screen, proposed:** the cardboard castle with a detached keep and the kitchen question; a chalked three-men's-morris board. The morris card needs no reviewer; the box card carries a small-parts note.

## Decisions for the developer

1. **Approve the castle-as-home rule.** Walls and a gate are drawn and never used; no weapon, archer, dungeon or siege appears at any band, and the KS3 war strand is a grown-up-card sentence. Alternative: a reviewed "castles were also for defence" line at U/M. Lean: draw the walls, say nothing, let the plan show the storeroom and the well.
2. **Placement and windows.** Cozy Village (beside Market Stall and Clock Tower) or Story Grove after `landLayout`'s capacity test; Storybook Square stays proposed. Exact appended levels, ranges and whether a first build is A–3 only.
3. **Market Days' home.** Keep House level 7 for 13+ via `grownup` in `bands`, or a separate `GROWNUP_GAMES` puzzle in Lantern Lake with a thirteenth-game capacity test. Lean: separate, so the child's game stays a household and Lantern Lake gets a colouring puzzle it does not have.
4. **Name a reviewer** (a medievalist, a heritage education officer or a KS3 history teacher) for every spoken or printed historical line: the household's jobs, the assize and the baker's dozen, the bell with no face, the three fields, sheep to cloth, the split tally, the Beaumaris figures, the Luttrell margins and the Domesday count.
5. **Unfree labour depth.** Younger rungs show people at work and say nothing. Decide whether the M/G *What can a plan prove?* case states in one reviewed sentence that most who worked the castle's lands could not leave them (and that Domesday counted slaves), or defers the whole claim. Lean: state it; never "everyone chose to be there".
6. **The Church boundary.** Monasteries as timekeepers, copyists, shepherds and hosts only; prayer, saints and the Crusades off the island; at most one reviewed grown-up line that the bells and the books were for prayer. Confirm, since California 7.6.2/8 would otherwise pull belief into the M rung.
7. **Keystone and the physics decision.** A stated ring rule with no simulation, or wait for chunk 25's physics approach and build a real arch. Lean: the stated rule now, in Block Tower's own dive.
8. **Records cross-listing.** Stock and foil and the jurors' count are River Tablets cases, so they wait for that game's levels 5–6; confirm, so no second records engine starts here.
9. **Guild exclusions.** Whether the grown-up card's apprentice-to-master story carries the one honest line that most guilds barred women from office and outsiders from trade. Lean: yes, one sentence, with Rouen as the exception.

**Decided 2026-10-23 (on the developer's delegation):** every lean: walls and a gate are drawn and never used, no weapon, dungeon or siege, the war strand a grown-up-card sentence; Keep House in Cozy Village if `landLayout` passes, else Story Grove, first build A–3; Market Days a separate Lantern Lake puzzle; the 13+ plan case states in one source-checked sentence that most who worked the lands could not leave and that Domesday counted slaves; monasteries only as timekeepers, copyists, shepherds and hosts; Keystone as the stated ring rule now (Water Road level 1 owns the arch); tally and Domesday cases wait for River Tablets 5–6; the guild card carries the one line on women and outsiders.
