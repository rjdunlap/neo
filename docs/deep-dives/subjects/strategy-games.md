# Strategy games: look ahead, stop the other row, and work backwards from the end

**Subject:** `strategy-games` · **Audiences:** L, T, P, K, S, U, M and G, as defined in the [subject tracker](../subjects.md#the-audiences) · **Requested:** the [tracker](../subjects.md#languages-arts-thinking-and-the-natural-world) row (two-player thinking games: Nim, mancala, tic-tac-toe, dots and boxes, chess puzzles; a kind bot and a "show me why" replay), added by the [standards audit](standards-audit.md) · **Researched:** 2026-10-31 · **Session:** `claude/research-strategy-games`, [PR #1055](https://github.com/rjdunlap/neo/pull/1055).

**Research and proposals only; nothing here is built.** A strategy game asks a player to choose a move by thinking about what the other player will do next. The island has many one-player logic puzzles (Secret Code, Ferry Jam, Garden Rows, Island Bridges, Sudoku Garden) and couch **face-offs** in which each player solves their own board ([COUCH-PLAY](../../COUCH-PLAY.md)); it has **no game in which she and an opponent move on one board in turn**. That is this study's gap. The rules for every activity forbid game-over screens, lives and guilt, so the study's question is how to give her a real opponent without a losing screen.

**Recommendation.** Define **Shell Rows** levels 1–3 first: a new game in which she and the pet take turns setting shells and pebbles on a 3 by 3 grid drawn in the sand. Early levels are not whole matches but **one-move puzzles** taken from a match in progress, following the order in which Crowley and Siegler (1993) found children add tic-tac-toe rules one at a time: *finish your row* (1), *stop the pet's row* (2), and *win first, then block* when both are possible (3). A wrong move is answered by the pet playing its reply: the row it makes glows, the pet says what happened ("I got three!"), and the board rewinds to her turn, the *show me why* replay. Later levels add *two ways to win* (a fork, 4) and *play it out* (a whole gentle match where a tie is a good ending, 5). **Last Pebble** (Nim and NRICH's *Got It* as stepping stones across a stream, working backwards from the far bank) is the school-top and 13+ follow-up, and **Seed Bowls** (sowing, after mancala, played together rather than against) is a preschool counting candidate held for a cultural-source review.

## What people can do, and what remains uncertain

- **Rules are added one at a time.** Crowley and Siegler, "Flexible strategy use in young children's tic-tac-toe", *Cognitive Science* 17(4), 1993 ([listing](https://informalscience.org/research/flexible-strategy-use-young-childrens-tic-tac-toe); **abstract summary opened, paper not read**): children's tic-tac-toe "strategies are rule based and … new rules are added one at a time"; even young children "flexibly tailor their strategy use" to circumstances, by putting their limited resources on the rules that fit their current goal. The rule names in the order used below (win, block, fork, and win before block) and the children's ages (about kindergarten to second grade) are **recalled**, not read; the defining session should open the paper or keep them marked recalled. **Consequence:** one rule per level, and a level that asks her to *choose* between rules (win or block) only after each has its own.
- **Linear number board games help number sense; that is not strategy.** Siegler and Ramani, *Developmental Science* 11(5), 2008 (WWC listing and search summary; **not opened**): four 15-minute sessions of a linear numbered race game closed a low-income preschool gap in number-line estimation, against a colour-square control. That supports Seed Bowls' counting along a row, and says nothing about looking ahead.
- **Chess does not make children smarter.** Sala and Gobet's 2016 meta-analysis in *Educational Research Review* and their 2017 review in *Current Directions in Psychological Science* (**search summaries; not opened**) found small effects of chess instruction on maths and reading that shrank as study design improved, and concluded that far transfer "rarely occurs". **Consequence:** grown-up wording names what a game asks of her ("looking at what the pet could do next") and claims no general thinking, school or attention benefit (the October 23 rule).
- **Going first and second.** NRICH treats "when is it better to go first" as the question that reveals the structure (opened); it is a school-top question.
- **Gaps.** No study was found on when children understand that an opponent *wants* something different (the theory-of-mind side of strategy), on how young children feel about losing to a computer opponent, or on dots and boxes or Nim with children. Searches for both returned nothing relevant. Recorded as gaps; the person checks below ask the second one directly.

## What fits

- **Puzzles before matches.** A one-move puzzle taken from a game in progress has one right move, a nameable wrong move and a short reply to show why. That fits *mistakes teach* exactly; a whole match does not, until the rules are hers.
- **The opponent is the pet, and it plays to teach.** At puzzle levels it plays its best reply so the consequence is honest; at *play it out* it plays at a handicap set by the level (sometimes missing a block), and the round completes on her win **or a tie**. A pet win replays to her last choice with the turning point glowing; it is never a loss screen.
- **Show me why** is the replay: after a wrong move the pet's reply plays out, the row that decides it glows, and the board steps back to her turn.
- **Cooperative where the youngest are.** Seed Bowls is played *together*: both players sow toward one goal, and the oware rule of giving seeds to an empty side is the island's kindness rule.
- **Does not fit:** captures as the fun for the young bands, chess's army framing, any rating or ladder of opponents, timed puzzles, and a second player required on one iPad for her to finish.

## A first game's ladder: Shell Rows

The scene: a 3 by 3 grid drawn in wet sand, each square at least 150 units; her shells on one side, the pet's pebbles on the other. Every round is three positions, each from a match in progress, with her to move. A square she touches darkens at once; lifting sets the shell.

| Level | First round | What she decides | Mistakes and hints | Rule test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1 · Finish the row** · pre-K 1, school 1 | Two of her shells in a row with the third square empty, and two pebbles elsewhere (no pebble threat) | Where the third shell goes | Another square: the pet sets a pebble, nothing happens, and the board rewinds with the row's two shells nodding, one miss. Two misses: the square glows (one hint). Right: the three shells shine and the pet claps "Three in a row!" | Every position has exactly one winning square and no opposing two-in-a-row |
| **2 · Stop the row** · pre-K 2, school 2 | The pet has two pebbles in a row; she has no row to finish | Which square stops the pet | Elsewhere: *show me why*, the pet sets its third pebble, its row glows, the pet says "I got three! Can you stop me?", rewind, one miss. Two misses: glow | Exactly one blocking square; she has no immediate win; the pet's win is real (minimax) |
| **3 · Win first** · school 3 | She has two in a row and so does the pet | Take her own win, not the block | Blocking: the pet says "You could have had three!", her winning square shimmers in the replay, one miss | Both threats present; exactly one winning square for her |

Levels 1–3 are the first slice. **Cost:** M. A board model and minimax, puzzle positions as authored seeds plus generated ones checked by the rule tests, shells, pebbles, the replay and the pet's lines. No new save field; nothing kept. A bot plays the right square from the model.

## Experience and presentation

- **The first unsupported action** is a tap on the gap in her own row, guided by the shine on her two shells. Touch-down darkens the square at once.
- **The payoff** is the row shining and the pet's delight when she wins, and its mock surprise when she blocks it ("Oh! You saw it!"). It never gloats and never ends a round on its own win.
- **Replay variety:** seeded positions, rotated and mirrored (eight symmetries of each), with the shells and pebbles drawn from beach finds.
- **Off-screen:** the table games card.

## Decisions for the developer

Under the [October 23 standing rules](../../archive/DECISIONS-2026-10-23.md#standing-rules) the defining session adopts each lean; only the person checks wait.

1. **Identity.** A new game, Shell Rows, rather than a Garden Rows or Peg Garden mode: neither has an opponent whose move answers hers. **Lean: new game.**
2. **Land.** Puzzle Peaks (thirteen today, Rule Machine queued as its fourteenth, so a fifteenth pages, never shrinks) or Bubble Beach for the sand. **Lean: Puzzle Peaks**, beside the other logic; the defining session checks `landLayout`.
3. **Whole matches.** Levels 1–3 are puzzles only. At level 5 *play it out*, the round completes on her win or a tie; a pet win replays to her last choice with the turning point glowing and counts one miss. **Lean: yes.**
4. **Mancala provenance.** Seed Bowls is a generic sowing game; a spoken or card line naming oware or another mancala game waits for a reviewer from or recognised by the culture concerned, as the ground rules ask. **Lean: generic until reviewed.**
5. **Who plays the other side.** The pet by default; a grown-up can take the pebbles at *play it out* only. **Lean: pet, grown-up at 5.**
6. **Chess.** No chess piece or army theme on the island; chess-style "best move" puzzles appear only as Shell Rows and Last Pebble positions. **Lean: none.**

**Device and person checks (for DEVICE-CHECKS once built):** does a five-year-old look at the pet's pebbles before setting a shell at level 2, or tap the last empty-looking square; does the *show me why* replay read as a lesson rather than a loss; how does she react when the pet wins at *play it out*?
