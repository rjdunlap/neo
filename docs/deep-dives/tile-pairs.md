## The game today

A grown-up clears a small layered stack of picture tiles by removing matching pairs. A tile can be taken only when nothing lies on top of it and its left or right side is open on its own layer; free tiles are bright and blocked ones muted. Tap one free tile, then its match; tap a selected tile again to put it back. Nothing is timed and nothing is lost: Undo restores the last pair or shuffle, Hint rings a pair that lies on a clearing route, and Shuffle lays whatever is left in open rows that can always be paired off. The purpose is **spatial planning after Mahjong solitaire**: seeing which tiles a pair uncovers and choosing, among several copies of a picture, the pair that keeps the rest clearable. It was built in October for roadmap step 8b (PRs #229 and #232), lives in Lantern Lake (`region: 'puzzle-peaks'`, `challenge: 'noticing'`), is upright-ready on a phone and is one of the couch catalog's grown-up games.

`BandLevels`: `grownup 1–6`, no `order`, no `entryLevels`.

**What a round draws from.** There is no frozen pool. `makeBoard` takes the level's fixed layout, deals up to 40 seeded geometric clearing routes (`seededRoute`, the same memoised search with the free position pairs tried in shuffled order) and keeps the first whose measured random-play clear rate sits in the level's `clear` band (`deal`, slice 1), else the closest; shuffled pictures go onto that route's pairs, so every board is solvable without rerolling. The pictures are the six `SHAPES` in the six `RAINBOW` colours, one colour per shape. A board lives in a 32-bit mask (`1 << tiles.length`), and `solve` is an exhaustive memoised search; both are sized for 24 tiles.

**Rules today.** A mismatch removes nothing, boings, says `tile-pairs.mismatch` and counts a miss; two since the last match show a hint. A blocked tile squeaks and explains itself, and is not a miss. After each pair the scene re-solves the remainder: when no route is left it says so (`tile-pairs.stuck`) and the help line changes; Hint then shuffles first (one shuffle, one help) and rings a pair. `finish` reports misses, hints and `score = undos + shuffles`.

**Couch.** `COUCH_INFO['tile-pairs']`: stick between free tiles, bottom to choose, left to undo, hint and shuffle in the pause menu; face-off on identical seeded boards, lower undos plus shuffles wins. There is no frozen course.

**Measured difficulty** (second pass, before slice 1; slice 1's deal dial now holds levels 1–4 at 95–100%, level 5 at 85–100% and level 6 at 75–100% by construction; a copy of `logic.ts` run under Node outside the test runner, 40 seeds a level). A *losing pair* is a legal matching pair after which no clearing route is left. The *random-play clear rate* is the share of play-outs that clear when every turn takes a uniformly random legal pair: a crude stand-in for a player who does not plan, and so a measure of how much planning a deal asks for. "Seeded routes" deals each board along a randomised geometric route (what slice 1 proposes) instead of the fixed one.

**Known gaps** (each read from the code on 2026-11-11, not from a playtest):

- **Levels 1–4 cannot be lost, and no level has a losing first pair** (measured above). With today's fixed route every pair taken in any order clears levels 1–4 (checked exhaustively for 40 seeds a level: every state reachable by matching pairs, about 200,000 in all, and none of levels 1–4's is stuck; levels 5 and 6 reach stuck states on 23 and 38 of the 40 seeds), so the choices the level table names ("which two of four copies", "which end to open first") have no wrong answer yet, and Hint, Undo and the stuck line never come into play there. Only levels 5 and 6 can strand a careless player, and only late (about the ninth or tenth pair). The ladder's difficulty is in its deals, not its layouts: the same layouts dealt along seeded routes range from no planning at all to boards where most random play-outs strand.
- **The same pairing every round** (closed by slice 1: the route is now seeded, as above; the text is the position before it). The geometric search is deterministic, so a level's route is always the same list of position pairs, and the seed only decides which picture each route pair gets. At level 1 (six pairs, six pictures) the board is the same six pairs of positions every time with the pictures swapped round; at levels 2–6 the positions that share a picture are always unions of the same fixed pairs. A returning player can learn where a tile's partner sits.
- **Covered tiles are invisible.** A raised tile is drawn 6 units up and across on a 108-unit face, so the tile beneath shows only a sliver of its edge and none of its picture. From level 2 on, 4, 8, 6, 12 and 10 of the tiles start hidden (levels 2–6). The solver and the hint see them; she cannot. Choosing "which two of four copies" is then partly a guess, and `tile-pairs.stuck` can announce a dead end that no visible information predicted. The learning purpose is planning, so this is the gap that matters most.
- **A pair just vanishes** (closed by slice 1: a matched pair lifts and fades and the tiles it frees pulse). Removal is one frame and a pop: nothing shows which tiles it freed, though that consequence is the thing to notice.
- **One look.** Six shapes in six colours on a cream tile, every round.
- **A short, flat top.** Levels 3–6 are all 24 tiles on three layers; the ladder ends where the first definition stopped.

**On strategy.** Players' guides agree on a short list, and each becomes a designed lesson below: remove the tiles that block the most ([Shanghai II's own strategy text](https://discmaster.textfiles.com/file/14421/Info_Mac%20IV%20CD-ROM%20(Pacific%20HiTech%20Inc.)(August%201994).iso/Games/Commercial/Shanghai%20II%201.05%20Updater/Updater%20to%20Shanghai%20II%201.05/Updater%20to%20Shanghai%20II%201.05.rsrc/TEXT_130_Strategy.txt), [Kristanix](https://kristanix.com/mahjong/mahjong_tips.php)); work long rows early ([Tom Sloper](https://sloperama.com/mjfaq/mjfaq13e.html)); with three free copies, leave the one that blocks least ([Arkadium](https://www.arkadium.com/blog/mahjongg-solitaire-strategy-ak/)); when all four copies are free take both pairs, and once one pair of a picture is gone the other is always safe; copies stacked on each other cannot all come out ([mahjong-pro](https://mahjong-pro.com/mahjong-solitaire-strategy-guide/)); keep a pair that opens nothing as a spare (Arkadium). One property of this rule reshapes those habits: removing tiles only ever frees others, so a pair that is free stays free until taken, and once the copies are matched up the order of the pairs cannot decide the outcome. With every picture visible, the only choice that can lose is **which copies are paired together** (which is why levels with exactly two copies of a picture never go wrong). Order habits such as "open the most first", "long rows early" and "keep a spare" earn their keep when pictures are hidden, which is how full-size games are played; once Peek shows every picture they become ways to see the copy choice sooner. The lessons below are therefore all copy choices. Mahjong-pro's measurements on random full-size deals rank them: counting available moves is the strongest single habit (13.7% of deals won, 18.6% looking three moves ahead), and looking ahead helps every habit.

**How puzzles are generated and graded.** Dealing backwards along a route (what `makeBoard` does) is the standard way to guarantee a clear ([a developer's write-up](https://dev.to/dev_nash/most-free-mahjong-games-can-deal-you-a-board-you-cant-finish-i-built-mine-backwards-1cia)). De Bondt grades difficulty by the share of random play-outs that succeed, which is the measure used above. A JAIST thesis ([abstract](https://dspace.jaist.ac.jp/dspace/bitstream/10119/12019/2/abstract.pdf), read from a summary) found that boards a weak solver clears but a strong one often fails feel *frustrating*, because good moves lead to bad results; a calm game should reject those. Neither GNOME Mahjongg (ten layouts, all 144 tiles) nor KMahjongg (72 layouts, smallest 74) has a layout under 50 tiles, and no source found a published pack of mahjong-solitaire puzzles with a single solution, so the small boards and lessons here are original designs.

**On solvability.** Michiel de Bondt's [paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1203.6559) shows that clearing a board is NP-complete even when the player may look under tiles ("peeking"), and his [solver page](https://www.math.ru.nl/~debondt/mjsolver.html) reports that about 3% of 10 million random turtle deals cannot be cleared even with peeking. Two things follow for this game. Random deals are unfair, so dealing along a known route (as `makeBoard` does) is right. And a board is only a planning puzzle when the player has the information the solver has; without peeking it is a memory-and-luck game with a plan-shaped surface. At 24 tiles the exact search is instant, so this game can afford to give her that information.

## What makes sense here

The rule is settled and good. What the game lacks is honesty about information, a visible consequence, and variety; new levels come after those. In order of value:

1. **Let her see under the stack.** A free look turns levels 2–6 from guessing into planning and makes the "no clearing route" line something she could have foreseen.
2. **Deal a different pairing each round**, so replay is a new puzzle rather than a remembered one.
3. **Show what a pair uncovers.**
4. **Then grow the stack**: taller first (where looking under matters most), then a matching rule with more freedom, then more tiles within what the exact solver can still prove.

A timer, a score, a tray that fills, daily streaks and rationed help all conflict with the project rules and are left out. The long-term arc stays inside the grown-up band: a handful of calm, always-solvable stacks whose difficulty is the depth of the plan, not the count of tiles.

**Second pass: where "much more" comes from.** The measurements above change the order of the growth. Bigger stacks alone do not add puzzle: levels 3 and 4 have 24 tiles and no wrong move. Three sources of new challenge stand out, each kept untimed and always clearable:

- **Deals chosen by a measured target.** Every level gets a band of random-play clear rate, and the round's deal is the first of a few seeded candidates inside it. This is the cheapest lever (about a millisecond a candidate) and it is also a correction slice 1 needs: seeded routes alone make level 5 much harder on average than today (median 60% against 93%) and leave level 1 unchanged.
- **Designed puzzles that teach one idea each.** Random dealing almost never produces a board with one right opening (1 in 5,000 seeded deals on levels 2–6), so these are frozen, hand-made boards checked by the solver, as Ferry Jam's harbours are. They are the "puzzle challenges" proper: short, all-visible boards where the trap is the lesson.
- **New geometry and rule variants** taken from the long history of the genre: short-side tiles, half-step bricks, a wild family, three-alike rounds, and larger stacks once the mask is widened. The exact search stays fast to 40 tiles and does not at 48 (measured below), so 40 is the ceiling.


## Experience and presentation

**First action.** Tap a bright tile, then another bright tile with the same picture; level 1 has no layers, so the first pair needs no explanation beyond the bright and muted contrast. That already works.

**What should feel good and does not yet.** Touch-down outlines the tile in blue and ticks (built). A matched pair should lift, drift apart and fade over about 0.2 s while the tiles it freed brighten with a short pulse, one soft pentatonic step higher per tile freed, before the board is ready again; today the pair is simply gone. The tally can say how many pairs are open now ("3 pairs open") beside the tiles left, which is the number a planner watches.

**Looking under.** **Peek** is the fourth button. It fades tile bodies with another tile beneath them to quarter opacity and keeps their outlines visible. Each stack shows its pictures side by side, from bottom to top, left to right. Peek, P or the top controller button toggles the view. Choosing a tile while looking closes the view and preserves the selection. Peek leaves the round counters and face-off score alone. Four 160-unit buttons at 180 spacing fit the 768-unit upright width and the landscape row.

**What varies on replay without changing the rule.** The pairing (slice 1), the pictures' places, and a seeded look: the current shapes, a second set of six from the other shapes in `src/art/shapes.ts` (`MORE_SHAPES` has five, so one more is drawn), and one of small island pictures if the [reuse map](../DESIGN.md#reuse-map) has six that differ in outline as well as colour. A look is chosen by the round's seed and never changes mid-round.

**Reasoning apart from reading, memory and motor skill.** No reading is needed to play; the tally and help line are extra. Peek removes the memory load. Targets stay 108 units through level 9.

**Supported completion and stopping.** Unchanged: Hint, Undo and Shuffle always lead to a clear stack and one sticker; a round is a few minutes and nothing is kept between rounds.

**Two people.** One chooses the first tile and the other finds its partner, or one plays and the other calls what a pair will uncover before it goes. The existing co-play line covers it; solo play needs nothing else.

**Needs a person, not a bot** (questions for whoever next plays it on a phone, none of them a gate on the slices below): whether the see-through view is readable at phone size, whether the uncovering pulse is noticed, and whether level 6 feels harder than level 4 or only busier.

## Proposed ladder

### Fresh content inside existing levels

- **A different pairing each round.** Make the geometric route seeded: at each step choose among the free position pairs with `ctx.rng` instead of taking the first, keeping the guarantee that the route clears (a randomised depth-first search over the same memo, or building the route backwards). Layouts, tile counts and picture counts do not change. The test that freezes levels 1–3 by hash is replaced on purpose by property tests: the layouts are the same sets of positions as before, 100 seeds a level all clear, and across those seeds each level produces many distinct pairings (level 1 at least ten). Nothing saved refers to a board, and the couch face-off still deals both players the same board from one seed.
- **A deal dial** (second pass). Each plan gains a target band for its random-play clear rate, and `makeBoard` deals up to 40 seeded-route candidates and keeps the first inside the band (the closest if none is), so the seed still decides the board and the face-off still shares it. In slice 1 the bands reproduce today's measured difficulty (levels 1–4 at least 95%, level 5 at least 85%, level 6 at least 75%), so seeding the route changes the pairing but not how hard a level is. Harder bands (levels 3–4 between 60% and 95%, levels 5–6 between 30% and 80%) come only after Peek (slice 7), so a board does not get harder before she can see why it went wrong. New levels state their own. The measure is 60 seeded play-outs, about 1 ms a 24-tile candidate on a Mac (40 candidates for every level took 24–48 ms), so even the worst case is a fraction of a second on a phone, spent once before the board is drawn (not measured on a phone); a test asserts that 100 seeds a level land inside the band and that no level's candidate loop exhausts on more than 5 of them. The dial at today's bands rides with slice 1, because seeding the route without it silently changes each level's difficulty.
- **Looks.** Two more picture sets as above, each test-checked for six pictures that differ pairwise in shape and in colour.

### Puzzle challenges: designed lessons (second pass)

Short boards made to teach one habit from the strategy guides, each with a trap that is the lesson, and each trap a choice of which copies to pair (see *On strategy*). A random deal on the existing layouts almost never has one right choice (1 in 5,000 measured), but a search over picture placements on a small sketched layout finds them readily: on 14–18-tile sketches, 0.4% to 4% of solvable placements had each lesson's property (20,000 placements a lesson, measured). So `scripts/tile-pairs-pool.mjs` sketches each lesson's layout, searches placements for the property, and the chosen boards are frozen in `puzzles.ts` with their lesson, as Ferry Jam's harbours are. Each level holds 6–8 boards; the seed picks one, may mirror it left to right and permutes the pictures (both keep the property), so repeats are rare.

They are ordinary levels on the ladder, reached by stepping up or by a pin, never a menu that gates anything. Taking the losing pair is not a miss (it is a legal move); when no route is left the stuck line is followed by the board's one-line lesson (`tile-pairs.lesson.<id>`), and Undo puts her back before the choice, which the AGENTS rule for revisable puzzles asks for. Misses, hints and score count as they do today.

### Younger-band openings

A **school** window at levels 1–2 is possible on paper: 108-unit tap targets, one rule, spoken instruction and hint. It would need child wording for `tile-pairs.stuck` and `tile-pairs.hint`, a `LEVELS` line, and the lands test to place it. It is a decision, below, and is not built by default.

### Easier entries and levels between existing ones

None. Twelve open tiles is already a gentle start for a grown-up, and 12 → 20 → 24 is an even slope.

### Left out on purpose

- **Timed and scored modes** (Lightning Tiles, Score Attack, Match Attack, the Dimensions clock): round-ending timers and pressure.
- **The seven-slot tray** of triple-tile games: a loss screen by another name.
- **A full 144-tile turtle.** At 108 units it cannot fit, and at the grown-up minimum of 64 it would need a scrolling board on a phone, a wider mask and a search that can no longer prove "no route" exactly. A 36-tile board is the honest next step and is a decision, below.
- **Rationed or earned hints and shuffles**, daily challenges, badges and streaks.
- **A Golden Tiles goal.** Attractive and untimed, but it adds a second finish condition to a game whose single goal is clear; revisit only if a playtest note asks for a shorter round.
- **Random deals.** About 3% of real turtle deals are unwinnable even with peeking; every board here stays dealt along a route.
- **Face-down tiles** (Dragon's Eye, Great Wall): a hidden picture turns the planning back into memory and luck, which Peek exists to remove.
- **Gravity and sliding** (Great Wall, Beijing): different interactions; Beijing's sliding grid is a separate idea, not a Tile Pairs level.
- **Lock-and-key and goal tiles** (Mahjong Story 2, Mahjong Journey): the only sources are portal pages and the games pair them with hearts and boosters; a lock adds a second rule without a new choice that the designed lessons do not already give.
- **Boards over 40 tiles** with the exact search: measured above. A bounded search that cannot prove "no route" would make the stuck line guess.
- **"Frustrating" deals**, where a sound habit loses: the fair-trap check rejects them from every frozen pool.

## Decisions for the developer

1. **Boards of 36 and 40 tiles (levels 15–16)?** Updated by the second pass: the exact search stays fast to 40 tiles with a widened mask, and both fit 6 columns at today's tile size, so it is a question of taste rather than cost. Lean: build after the tricky and lesson levels, and only if a playtest says 30 tiles feels short.
2. **Open levels 1–2 to the school band?** Lean: not now; the grown-up game is the audience, and Memory Match and Pile Match already serve matching for children.
3. **A frozen couch course?** Couch play retires at Lands stage E, so the default is no.
4. **A builder-and-clearer mode for two grown-ups** (after Dragon's Eye, made cooperative)? One places pictures pair by pair onto the free spaces of an empty layout, which is the backwards deal and so always clearable; the other clears it. It is a new interaction and a co-play mode rather than a level. Lean: not before levels 10–21; worth trying if the two of you play Tile Pairs together.
5. **The lesson levels interleaved by `order`, or appended at the top?** Lean: interleaved, so each habit arrives just before the board that needs it.

None of these blocks a slice below.
